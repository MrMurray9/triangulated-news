Science corner

Science corner: Stanford and Gladstone map 12,748 genes in 22 million human T cells to decode immune “gene networks”

Researchers at Stanford Medicine and the Gladstone Institutes report in Cell a genome-scale perturb-seq atlas of human T cells: using CRISPR, they dialled down 12,748 genes one at a time across about 22 million T cells from four donors and measured how the rest of the genome responded. Led by Gladstone’s Alex Marson and Stanford’s Jonathan Pritchard, the public dataset is meant as a “lookup table” so disease gene-activity patterns can be matched to the effect of turning down a single gene.

The team says it is the first perturb-seq resource at this scale in primary human cells (not just lab-grown cell lines). They showed it can help interpret autoimmune-disease genetics and rare immune-cell count disorders. Follow-on work will boost the same genes, map regulatory T cells, and build better tools to compare big datasets.

Why it matters: If gene networks — not single mutations — drive many diseases, a map like this is how drug hunters pick better targets.

Sources (3)
  • Stanford Medicine — Massive dataset provides blueprint to decode the human immune system
  • Gladstone Institutes — research home
  • Cell — journal of record for the study
Community Note

This is a research resource, not a new approved therapy. Findings in T cells may not transfer unchanged to other cell types. Funding and data-use details are in the Cell paper.

Sources: Stanford Medicine; Gladstone; Cell.
