Alberta

Alberta’s main landowner group tells farmers and ranchers to refuse new oil and gas leases until the province deals with orphan wells and unpaid bills

The Alberta Surface Rights Federation sent Premier Danielle Smith a letter on Thursday saying it will now “actively recommend that landowners resist further energy development on their lands.” President Bill Heidecker, a central Alberta cattle rancher with dozens of inactive wells on his property, said the group’s private talks with the province had been ignored. “We’re pissed and we’ve had enough,” he told The Orchard (Reuters via EnergyNow, The Orchard, CTV News Calgary).

The group isn’t telling people to block companies physically. It wants landowners to withhold consent and refuse to sign new surface leases, which forces companies to apply for a legal right-of-entry order instead. Its complaints include abandoned wells, slow cleanup of old sites, unpaid lease payments and municipal taxes, and weak oversight by the Alberta Energy Regulator. Heidecker says those problems should be fixed before the province pushes ahead with the proposed Pacific Link oil pipeline (Reuters, The Orchard).

Why it matters: Rural landowners have long been the oil industry’s quiet partners in Alberta, so an open revolt from a ranchers’ group could slow drilling and complicate plans for more pipelines.

Sources (3)
  • EnergyNow (Reuters): Alberta landowners’ group tells farmers, ranchers to resist oil development
  • The Orchard: Alberta surface rights group declares war on ‘one-sided’ energy deals
  • CTV News Calgary: Group urges landowners to resist oil and gas development in orphan well cleanup dispute
Community Note

In Alberta the province owns most mineral rights, so landowners can’t stop development outright; refusing a lease mainly adds time and a legal step for the company.

Neither the Alberta nor the federal government had commented when Reuters published. Alberta has about 77,000 inactive wells, according to the figure cited by The Orchard.

Sources: Reuters (via EnergyNow); The Orchard; CTV News Calgary

Canada

Ottawa won’t take its loss on the federal project-review law to the Supreme Court, and will rewrite the act instead; Smith says “Alberta wins again”

Environment Minister Julie Dabrusin’s office confirmed Thursday that the federal government won’t appeal an Alberta Court of Appeal opinion that found much of the Impact Assessment Act unconstitutional. In a 4–1 opinion released Oct. 9, the court said Parliament’s 2024 fixes didn’t cure the problems the Supreme Court flagged in 2023, and that the law still reaches into areas run by the provinces (The Hub, Canada’s National Observer, The Deep Dive).

Ottawa says it will amend the act again “swiftly,” on top of the faster reviews already proposed in Bill C-39, but hasn’t said what the changes will be or when. Premier Danielle Smith, who calls the law the “No More Pipelines Act,” said “Alberta wins again.” Environmental groups including West Coast Environmental Law and Ecojustice called the decision an unprecedented retreat from federal environmental oversight (Canada’s National Observer, The Hub).

Why it matters: The law decides how big projects like pipelines, mines and highways get reviewed, so a narrower federal role could speed approvals but leave more of the environmental checks to provinces.

Sources (3)
  • The Hub: Ottawa to amend Impact Assessment Act instead of appealing Alberta court loss
  • Canada’s National Observer: Ottawa won’t appeal as Alberta court again rules Trudeau-era environmental law unconstitutional
  • The Deep Dive: Ottawa accepts Alberta court ruling against Impact Assessment Act
Community Note

The Alberta ruling was a reference opinion, which isn’t binding in the same way as a trial judgment, but Ottawa has chosen to follow it rather than test it at the Supreme Court.

Until new amendments are tabled, the current act stays on the books, so project builders are still working under a law three appeal-level opinions have found goes too far.

Sources: The Hub; Canada’s National Observer; The Deep Dive

Ottawa serves Stellantis a formal default notice over the Brampton plant sale and gives it 30 days to fix the breach or face a fight to claw back subsidies

What changed since the last edition: On Wednesday morning we reported Carney calling the sale a “betrayal.” Since then Thursday’s meeting ended without a deal, and Ottawa has formally declared Stellantis in default with a 30-day deadline.

After a four-way meeting in Toronto on Thursday with Stellantis, Unifor and Ontario failed to change the plan to sell the idle Brampton assembly plant to armoured-vehicle maker Roshel, Industry Minister Mélanie Joly served the automaker a notice of default on its 2022 federal funding agreement. Stellantis has 30 days to cure the alleged breaches; if it doesn’t, Ottawa says it can end the deal and seek repayment of subsidies with interest (The Canadian Press via CTV News, The Globe and Mail, AM800 CKLW).

Joly said Ottawa has “levers” to hold the company to account and told reporters, “We’ll sue them” if needed. Stellantis Canada CEO Trevor Longley said in a statement that the company remains committed to Canada. Unifor says the sale is still going ahead and the 3,000 Brampton jobs remain in limbo (Global News, Stellantis, AM800 CKLW).

Why it matters: Billions in public money went to auto-sector deals in the past few years, and this is the first big test of whether Ottawa can make a company pay it back when promised jobs don’t materialise.

Indonesia’s vice-president, the son of former president Joko Widodo, resigns after a court ruling questions whether his overseas schooling met the legal requirements

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, 39, handed his resignation letter to Indonesia’s People’s Consultative Assembly on Thursday. Court rulings had questioned whether his overseas education met the constitution’s requirements for the job, and lawmakers had begun pushing for impeachment. President Prabowo Subianto accepted the resignation “with a heavy heart” at the Merdeka Palace in Jakarta and hugged him at the end of his statement (AP, Reuters, The Straits Times, The Jakarta Post).

Gibran’s father, former president Joko Widodo, said the family would keep supporting Prabowo. The assembly now has 60 days to choose a new vice-president, a decision that could shift the balance of the ruling coalition in the world’s fourth-most-populous country (The Straits Times, Reuters, Rappler).

Why it matters: Gibran’s place on the ticket sealed the alliance between Prabowo and the popular Widodo; his exit tests whether that partnership holds.

Brooklyn building collapse death toll doubles to eight, including two children, as investigators begin the slow search for a cause

What changed since the last edition: Thursday morning we reported four deaths. City officials have since confirmed eight, two of them children, and at least 18 injured.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani said Thursday that eight people died when a three-storey apartment building in Flatbush fell into a neighbouring construction pit: six adults and two children. Six died at the scene and two in hospital. At least 18 people were hurt, including four firefighters, and one person remains in critical condition (CBS New York, NY1, The Washington Post).

Crews are still clearing rubble, and officials say finding out why the building gave way, including what role the excavation next door played, will take time. Investigators have to remove debris carefully to preserve evidence (AP, The Washington Post).

Why it matters: It’s one of New York’s deadliest building collapses in years, and it puts a spotlight on how closely the city watches digging next to older homes.

Science corner

Science corner: tiny solar cells on a chip use light, not wires, to line up living cells, a step toward lab-printed tissue that grows the right way

When scientists bioprint tissue, the cells don’t stay where they’re put; they drift and spread. Gentle electric fields can steer them, but that usually means wires and electrodes poking into the sample. A Penn State team led by Ibrahim Ozbolat tried a wireless approach: they dotted silicon solar cells, some only 80 micrometres across, thinner than a human hair, in patterns across a chip (Penn State, Advanced Functional Materials).

Shining light on the chip makes each tiny solar cell produce its own local electric field. Connective-tissue cells called fibroblasts placed on top lined up and took shapes that followed the solar-cell pattern, and the team could tune the result by changing the cells’ size, layout and the brightness of the light (Penn State, Mirage News).

Why it matters: Tissue that grows with its cells pointing the right way works better, so light-powered steering could one day help labs print muscle, skin or nerve repairs without implanted wiring.

Sources (3)

  • Advanced Functional Materials: Configuration-encoded cell alignment by light-gated silicon photovoltaic microcell arrays
  • Mirage News: Microscopic solar panels could shape 3D-printed tissue
Community Note

So far it has only been shown on flat chips in the lab, not inside printed 3D tissue or in animals.

Coverage is thin: the main sources are the peer-reviewed paper and Penn State’s release, with Mirage News republishing it, so we treat this as a light science item.

AI news and advances

Google’s new everyday AI model, Gemini 4 Hydrogen, replaces its “Flash” line and outscores a bigger sibling on a common benchmark while running faster

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on Thursday, two days after opening its top-end Gemini 4 Carbon to developers. Hydrogen is the fast, cheaper model meant for everyday chats, coding and AI agents, and it drops the old “Flash” name. It reads up to a million tokens at once, about several long books, and offers four “thinking” levels (Google, 9to5Google, VentureBeat).

Google says Hydrogen beats its mid-sized Argon model on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, 51 to 50 at its default setting and 54 at its highest. It is rolling out in the Gemini app, including for free users through automatic model selection, and in Search. For developers it costs US$1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, more than the Flash model it replaces (9to5Google, Google AI Studio, VentureBeat).

Why it matters: The cheap, fast models are the ones most people actually use day to day, so upgrades here reach far more users than the flagship launches do.

Sources (4)

  • 9to5Google: Google announces Gemini 4 Hydrogen with ‘significant leaps’ in speed, intelligence
  • VentureBeat: Google launches Gemini 4 Hydrogen — a faster, smarter, pricier Flash successor
  • Google DeepMind: Gemini 4 Hydrogen model card

XBears fans searching X for “Burden” injury news find a struggling teen in Australia instead, and flood them with kindness

Seen on: X, where Bears fans’ replies to the post spread during and after Sunday’s game (CBS Chicago).

When Chicago Bears receiver Luther Burden left Sunday’s game with a concussion, fans searched X for “Burden.” What came up for many was a post from an Australian user named Yuri saying they felt like a burden. After one rude reply, Bears fans piled in with support, from “people love you, and now Chicago Bears fanbase loves you too” to “life is worth living homie, I promise” (CBS Chicago, The Mary Sue, We Got This Covered).

Yuri, who said they had never heard of the Bears, went from a handful of followers to about 10,000 in two days, and fans have since been wishing them luck on school tests and offering game tickets (CBS Chicago, The Mary Sue).

Why it matters: Sports social media is better known for pile-ons; this time a fan base used its reach to make a stranger feel less alone.

OA four-year-old on a zoo field trip sings “God Bless America” to a rescued bald eagle, and the zoo invites him back

Seen on: Social media generally; outlets report millions of views but don’t single out one platform (News 4 San Antonio).

Ezekiel Saldana of Floresville, Texas, had been practising “God Bless America” in his after-school choir for Veterans Day. On a school trip to the San Antonio Zoo, he spotted a wooden American flag and a bald eagle and started singing on his own while his mom filmed. The video drew millions of views (News 4 San Antonio, Fox News, Hoodline).

The eagle is Chinook, who hatched in 1995 and can’t fly long distances because of an old wing injury. The zoo gave the family four free memberships, and on a return visit Chinook stayed out for a photo while Ezekiel sang to him again. His message for veterans: “Thank you for doing that” (News 4 San Antonio, Hoodline).

Why it matters: Sometimes the internet just needs a small kid, a big bird and a song.
