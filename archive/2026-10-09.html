Alberta Court of Appeal rules 4–1 that Ottawa’s amended Impact Assessment Act is still unconstitutional

In an opinion released Friday, Reference re Impact Assessment Act, as amended (2026 ABCA 320), four of five Alberta Court of Appeal justices found that the federal law’s “designated projects” regime still reaches into provincial jurisdiction, even after Parliament amended it in 2024 following the Supreme Court of Canada’s 2023 ruling against much of the original act. Justice Kevin Feehan dissented. Alberta brought the reference, with Ontario and Saskatchewan backing it, The Canadian Press reported.

The majority said the act still lets Ottawa stop or set conditions on a project within provincial jurisdiction if it has even a narrow federal effect. “The (Impact Assessment Act) creates great uncertainty due to the potential for previously unpronounced standards to apply to designated projects,” the court wrote. It found that part of the law and its project-list regulations unconstitutional except where they apply to federal undertakings, according to the Canadian Constitution Foundation, an intervener.

Why it matters: The act governs federal reviews of big Alberta projects such as mines and pipelines; former premier Jason Kenney dubbed it the “no more pipelines act.”

Sources (3)
  • The Canadian Press (via iPolitics)
  • Court of Appeal of Alberta — opinion, 2026 ABCA 320 (PDF)
  • Canadian Constitution Foundation (intervener)
Community Note

A reference opinion is advisory: it doesn’t by itself strike the law off the books, and Ottawa can appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada as of right, as it did after the court’s 2022 opinion. At edition time, Canadian Press was the only news organization outside excluded outlets reporting the ruling; this summary is checked against the court’s own opinion and the intervener’s statement. This edition didn’t find a federal response yet.

Sources: Court of Appeal of Alberta; Canadian Press; Canadian Constitution Foundation.

Poilievre urges Ottawa to sue Stelco’s U.S. owner as Hamilton layoffs begin

Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre on Friday called on the federal government to sue Cleveland-Cliffs, the Ohio-based owner of Stelco, as soon as Monday, seeking to bring back workers and restart production or force a sale to a Canadian owner. Speaking in Burlington, Ont., across the harbour from Stelco’s Hamilton works, he also proposed scrapping the industrial carbon tax, cracking down on Chinese steel imports, speeding up LNG projects and negotiating a tariff-free deal with the U.S.

Stelco says up to 500 workers at its Hamilton and Lake Erie sites could be laid off; the first layoffs take effect Sunday, with about 330 Hamilton workers affected over three weeks, according to United Steelworkers Local 1005. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly gave Cleveland-Cliffs five business days on Monday to show how it will meet its employment commitments or face legal action. CEO Lourenco Goncalves said the layoffs are temporary and workers would be recalled if Canada and the U.S. reach a steel trade deal.

Why it matters: It’s a test of whether Ottawa will enforce the job promises foreign buyers make under the Investment Canada Act, with steel tariffs still weighing on Canadian producers.

U.S. sanctions the International Criminal Court itself; Canada joins allies in “strongly” disagreeing

Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced sanctions on the ICC as an institution on Friday, banning U.S. entities from transactions with what he called a “rogue court.” “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC,” he said. Exemptions create what amounts to a six-month grace period, which the State Department said gives member states time to address U.S. objections.

The court called the move “an assault on the rule of law” and said “history will judge this moment.” Canada joined the U.K., France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Denmark and the Netherlands in a joint statement saying they “regret and strongly disagree with” the sanctions. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called it “a serious blow,” while Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who faces an ICC arrest warrant, praised it. The announcement came hours after former ICC judge Navi Pillay won the Nobel Peace Prize.

Why it matters: Canada is a founding supporter of the ICC; the sanctions could cut the court off from U.S. banks and software it relies on, and put Ottawa at odds with Washington.

Science corner

Cornell team makes a working transistor from a 7-electronvolt semiconductor, the widest bandgap yet

Writing in Nature this week, a Cornell-led team reports silicon-doped aluminium gallium oxide films, α-(AlGa)₂O₃, with bandgaps above 7.0 electronvolts, wider than cubic boron nitride, previously the widest-bandgap semiconductor known. Materials with gaps this large normally behave as insulators, but the team grew the films on sapphire with a technique called suboxide molecular-beam epitaxy and got room-temperature conductivity more than 100 million times higher than earlier attempts in this range.

They built working devices from it: a Schottky diode and a field-effect transistor they call the AlphaFET. In an accompanying commentary, Hironori Okumura of Japan’s University of Tsukuba said the work “pushes semiconductors into a regime that has long been reserved for insulators.”

Why it matters: Wider bandgaps let power electronics handle higher voltages more efficiently in less space, which matters for grids, EV chargers and data centres.

Sources (3)
  • Nature — Steele et al. paper
  • Nature — News & Views (Okumura)
  • Scienmag
Community Note

These are lab demonstrations, not products; device performance, reliability and manufacturing at scale still have to be shown. Silicon carbide and gallium nitride remain the commercial wide-bandgap materials today.

Sources: Nature; Scienmag.

Anthropic launches free AI vulnerability scans for open-source projects and a program to defend power and water systems

Anthropic announced an “Anthropic Cyber Mission” with two parts. A Critical Infrastructure Defense Program brings its Claude models and on-site engineers to security firms that protect grids, water systems and plants; 11 founding partners include CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Dragos, Accenture, Deloitte, Hitachi and Rockwell Automation. A free OSS Scanner gives eligible open-source projects periodic vulnerability scans with reproducible reports and suggested fixes.

Anthropic says its models flagged more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities in widely used software over six months. In an accuracy check, expert testers cleared 85 of 97 critical and high-severity findings for disclosure, SiliconANGLE reported. The company also pledged $150 million over three years to the U.S. government’s Genesis Mission science program.

Why it matters: Operators of utilities, including in Alberta, run decades-old equipment that’s hard to patch; AI tools are being aimed at both finding and fixing those weaknesses.
