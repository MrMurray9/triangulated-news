Alberta

Alberta’s new consumer bill would force makers to sell repair parts and manuals, add a car “lemon law,” and make ticket resellers show the full price up front

Service Alberta Minister Dale Nally tabled Bill 34 on Wednesday. It would set up a right-to-repair framework so owners and independent shops can get the parts, tools and manuals needed to fix things they own. The province says it will list the covered products later in regulations, and named vehicles and electronics as examples (Government of Alberta, Edmonton Journal, The Canadian Press).

A new “lemon law” would let buyers go to court over a new or leased vehicle that cannot be fixed in its first three years or 60,000 km: after three failed tries at the same warranty defect, 12 failed repairs in total, or more than 30 days off the road. Ticket resellers would have to show the full price including fees, and the province could later cap resale markups. The bill would also let Albertans freeze their credit reports and allow a pilot of lottery sales at 30 liquor stores (Edmonton Journal, Government of Alberta, The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: Most of the details, and the timing, will be set later in regulations, so the bill’s real effect on repair costs and concert ticket prices depends on what the province decides next.

Canada

Joshua Kutryk heads out this morning on his first spacewalk, the first by a Canadian since 2019

Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and NASA astronaut Anil Menon are set to leave the International Space Station’s Quest airlock at about 8 a.m. Eastern (6 a.m. in Edmonton) for a spacewalk of nearly seven hours. They will install and unfold a new roll-out solar array to boost the station’s power (NASA, Canadian Space Agency, Spaceflight Now).

It is Kutryk’s first spacewalk. Kutryk, who grew up on a farm near Fort Saskatchewan, will be the fifth Canadian ever to walk in space. A second spacewalk is planned for Nov. 5; together the new arrays are expected to raise the station’s power from about 160 to 215 kilowatts (The Canadian Press, Spaceflight Now, Canadian Space Agency).

Why it matters: It is a milestone for Canada’s astronaut program, and for Alberta: Kutryk is from just outside Edmonton.

Europe drops the “associate member” label as Carney opens the Canada-EU summit in Montreal today

What changed: Earlier this week the draft text was expected to describe Canada as an EU “associate member.” That label has now been dropped, and the summit itself begins today.

Prime Minister Mark Carney welcomes European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and other EU leaders to Montreal today for a two-day summit. After objections from Germany and France, the EU has backed away from calling Canada an “associate member”; a draft joint statement now calls for “ever closer association” instead (The Globe and Mail, AP, The Canadian Press).

Officials on both sides say the wording change will not slow plans for closer ties on digital trade, critical minerals and satellite communications. Carney says the summit will start further negotiations over the coming year, as both sides try to depend less on the United States (The Globe and Mail, AP, The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: Europe is Canada’s second-largest trading partner, and deeper ties are central to Carney’s plan to cushion the U.S. trade war.

A “live at sea for years” cruise ship halts in Cambodia over fuel costs, leaving about 200 passengers looking for somewhere to stay

Florida-based Villa Vie Residences has suspended its multi-year round-the-world voyage aboard the MV Odyssey in Sihanoukville, Cambodia. Science corner

Science corner: a common, cheap mineral could help solar panels hang on to the energy they catch

Solar materials usually force a trade-off: the ones that soak up light best also tend to lose the captured energy fastest, before it can be turned into electricity. A team from the University of Oxford and Wuhan University reports in Nature that bismuth sulfide (Bi₂S₃) sidesteps this. It absorbs light strongly, then parks the energized electrons in a slightly lower energy state where they are much slower to recombine (Nature, University of Oxford, Nanowerk).

The researchers call it a “pseudo-direct” semiconductor and estimate that it cuts this energy loss by about 75 per cent at room temperature. The finding points to a new way to design solar materials that waste less of the light they absorb (University of Oxford, Nature, Nanowerk).

Why it matters: If other materials can be designed the same way, future solar cells could get more power from the same light.

AI news and advances

The Netherlands plans to cut 25,000 civil-service jobs over 10 years, leaning on AI to do more of the work

The Dutch cabinet wants to shrink central government by about 25,000 full-time jobs by 2035, roughly 15 to 20 per cent of the workforce, through AI, more digital services, shared back-office work and less red tape. Police and defence are exempt (Reuters via Yahoo Finance, NLnow, The Next Web).

The government is targeting savings of €900 million in 2027, rising to €3.5 billion a year from 2035. Detailed plans for how AI will be used are due in early 2027 (Bloomberg Law, The Next Web, NLnow).

Why it matters: It is one of the first times a national government has put a firm job-cut number on AI, and other countries, Canada included, will watch whether the savings and service levels hold up.

Trump says he will “ban” the term artificial intelligence and wants it called “Super Intelligence” instead

In a Truth Social post, U.S. President Donald Trump said on Oct. 27 that he would “ban” the term “AI” and call it “SI,” for Super Intelligence (Reuters via Investing.com, Forbes, The Hill).

A president can set wording for federal government documents, but cannot stop companies, media or the public from using the term (Forbes, The Hill, Reuters via Investing.com).

Why it matters: It is mostly a fight over words, but it shows how the White House wants to frame the technology as U.S. agencies buy more of it.

Sources (3)

  • Forbes — Trump says he will ban term ‘artificial intelligence’ in latest push for ‘Super Intelligence’
  • The Hill — Trump says he will ban the term artificial intelligence, making switch to super intelligence
Community Note

“Superintelligence” usually means a hypothetical AI smarter than people at nearly everything, which today’s systems are not, so most researchers see the new label as inaccurate. This is about naming only; it does not change any AI rules.

Sources: Reuters; Forbes; The Hill.
