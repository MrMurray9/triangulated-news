Nearly 175,000 Albertans voted on the first day of advance polls — above the 2023 election’s daily pace

Elections Alberta says 174,532 people cast ballots on Tuesday, the first of five advance-voting days for the Oct. 19 referendum. For comparison, advance polls in the 2023 provincial general election averaged 151,727 voters a day. Some stations stayed open hours past closing to clear lineups, and the agency says it has hired more than 46,000 election workers while preparing for record turnout.

Wednesday’s voting hit a weather snag: Elections Alberta says a wind-caused power outage around 2 p.m. closed advance voting at the Athabasca Seniors Centre and the Returning Office in Clyde (Athabasca-Barrhead-Westlock), with ballots and boxes secured and power expected back by about 4:30 p.m. Advance voting runs through Saturday, Oct. 17; mail-in (special) ballots must reach Elections Alberta in Edmonton by 5 p.m. Friday, Oct. 16.

Why it matters: Strong early turnout is the first hard number in a campaign both sides say will be decided by who shows up — and it means long lines are possible again this week.

What changed since the last edition: This morning covered day-two voting and the Friday mail-ballot deadline. New this evening: Elections Alberta’s first-day turnout figure (174,532), the comparison with 2023, and a wind-related closure in Athabasca-Barrhead-Westlock on Wednesday afternoon.

Science corner

Science corner: a chip-scale laser gyroscope from UC Santa Barbara and Caltech senses rotation as slow as 37 degrees per hour

Researchers led by Daniel Blumenthal (UC Santa Barbara) and Kerry Vahala (Caltech) report in Optics Letters an integrated Brillouin ring-laser gyroscope built on a silicon nitride photonic chip that measured rotation as slow as 37 degrees per hour — approaching Earth’s own rotation rate of 15 degrees per hour, and nearly 2,000 times better than the team’s earlier silicon nitride Brillouin gyroscope (72,000 degrees per hour).

Two lasers send light around a roughly 24-millimetre ring in opposite directions; when the chip turns, the two light signals shift slightly in frequency, and that difference reveals the rotation. The ring holds light extremely well (an intrinsic Q factor above 163 million), uses only milliwatts of power and can be made with standard CMOS chip processes. The researchers are now cutting stray back-scattered light and plan a fully packaged, all-on-one-chip version for field tests.

Why it matters: Optical gyroscopes guide aircraft and spacecraft, but they are bulky; a rugged chip version with no moving parts could bring precision navigation to cars, drones and anywhere GPS is jammed or unavailable.

Sources (3)
  • Optica — New chip-scale gyroscope measures rotation with unprecedented sensitivity
  • Optics Letters — Integrated microresonator Brillouin gyroscope demonstrating 37 deg/h rotation measurements (DOI 10.1364/OL.589952)
  • Caltech Vahala Research Group — chip-based laser gyroscope research
Community Note

This is a lab demonstration, not a product: the lasers and detectors are not yet all on one chip, and navigation-grade gyroscopes need even finer, more stable performance. Results are from a peer-reviewed journal paper and the publisher’s release; independent replication is still to come.

Sources: Optica; Optics Letters; Caltech.
