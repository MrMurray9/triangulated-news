Alberta

Alberta opens $250 million in funding to build about 550 continuing care spaces and fix up 300 seniors’ lodge rooms

Assisted Living and Social Services Minister Nathan Neudorf said on Monday that the province is taking applications for two programs. The Continuing Care Capital Program has $200 million to build about 550 new continuing care spaces, and the Seniors Lodge Modernization Program has $50 million to build or upgrade about 300 lodge spaces. Together that’s roughly 850 affordable homes for seniors (Calgary Herald, Medicine Hat News, Government of Alberta).

Alberta has more than 890,000 seniors today, and the province expects that to pass one million by 2034. “We need to prepare for that now,” Neudorf said. Alberta already funds more than 30,000 continuing care spaces, with about 3,400 under development, and has nearly 11,500 lodge units, about 5,400 of them in rural communities. Operators, non-profits and municipalities can apply until Jan. 31, 2027 (Calgary Herald, Medicine Hat News, Alberta.ca).

Why it matters: Families waiting for a continuing care bed for a parent, especially in smaller towns, are the ones this money is meant to help, though new spaces will take years to build.

Canada

Carney names Bridgewater co-investment chief Karen Karniol-Tambour as the first CEO of the $25-billion Canada Strong Fund

Prime Minister Mark Carney announced on Monday that Karen Karniol-Tambour, co-chief investment officer at U.S. hedge fund giant Bridgewater Associates, will lead Canada’s first national sovereign wealth fund. She is a Canadian citizen who has spent her whole career at Bridgewater since graduating from Princeton in 2006, rising from investment associate to head of research and then co-CIO in 2023. She will leave the firm over the next few weeks (Prime Minister’s Office, The Globe and Mail, Reuters via U.S. News).

Ottawa is putting $25 billion into the fund over three years. It will co-invest alongside federal programs in “nation-building” projects and make its own strategic bets in Canadian companies, on commercial terms and at arm’s length from government with its own board. For now it is set up as a subsidiary of the Canada Development Investment Corporation until legislation makes it a standalone Crown corporation. Ordinary Canadians will be able to invest through principal-protected “Canada Strong Notes” sold by banks, with a launch expected in early 2027 (PMO, BetaKit, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: If it works, Canadians get a direct share of the returns from big projects such as pipelines, ports and mines, and a chance to buy in through their own bank.

After a decade of delays, Canada’s Real-Time Rail goes live for 24/7 instant payments, but none of the Big Six banks are on it yet

Payments Canada switched on the first phase of the Real-Time Rail on Monday, a national system that lets financial institutions send, clear and settle payments in seconds, 24 hours a day, every day of the year. The eight launch members are Wealthsimple, KOHO, Questrade’s Questbank, Peoples Trust Company, Paramount Commerce, DoBusiness.com, Digital Commerce Bank and Bank of America. “A new era for payments,” Payments Canada CEO Susan Hawkins called it (Payments Canada, BetaKit, American Banker).

The project started in 2015 and was meant to launch in 2019, then slipped to 2022, 2023 and finally 2026. Canada’s biggest banks aren’t in the first phase, which some fintech leaders say limits how useful it is for now; KOHO’s CEO told BetaKit it would be largely performative without them. Payments Canada says the system will reach more than nine in 10 Canadians within a year, with the launch-phase members processing their full volumes by the third quarter of 2027. Interac, which runs e-Transfer, put out its own statement on the launch (BetaKit, Payments Canada, Interac).

Why it matters: Over time this could mean paycheques, bills and business payments that clear instantly on weekends and holidays instead of waiting days for the bank to process them.

France says Iran’s Revolutionary Guards hacked the fridges in about 700 French supermarkets, pushing some past 50°C

Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said on Monday that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps was behind the Oct. 11–12 attacks, which took over digital thermostats on thousands of freezers and chiller cabinets and pushed some from as cold as -27°C to well over 50°C. He called it an act of “unprecedented scale and unprecedented aggression,” summoned Iran’s ambassador and promised Europe-wide sanctions. Thousands of tonnes of food were thrown out, and in some stores the heat sparked fires (The Guardian, AP, NBC News).

Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said investigators found the hallmarks of Shahid Kaveh, an IRGC-linked cyber unit, including the use of Farsi and IP addresses traced to devices in Iran, and opened a case against its leader and deputies. Since then, hackers have also reached the cold stores at France’s blood service, where alarms caught it in time, and retailers in 11 European countries reported refrigeration problems. EU foreign-policy chief Kaja Kallas said ministers will discuss a response next Monday. Separately, Canada reimposed sanctions on 11 Iranian entities on Tuesday at the G7 meeting in France (The Guardian, NBC News, AP, Global Affairs Canada).

Why it matters: It shows how ordinary internet-connected equipment, like store thermostats, can be turned into a weapon, and similar systems run grocery stores and cold chains in Canada too.

Russia, Ukraine and the U.S. sit down in Abu Dhabi for their first real peace talks since February, with security guarantees on the table

The two-day trilateral session opened Tuesday in the United Arab Emirates. Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff lead for Washington, Rustem Umerov for Ukraine, and Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky is part of the Russian team. The White House says the talks will focus on future security guarantees. U.S. officials told reporters they want a “final end” to the war and are proposing a new “long-term security architecture” between Russia and Europe, and that Ukraine could get guarantees “very similar to NATO” (AP, The Guardian, Interfax-Ukraine).

Before the three-way meeting, the U.S., Ukraine, the EU, NATO, France, Germany, Italy and the U.K. issued a joint statement that any deal must respect Ukraine’s sovereignty and territorial integrity and include credible guarantees. Europeans are wary: Kallas said Russia has so far been “pretending” to negotiate, and officials fear Washington could offer big concessions, including a rollback of NATO’s footprint in eastern Europe. Meanwhile, G7 foreign ministers met Ukraine’s Andrii Sybiha in France on Tuesday (The Guardian, Council of the EU, AP).

Why it matters: A durable ceasefire would ease pressure on energy and food prices worldwide, and Canada, as a NATO and G7 member, would have a say in any new security setup.

A train slams into the back of another south of Cairo, killing seven, and prosecutors say its driver had switched off the automatic brakes

On Sunday a Giza-to-Fayoum passenger train struck the rear of a stationary Cairo-to-Aswan train near El Ayyat in Giza province, derailing several carriages. Seven people were killed and more than 100 were hurt; officials have given injury counts from 112 to about 125 (AP, AFP, The National).

Prosecutors said Monday that technical checks found the Giza–Fayoum driver, who died in the crash, had disabled his train’s automatic control system. Passengers said the train was going fast from the moment it left El Ayyat. The controller and stationmaster at the next station admitted they hadn’t watched the signal panel, and the controller allegedly altered the log to hide it; both were remanded in custody. A Cairo University engineering panel will review the railway’s signalling, staffing and past accidents, and some pro-government MPs are calling on Transport Minister Kamel El Wazir to resign. He apologised to victims’ families and promised that anyone negligent would “not escape punishment” (The National, Egypt Today, AP).

Why it matters: Egypt’s railway has been hit by deadly crashes again and again, so this inquiry tests whether billions spent on safety upgrades are actually working.

Business

Qantas will cut about 850 head-office jobs and hand much of the work to Accenture teams in India, and its shares jump 6%

Qantas Group CEO Vanessa Hudson told staff on Tuesday that roughly 850 corporate roles will go over the coming years under a new multi-year deal with consulting firm Accenture, with the first changes in place by mid-2027. Finance, procurement and marketing are first, and the outsourced work will be “delivered mostly by its teams in India and some in Australia.” Qantas says it will redeploy as many people as it can (ABC News Australia, 7NEWS, AdNews).

Hudson said the airline runs on “more than 1,000 legacy applications that were never designed to work together,” and that the overhaul will “accelerate our use of AI.” Investors liked it: the shares rose more than 6% on the day. The Australian Services Union said it had been told there was no plan to offshore jobs and called the news “really shocking” for members, most of them in Sydney. Telstra and Coles have made similar Accenture deals. The cuts come days after Qantas said it would trim about 4% of Qantas and Jetstar flights because of high fuel costs (ABC News Australia, Cyber Daily, 7NEWS).

Why it matters: It’s a clear example of AI and outsourcing reaching white-collar office jobs at a profitable company, a pattern Canadian airlines and banks are watching closely.

Science corner

Science corner: a Johns Hopkins robot cuts tumours out of eight model kidneys on its own, making 77 cuts without a single error

Taking a tumour out of a kidney while saving the healthy part is fiddly work, because the organ shifts and squishes as the surgeon cuts, and the tumour you saw on the CT scan isn’t quite where it was. Engineers led by Axel Krieger built a two-armed robot to handle that: one arm uses a gentle vacuum gripper to pull tissue aside, and the other holds an electrosurgical cutter. An AI model predicts how the tissue will deform and keeps re-estimating where the tumour sits after every cut (Johns Hopkins University, IEEE Transactions on Robotics).

The team made eight lifelike kidneys out of hydrogel, four of them copied from real patients’ CT scans. The goal was to remove each tumour plus the standard 6-millimetre safety border. Across all eight operations, the robot removed every bit of tumour, stayed within about 1.7 mm of the planned border and made 77 cuts with no errors. When the researchers switched off the part that updates its picture of the moving tissue, every surgery failed (Johns Hopkins, IEEE T-RO, arXiv preprint).

Why it matters: Robots that can track soft, moving organs could one day make delicate cancer surgery more consistent and available in more hospitals, including smaller ones.

Sources (3)
  • Johns Hopkins Mechanical Engineering: Mechanical engineers use robotics device to remove kidney tumor
  • IEEE Transactions on Robotics: Occupancy Network-Guided Autonomous Robotic Partial Nephrectomy
  • arXiv: Autonomous Robotic Partial Nephrectomy (preprint)
Community Note

This was done on gel models that don’t bleed, not on real tissue or patients, and the university calls it semi-autonomous. The next step is testing on organic tissue; making it work through small keyhole incisions is a longer-term goal.

Sources: Johns Hopkins University; IEEE Transactions on Robotics; arXiv

AI news and advances

Meta releases Muse Ember, a tiny, cheap AI model built to do the repetitive grunt work for AI agents at 10 US cents per million input tokens

Meta Superintelligence Labs launched Muse Ember on Monday, its smallest and cheapest model so far. Meta says it is built for “the work agents repeat all day: tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing.” It costs US$0.10 per million input tokens, and Meta’s charts show it beating Anthropic’s Haiku and OpenAI’s Luna on cost per task in Artificial Analysis benchmarks. VentureBeat reports it nearly matches Claude Haiku 5.5 at about a third of the estimated cost (TechCrunch, The Verge, VentureBeat).

The idea is to pair it with a bigger model: “Muse Wonder plans, Muse Ember handles the volume,” Meta says. A customer-support bot, for example, could use the big model to set the rules and Ember to apply them across tens of thousands of cases. It also reads images, charts, scanned documents, audio and video without a separate model. Ember is the fourth Muse model this year, after Spark, the open-source Glimmer and the frontier Wonder model in October (TechCrunch, Meta, The Verge).

Why it matters: Cheap, capable small models are what make AI assistants affordable to run at scale, which pushes prices down for every business using them.
