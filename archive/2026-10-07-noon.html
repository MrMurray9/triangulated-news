Alberta restores full disability payments for couples and shortens the path back to AISH

The province is repealing a rule that set each partner’s living allowance at 88 per cent of the standard rate when both members of a couple qualify for AISH or the new Alberta Disability Assistance Program (ADAP). Affected clients will automatically be repaid the difference back to the August 2026 benefit period, with payments promised before the end of the year.

Former AISH clients who were moved to ADAP and want to return to AISH now face a shorter application: information already on file can be reused, cutting the non-medical portion by 75 per cent, and Alberta nurse practitioners can now sign the medical report. Minister Nathan Neudorf said the changes respond to client feedback; NDP critic Marie Renaud said the government was cleaning up a mess of its own making.

Why it matters: At the $1,940 standard rate, the 12 per cent cut cost each partner $232.80 a month — about $465 for a couple — so the retroactive payments matter for affected Alberta households.

Community Note

The province did not say how many couples were affected or what the retroactive payments will cost. A shorter AISH application does not guarantee re-approval; files are still reviewed. ADAP itself remains in place — the changes adjust it rather than reverse the AISH-to-ADAP transition.

Sources: CTV News; Medicine Hat News; CochraneNow (citing the amended regulation).

Premier’s office distances Smith from UCP riding president’s meeting with U.S. Republican strategists

A Globe and Mail investigation published on the weekend reported that Republican strategists from Michigan met Alberta separatists near the Calgary airport in January to discuss software for identifying and mobilizing supporters. Mitch Sylvestre — president of the Bonnyville-Cold Lake UCP constituency association and leader of the Alberta Prosperity Project — attended; he told the Globe he was uncomfortable with the pitch and refused to hand over the group’s membership list.

Why it matters: The reports land less than two weeks before the Oct. 19 referendum, whose Question 10 asks whether Alberta should begin the legal process toward a binding separation vote.

Ottawa will legislate to keep MAID closed to people whose only condition is mental illness

Justice Minister Sean Fraser said Wednesday the government will table legislation this fall to indefinitely exclude people whose sole underlying condition is mental illness from medical assistance in dying, rather than let eligibility begin on March 17, 2027. “While there is not a perfect consensus on this issue, we believe this is the correct approach at this particular time,” he said, citing concerns about inadequate access to mental-health services.

The same bill will amend the Criminal Code so clinicians are not treated as criminals for providing MAID based on an advance request from a patient expected to lose capacity; Fraser said he expects provinces to then consider whether to offer it. He gave no tabling date. The government will not support Conservative MP Tamara Jansen’s Bill C-218, up for a second-reading vote today, arguing its “mental disorder” wording is too broad.

Why it matters: It settles, for now, a question Ottawa deferred twice and lines up with the June joint committee’s recommendation — but nothing changes in law until a bill passes Parliament.

What changed since this morning: This morning the ministers were only scheduled to outline a “path forward”; at noon ET they announced a permanent exclusion plus advance-request changes.

Sources (4)

Community Note

This is an announced bill, not yet tabled; the current exclusion stays in force until March 17, 2027 unless Parliament acts. Dying With Dignity Canada’s court challenge to the exclusion is still before the courts, and three senators and a Bloc MP on the committee dissented, calling for a Supreme Court reference.

Ottawa gives Stelco’s U.S. owner five business days to show how it will keep its job promises

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly wrote to Stelco president Paul Simon on Monday expressing “extreme disappointment” over plans to lay off up to 500 workers at Hamilton Works. She asked Cleveland-Cliffs to give officials, within five business days, its plan for meeting the undertakings it made under the Investment Canada Act when it bought Stelco for $3.4 billion in 2024.

Those conditions include keeping the same number of unionized jobs and most non-union jobs for five years. Joly said the Act lets Ottawa ask a superior court to order compliance, divestiture or penalties. Cliffs has blamed 50 per cent U.S. tariffs for shutting its Canadian galvanized-steel business.

Why it matters: It tests whether the conditions Ottawa attaches to foreign takeovers can be enforced when tariffs change a company’s economics.

U.S. proposes a $70,000 fee before international graduates can work under OPT

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security proposed a rule Wednesday that would make schools pay $70,000 per F-1 student for an initial period of Optional Practical Training and $30,000 for each later OPT period, including STEM extensions. OPT lets international students work in their field during or after their studies.

DHS says the fees target fraud and protect American workers. Schools could pass the cost on to students or employers. The rule is to be published in the Federal Register on Oct. 8, with comments accepted until Nov. 9; it would not take effect until finalized, and immigration lawyers expect court challenges.

Why it matters: Canadians studying in the U.S. on F-1 visas would be affected if it is finalized, and it could make Canadian universities relatively more attractive to international students.

Sources (4)
  • U.S. Department of Homeland Security
  • Associated Press
  • Fragomen (immigration law firm)
  • Newsweek
Community Note

This is a proposal, not law, and it would not apply retroactively to OPT already approved. Some early reports and social posts cited a $100,000 figure; the DHS release sets $70,000 initial and $30,000 subsequent. The separate $100,000 fee on certain H-1B petitions is currently blocked in court.

Sources: DHS; AP; Fragomen; Newsweek.

Science corner

The first working nuclear clocks: thorium-229 crystals now keep time on their own

Two independent teams reported in Nature on Wednesday the first clocks that “tick” on a transition inside an atomic nucleus rather than in an atom’s electrons. A TU Wien–PTB team in Vienna and a Tsinghua University-led team in China both locked a continuous ultraviolet laser to thorium-229 nuclei embedded in millimetre-sized calcium fluoride crystals.

TU Wien says its prototype stabilizes itself and ran for more than 24 hours without intervention, at a precision of about 10−15 — roughly one second in 30 million years. That is not yet as good as the best optical atomic clocks, but nuclei are far less sensitive to outside disturbances, so the ceiling is higher.

Why it matters: Better clocks underpin GPS, telecom networks and tests for dark matter; a solid-state nuclear clock could eventually be smaller and more robust than today’s best timekeepers.

Sources (4)
  • TU Wien
  • Physics World
  • El País English
  • Nature — research paper
Community Note

These are first prototypes. The measured nuclear linewidth (about 30 kHz) is still far wider than the theoretical limit, and both teams say better lasers and host crystals are needed before nuclear clocks beat atomic ones. The video below is a NIST explainer from last year, not footage of the new devices.

Sources: TU Wien; Physics World; Nature.

Mistral previews Large 4, a 1-trillion-parameter model it promises to release as open weights

French AI lab Mistral launched a public preview of Mistral Large 4 — nicknamed “Le Chonk” — through its API on Tuesday, calling it its largest and most capable model. It says the natively multimodal model was trained from scratch on about 4,000 Nvidia GPUs in its own European data centres and will release the weights by the end of October.

Mistral is pitching it at cybersecurity, finance, law and engineering work, and says it beats every open-weight model developed in the U.S. or Europe. Until the weights are out, it is red-teaming the model with security firms, vetted partners and governments.

Why it matters: An open-weight frontier model run in Europe gives Canadian organizations another option they could host themselves instead of relying on U.S. or Chinese providers.

Google releases EmbeddingGemma 2, an open model that searches text, images, video and audio on-device

Google released EmbeddingGemma 2, an open, Apache-2.0-licensed embedding model that maps text and code, images, video and audio into one shared vector space for search and retrieval. It has about 740 million parameters, and developers can load only the 270-million-parameter text part when they don’t need the image and audio encoders.

Google says it is built to run on phones and laptops for private, offline search and retrieval-augmented generation. Weights are on Hugging Face.

Why it matters: Small, open models like this let apps search a user’s own photos, recordings and documents without sending them to the cloud.

Sources (3)

  • The Times of India
Community Note

“Best-in-class” is Google’s own framing; independent leaderboard results will take time. An embedding model powers search and matching — it does not generate text or chat by itself.
