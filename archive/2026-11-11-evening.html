Alberta

About 300 members of the soldiers’ own battalion march to the cenotaph as Alberta’s Remembrance Day ceremonies honour the four killed in the Wainwright training fire

The 3rd Battalion, Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry paraded to the cenotaph in St. Albert on Wednesday, where Sgt. Morgan Wong, 26, of Edmonton; Pte. Shaun Farrell, 23; Pte. Aidan Goulding, 23; and Pte. Hayden Lykkemark, 19, were remembered by name. The Globe and Mail reported that at least 2,000 people lined the downtown street for the service (Global News, The Globe and Mail).

“There are cenotaphs all around our country filled with names and sadly, a few new names will be added to the roles of fallen soldiers,” said retired padre Maj. Ken MacRae, who also asked the crowd to remember the injured still in hospital. In Wainwright itself, a town of about 7,000 next to the base, hundreds of soldiers, residents and politicians, including federal Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre, filled the town’s arena for its annual ceremony. The four died on Oct. 17 when strong winds pushed a grass fire onto about 84 soldiers during attack training. Thirty-nine others were hurt, and 21 were still in hospital as of Nov. 5 (Global News, The Globe and Mail, National Observer).

Why it matters: CFB Wainwright is one of the country’s busiest training bases, and Wednesday’s ceremonies showed how close this loss sits for Alberta communities, a reminder that military service carries real risk even in training at home.

Canada

A pilot in his 60s is killed when his small plane crashes near Saint-Georges, Que., minutes after flying in the town’s Remembrance Day ceremony

Quebec provincial police say the crash happened around 11 a.m. local time Wednesday in a wooded area near the Saint-Georges airport, in the Beauce region about 100 kilometres south of Quebec City. The pilot, a local man in his 60s, was alone on board and was pronounced dead at the scene. The city says he had just taken part in its Remembrance Day commemoration and was coming in to land when the plane went down and caught fire (Global News, Toronto Sun, The Globe and Mail).

A witness told Le Journal de Québec she saw a formation of six planes, then watched one break away and nosedive. The Canadian Armed Forces said the aircraft wasn’t a military plane and no Forces members were involved. The Transportation Safety Board has sent investigators. “What has just happened is truly sad and terrible,” said Saint-Georges Mayor Manon Bougie. Premier Paul St-Pierre Plamondon and federal ministers sent condolences (Toronto Sun, Global News).

Why it matters: A civilian tribute flight turned deadly on the one morning the whole country stops to remember, and the safety board’s findings could affect how small-plane flypasts are run at ceremonies.

World

U.S. prosecutors say a jailed operative for Tehran’s Revolutionary Guards used coded calls home to help plan September’s foiled attack on RAF Fairford, the British base hosting American bombers

An eight-count indictment unsealed in New York on Tuesday charges three men linked to Iran’s Revolutionary Guards and the Iraqi militia Kata’ib Hezbollah: Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi, Mohsen Mohajeri and Mohammad Ali al-Hassani. Prosecutors say al-Saadi, held in a U.S. jail since May on earlier terrorism charges, passed messages through relatives in Iraq, and that al-Hassani then paid people in Britain to scout the base, buy vans and fuel barrels, research chemical accelerants and buy metal that could serve as shrapnel (U.S. Justice Department, The Guardian, Politico).

On Sept. 26, three vans dressed up with fake fuel-delivery and pool-supply decals were sent toward RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire. At least two were turned back at a security roadblock, and police arrested the occupants early the next morning. The Guardian reports five London men were arrested near the base after a farmer reported vans blocking a road. The indictment also ties the three to about 18 attacks in Europe this spring, seven in the U.K., including arson attacks and a stabbing of two Jewish men in London (Justice Department, The Guardian, The Washington Post).

Why it matters: Fairford was used for U.S. bombing runs on Iran, and the case is the clearest picture yet of Iran-backed networks trying to strike Western targets on European soil.

Business

Bank of Canada notes show its leaders think more rate hikes may be needed as high energy costs start feeding into other prices

The central bank’s summary of the talks behind its Oct. 28 decision, released Tuesday, shows the six-member governing council weighed holding its key rate at 2.25% or raising it to 2.5%. It chose the hike, its first since 2023, because oil was about US$35 a barrel above what it assumed in July and businesses were signalling they would pass more of their fuel, chemical, plastic and computer-chip costs on to customers (Bank of Canada, The Globe and Mail, Financial Post).

“Members agreed there could be a need to increase the policy rate further,” the summary says, but they will go “one meeting at a time.” The bank now expects inflation to climb to 3.5% by the end of this year and stay above 3% through much of 2027, returning to 2% by mid-2028. Core inflation is still near 2%, and unemployment is around 6.5%. The next rate decision is Dec. 9 (Bank of Canada, Global News/The Canadian Press, Financial Post).

Why it matters: Another increase in December would hit variable-rate mortgages and anyone renewing a loan, as the cost of the Middle East war keeps working its way through the economy.

Science corner

Science corner: DeepMind’s AlphaGenome 2 predicts which cell types a DNA spelling change affects, matching in minutes a lab finding that took 18 months

Almost all of our DNA doesn’t code for proteins. Instead it works like a set of switches that turn genes up or down, and different switches matter in different cells. AlphaGenome 2, released Wednesday, takes a stretch of DNA up to a million letters long plus a chosen cell type, such as a gut immune cell or a heart muscle cell, and predicts how a single-letter change will affect gene activity in that cell. It was trained on measurements from more than 100 million individual cells across 68 tissues, and it can be pointed at a new cell state, like vaccine-activated immune cells, using a sample of just a few dozen cells (Google DeepMind, Nature, The Verge).

As a test, the team asked it about a variant linked to inflammatory bowel disease that a Francis Crick Institute group spent about a year and a half tracing to the ETS2 gene in gut immune cells. The model picked out the same variant and the same effect. “They’ve managed to figure out the answer in 30 minutes, and it took us a year and a half of experiments,” the Crick’s James Lee told Nature. DeepMind says it finds nearly twice as many gene-changing variants as the first version. It’s free for non-commercial research through an online API (Nature, Google DeepMind).

Why it matters: Most genetic links to diseases point at these non-coding switches, and knowing which cells they act in tells researchers where to aim new treatments.

AI news and advances

An AI office suite to rival Microsoft’s: Claude Sheets, Whiteboards and Meetings arrive, and Microsoft stock slips for a second day

At an event in San Francisco on Tuesday, Anthropic launched new Claude tools for office workers who don’t write code. Claude Sheets lets the AI build and edit spreadsheets alongside people, Claude Whiteboards gives teams a shared brainstorming board, and Claude Meetings can join a call, take notes and turn the discussion into tasks. Sheets and Whiteboards are in beta for all paid plans, Meetings is in beta for Team and Enterprise customers, and Claude Data, a dashboard tool, is now generally available (The Verge, TechCrunch, Anthropic).

Anthropic also added “interactive connectors” that open partner apps such as Adobe’s PDF editor, HubSpot and Atlassian right inside Claude. On Wednesday, Microsoft shares fell about 2%, a second straight drop that CNBC tied to worries the tools could compete with Office. The launch comes weeks before Anthropic’s planned stock market debut (CNBC, The Verge).

Why it matters: Spreadsheets, meetings and whiteboards are where most office time goes, so a serious AI rival to Microsoft and Google here could change the software millions of people use at work.

FFans of Jackie, Big Bear’s famous livestream eagle, sent so many cards to the centre that cared for her that it papered a whole room with them

Seen on: Facebook, in a thank-you video posted by the Ojai Raptor Center, per the Los Angeles Times and Popular Science.

Jackie and her mate, Shadow, had been watched by millions on a nest camera above Big Bear Lake, California, since 2018. In July she was found weak and unable to fly after a fight with two younger eagles, and she was treated at the Ojai Raptor Center until she died in August (Los Angeles Times, NBC Los Angeles).

In a video the centre posted on Facebook, director Chris Eksteen shows a staff break room with its walls, door and table covered in cards, letters and children’s drawings from every U.S. state and countries including Australia, France and Germany. So much mail arrived that the local post office set aside a special cart for it. “Thank you for the compassion, thank you for believing in the work that Ojai Raptor Center does,” Eksteen said, and he asked people to support their own local wildlife rescues (Los Angeles Times, Popular Science).

Why it matters: It’s a sad story with a kind ending: strangers around the world thanking the people who quietly care for injured wildlife.
