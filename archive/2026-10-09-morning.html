Province orders Edmonton to scrap its 2024 infill zoning rules within three months and bring back parking minimums

Premier Danielle Smith and Municipal Affairs Minister Dan Williams announced Thursday that Edmonton must, within three months, revert its residential infill rules on housing types, density and height to those in place before the city’s January 2024 zoning overhaul, and restore the minimum on-site parking requirements that applied before 2020. The city then has 18 months to review its land-use bylaw. Calgary, which already repealed blanket rezoning, was told to keep that repeal while its own review finishes next year. Williams said the order will be formalized by cabinet order in council and called it “a reset,” adding: “It’s not an ultimatum, but a conversation.”

Smith said communities had been “blindsided” and that “when municipalities have taken actions that are undermining people’s property value, then we have to step in.” Mayor Andrew Knack said the city was told the day before, warned of higher property taxes and housing costs, and said he will likely call an emergency council meeting next week. Alberta Municipalities president Dylan Bressey called it an “expensive attack on municipal autonomy,” while Coun. Karen Principe and the group Edmonton Neighbourhoods United backed the move.

Why it matters: It decides what can be built on residential lots across Edmonton, including the eight-unit multiplexes that have been approved automatically under the 2024 rules, and touches property values, rents and taxes.

Sources (3)

  • CTV News Edmonton
Community Note

Alberta’s Municipal Government Act lets the province direct a municipality to change its bylaws, so this is within provincial power; the dispute is over whether it should be used here. Claims about effects on property values, taxes and affordability come from each side and haven’t been independently measured yet; city administration has a report on infill’s effect on neighbouring property values due by the third quarter of 2027.

Cold Lake First Nations asks the Federal Court to quash Pacific Link’s “national interest” designation

Cold Lake First Nations filed an application for judicial review in Federal Court on Thursday challenging Ottawa’s Oct. 1 decision to designate the Pacific Link pipeline, which would carry up to one million barrels of oil a day from Alberta to the B.C. coast, as a project in the national interest under the Building Canada Act. It asks the court to quash the designation and declare that Canada owes the Nation a duty to consult.

The application says the decision breached Canadian law and is inconsistent with the honour of the Crown, and that “increasing oilsands production will have significant adverse effects” on members’ Treaty 6 way of life. The Nation says it supports responsible development and wants meaningful consultation on both Pacific Link and the linked Pathways carbon-capture project, whose storage hub would sit in its traditional territory.

Why it matters: Pacific Link is the centrepiece of the Canada–Alberta energy deal; a court fight could affect the plan to finish reviews within a year and start construction next September.

Canada

Ottawa announces $2-billion heat-pump rebate: up to $10,000 for households below median income

Prime Minister Mark Carney announced in Sherbrooke, Que., on Thursday a $2-billion, eight-year program aimed at one million home retrofits. Households below the median income would get up to $10,000 toward a heat pump and other homeowners $2,000; the money also helps apartment owners retrofit 280,000 units. The government says applications will go through an online portal with approval within 24 hours and no required energy audits, and the program is expected to start early next year.

“Heat pumps are cleaner and safer than oil and electric furnaces. They are two to three times more efficient,” Carney said. The Canadian Press reports the program targets people needing emergency or near-term replacement of a heating system, while the earlier oil-to-heat-pump program is being wound down.

Why it matters: Most Alberta homes heat with natural gas, and cold-climate performance matters; the full eligibility rules, including for Albertans, haven’t been published yet.

The emissions estimate (more than 25 million tonnes) and the “two to three times more efficient” claim are the government’s; heat-pump savings depend on climate, model and local gas versus electricity prices, and very cold days may need backup heat.

Major-projects bill C-39 passes its first House vote 296–26 with Conservative support

Bill C-39, the Building Canada Strong Act, passed second reading Thursday 296–26 and was sent to a special legislative committee. The Conservatives voted with the Liberals; the Bloc Québécois, NDP and Greens voted against. The omnibus bill aims to speed approvals for major projects and changes the Canada Labour Code, adding special mediators and a national-interest test for when the labour minister can intervene in a strike or lockout.

NDP parliamentary leader Don Davies said “you cannot build a stronger Canada, at the expense of workers’ rights,” and the Canadian Labour Congress said the labour changes should be removed. Federally regulated employers, through FETCO, said the bill doesn’t go far enough because it doesn’t let the minister act before a work stoppage. The committee isn’t expected to meet until the House returns the week of Oct. 19.

Why it matters: The bill is the legal machinery behind fast-tracking projects like Pacific Link, and its labour rules would apply to federally regulated workplaces in Alberta such as railways, airlines and ports.

Community Note

Second reading approves a bill in principle; it can still be amended at committee and must pass third reading and the Senate. The NDP, which lacks official party status, has no seat on the committee but can propose amendments.

Former UN rights chief Navi Pillay wins the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize

The Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded the prize Friday to Navanethem “Navi” Pillay, 85, “for her efforts to promote peace and international law.” Born in Durban under apartheid, she defended Nelson Mandela and other anti-apartheid activists, then served as a judge on South Africa’s High Court, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda and the International Criminal Court, and as UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. The committee says she now sits as a judge at the International Court of Justice in the case in which Myanmar is accused of genocide.

The committee credited her with a key role when the Rwanda tribunal established that rape and sexual violence could constitute genocide and crimes against humanity. It received 287 nominations. Pillay joked in Nuremberg, “If you clap a little bit more I may share this prize with Trump,” adding, “Being a lawyer and a judge, I still want to verify this.” The prize is worth 12 million Swedish kronor and will be presented in Oslo on Dec. 10.

Why it matters: It’s a pointed endorsement of international courts at a time when, as the committee put it, judges are being sanctioned and their institutions attacked; Canada is a founding supporter of the ICC.

Business

Canada sheds 68,000 jobs in September and unemployment rises to 6.5%, but Alberta adds 23,000

Statistics Canada said Friday that employment fell by 68,000 (−0.3 per cent) in September, a second straight monthly drop after August’s loss of 42,000, and the unemployment rate rose 0.1 points to 6.5 per cent. Economists polled by Reuters had expected a gain of about 9,200 jobs, The Globe and Mail reported before the release. The losses were concentrated among youth aged 15 to 24 (−48,000) and in educational services (−35,000), health care and social assistance (−23,000) and manufacturing (−13,000); public-sector employment fell for a fourth month.

Alberta bucked the trend: employment rose by 23,000 (+0.9 per cent) and the province’s unemployment rate fell 0.4 points to 6.4 per cent; Alberta employment is up 76,000 from a year earlier. Quebec lost 49,000 jobs and B.C. 20,000. Average hourly wages rose 2.3 per cent from a year earlier, and the participation rate fell to 64.8 per cent, the lowest since December 1997 outside the pandemic, which the agency links largely to population aging.

Why it matters: Alberta’s labour market is outperforming the country, while the national weakness is the last jobs report the Bank of Canada sees before its Oct. 28 rate decision.

Science corner

The first working nuclear clocks: teams in Vienna and Beijing keep time with thorium-229 nuclei

Two papers published in Nature this week report the first nuclear clocks, which keep time using a transition inside the atomic nucleus of thorium-229 rather than electrons, as atomic clocks do. A team at TU Wien with Germany’s PTB built a clock that stabilizes itself: thorium in a crystal absorbs laser light only at exactly the right frequency, and the laser is automatically corrected. It ran for more than 24 hours without intervention, with precision of about 10−15 over a day, roughly one second in 30 million years.

A separate team led by Tsinghua University reported a clock about six times as stable as Vienna’s and showed that two separately grown crystals kept the same time, a sign the clocks could be reproduced, the South China Morning Post reports. Writing in Nature, metrologist Akio Kawasaki called the results “a milestone in the field of metrology.”

Why it matters: Because nuclei are far less sensitive to outside disturbances, such clocks could eventually beat today’s best timekeepers, which underpin GPS, telecom networks and tests of fundamental physics.

Sources (4)
  • Nature — News & Views
  • TU Wien — press release
  • South China Morning Post
  • Nature — Huang et al. paper
Community Note

These are lab prototypes and are not yet as precise as the best optical atomic clocks; TU Wien says precision still has to be “drastically improved” with stronger lasers and better crystals. The Vienna team also used its clock in a dark-matter search and found no signal.

Sources: TU Wien; Nature; SCMP.

OpenAI says Iranian and Russian operations used ChatGPT to get fake bylines and narratives into real news outlets

OpenAI said Thursday it banned accounts behind two influence operations. An Iran-origin network created seven fake journalist personas and used ChatGPT to pitch and polish articles, mainly about the U.S.–Iran war; OpenAI found almost 100 articles published or syndicated across about a dozen small-to-medium outlets. OpenAI rated the two campaigns 4 and 5 on its six-point impact scale, the first time it has reported operations it considers high-impact.

Why it matters: It shows AI-assisted propaganda reaching readers through legitimate-looking outlets, not just bot accounts, which is exactly why this site checks every story against several independent sources.
