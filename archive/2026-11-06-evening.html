Elections Alberta pegs the October referendum at about $84 million, and MLAs approve nearly $60 million in extra funding

Chief electoral officer Gordon McClure told the Standing Committee on Legislative Offices that Elections Alberta projects the Oct. 19 provincial referendum cost $84,268,919 to administer. The agency’s base budget before the vote was about $34 million; late Thursday the bipartisan committee unanimously approved a $59,552,176 supplemental — including about $49.6 million to cover referendum operations, $9 million toward the Oct. 18, 2027 general election, and nearly $1 million for extra organizational capacity (Elections Alberta, Edmonton Journal, CTV News Edmonton).

McClure called the 10-question vote an “unprecedented” event: more than 22 million ballots were hand-counted under Alberta’s ban on tabulators, with more than 47,000 temporary workers involved. Counting staff alone ran about $26 million. Roughly 70% of 3.15 million eligible voters cast a ballot on at least one question. He said the office still expects to deliver the 2027 election on the new electoral map (Elections Alberta, Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: Albertans now have a price tag for last month’s multi-question referendum, and Elections Alberta is already shifting cash toward the 2027 campaign.

Smith and Corb Lund clash over how the premier handled his anti-coal petition versus Jeffrey Rath’s

Musician Corb Lund told the Edmonton Journal that a May meeting with Premier Danielle Smith about his Water Not Coal petition felt unfair after separatist petitioner Jeffrey Rath posted text exchanges showing Smith advising him on citizen-initiative rules. Lund said he waited years for a meeting and wanted clarity on ballot wording and the government website, not a process tutorial. Smith’s office called the discussion “cordial,” said the government engaged meaningfully, and pointed to Eastern Slopes protections Lund “neglected to mention” (Edmonton Journal, Postmedia).

In the legislature, Smith said explaining regulations to Albertans — including Lund and Rath — is “part of the job.” Elections Alberta earlier found Lund’s petition about 5,600 signatures short after validation; his lawyers were in Court of King’s Bench in Edmonton on Friday for a procedural step toward a March judicial-review hearing (Edmonton Journal, CTV News Calgary, Global News).

Why it matters: The fight over who got what help from the premier keeps Alberta’s coal-on-the-slopes politics alive while Lund’s court challenge proceeds.

Canada sheds 4,700 jobs in October and unemployment hits a six-month high of 6.7%

Statistics Canada’s Labour Force Survey for October shows employment little changed (−4,700; about 0.0%) while the unemployment rate rose 0.2 percentage points to 6.7%, the highest since April’s 6.9%. Analysts polled by Reuters had expected a gain of about 13,600 jobs and a steady 6.5% jobless rate. Full-time work fell 18,100; private-sector employment dropped 53,000 — its first decline since February — while the public sector rose about 50,000 (Statistics Canada, Reuters, The Globe and Mail).

Wholesale and retail trade led losses (−about 29,000), with transportation and warehousing also down; construction and educational services added jobs. Youth unemployment jumped 0.7 points to 13.7%. Alberta employment fell about 24,000 (−0.9%). Average hourly wage growth for employees cooled to roughly 2.2% year over year. Markets dialed December Bank of Canada hike odds to about 35% as the loonie slid to a nearly three-week low (Statistics Canada, Globe and Mail, Financial Post).

Why it matters: A third straight soft jobs print cools the case for another BoC hike next month and underlines how trade and cost pressures are hitting private payrolls.

Ottawa will extend the foreign-buyer ban on existing homes to 2029, while carving out new builds

Budget 2026 materials and housing coverage say the federal government will prolong the prohibition on non-Canadians buying existing residential properties through Jan. 1, 2029. New residential properties would be exempt, though the budget text does not fully define “new” or lock an implementation calendar — legislation and regulations still have to follow (The Globe and Mail, National Post, Canadian Mortgage Professional / MPA).

Realtors and mortgage brokers note buyer uncertainty after the U.S. midterms and recent rate moves, and say clarifying the new-build carve-out will matter for condo and purpose-built rental marketing (Globe and Mail real-estate wrap, MPA).

Why it matters: Anyone shopping for a resale home — or selling one to a non-resident — gets a longer runway of the existing ban, while new-supply sales may reopen to foreign buyers once rules are written.

UK and France lead ~2,800 troops in Exercise Koalicja, the first big drill for a post-ceasefire Ukraine force

Britain and France will jointly lead about 2,800 troops from 11 countries in Exercise Koalicja (“coalition”) in southeastern Poland from Nov. 9 to 22. The Multinational Force Ukraine (MNF-U) — the military arm of the Coalition of the Willing — will rehearse moving units across borders, operating under one command, and keeping a force supplied. France contributes about 1,100 troops; the UK about 300, including 7th Brigade (“Desert Rats”), plus RAF Typhoons and a Voyager tanker simulating how allies could reassure civilian flights if Ukraine reopens its skies (UK Ministry of Defence, France 24 / AFP, Kyiv Post).

Poland will host land elements near Nowa Dęba, about 100 km from the Ukrainian border, but says it would keep its own soldiers out of Ukraine after a ceasefire while coordinating transit. Turkey will run a maritime planning cell in Istanbul on mine clearance for commercial shipping. Commanders stress the force is not NATO, not a peacekeeping or ceasefire-monitoring mission, and would enter Ukraine only after fighting stops and at Kyiv’s request (GOV.UK, France 24, Kyiv Post).

Why it matters: Allies are practising the logistics of a postwar presence even though no ceasefire exists — a signal to Kyiv, Moscow and Canadian partners watching European security.

Science corner

Science corner: a cholera-biofilm peptide that sticks like glue — even under water

Researchers report in Science Advances that a short peptide drawn from Vibrio cholerae’s biofilm adhesin Bap1 can bind wet glass, plastics and lipid membranes. In lab tests, macroscopic wet glass-to-glass bonding exceeded about 80 kPa, with performance especially strong in salty water — the kind of setting that defeats many ordinary polymer glues (Science Advances, Nature research highlight, Nov. 6).

The work points toward medical and marine adhesives that have to cure or hold in wet conditions, from wound closure to underwater repair. Scaling, toxicity and shelf life are still open questions before any commercial glue could ship (Nature, Yale biofilm-lab publication list).

Why it matters: A pathogen’s trick for sticking to surfaces could seed safer wet-environment adhesives — if engineers can bottle the peptide without the germ.

Sources (3)
  • Science Advances: A biofilm-derived peptide as an underwater adhesive (Huang et al.)
  • Nature: Cholera bacterium’s good deed — inspiring a glue with rare properties
  • Yale Yan Laboratory publications list citing the Science Advances paper
Community Note

OpenAI says it won’t use NDAs with public officials on future “compute campus” projects

OpenAI published a National Community Compact and Compute Campus Policy Blueprint, pledging that every future compute campus it owns and operates will drop non-disclosure agreements with public officials except for narrow security, trade-secret or competitively sensitive carve-outs. The company also says it will pay the full cost of electricity and related infrastructure, publish jobs and hiring reports, and write local compacts shaped by resident priorities (OpenAI, The Verge, Reuters).

The announcement follows midterm backlash against data-centre expansion and tracks Amazon’s October move to stop using NDAs with government agencies on similar projects. OpenAI frames the package as both voluntary commitments and a policy ask for state utility commissions and lawmakers (OpenAI, The Verge, TechCrunch on Amazon’s earlier NDA shift).

Why it matters: Communities hosting AI power plants get a clearer transparency baseline — and Alberta towns courting campuses can measure pitches against it.
