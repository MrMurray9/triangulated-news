Alberta

Edmonton council heads to an Oct. 16 special meeting on the province’s infill rollback order

A day after Premier Danielle Smith ordered Edmonton to return its zoning rules on housing types, density and building heights to their earlier form and restore pre-2020 minimum on-site parking, the city and province remained at odds Friday. Council has three months to comply and has scheduled a special meeting for Oct. 16 to discuss the directive and possible next steps, Global News reported. The province says the rules will stay in place while Edmonton spends 18 months on a broader land-use review with public consultation.

Smith said the change addresses “unintended pressures on utilities, water supply, parking, [and] other municipal services.” Mayor Andrew Knack has said it will raise taxes and housing costs, and Alberta Municipalities called it provincial overreach. University of Alberta housing researcher Damian Collins told CTV News the city’s goal of putting half of new housing in established neighbourhoods depends heavily on infill, and that without it more growth would land on the city’s edges. The group Edmonton Neighbourhoods United welcomed the order as a chance for “meaningful community engagement.”

Why it matters: The rules decide what can be built on many Edmonton residential lots, including how dense new homes can be and how much parking new projects must include.

What changed since yesterday: The order was covered in yesterday morning’s edition; council has now set an Oct. 16 special meeting and municipal leaders have formally objected.

Watchdog: man who died in Edmonton police custody lay down in a release cell without biometric monitoring

The Alberta Serious Incident Response Team (ASIRT) released new details Friday on the Sept. 17 death of a 27-year-old man held at the Edmonton Police Service’s Detainee Management Unit. He was arrested Sept. 16 for alleged shoplifting and breaching release conditions, screened by an on-site paramedic and placed in a cell with biometric monitoring.

After a justice of the peace ordered his release by video the next day, staff moved him just after 5:20 p.m. into a release cell with two other detainees while paperwork was finalized. That cell had cameras but no biometric monitoring. Video shows him moving until about 6:40 p.m., when he lay down on the floor; an officer found him unconscious at 7:02 p.m. He died before he could be taken to hospital.

Why it matters: It raises questions about how detainees are monitored in Edmonton’s holding cells in the final hours before release.

Canada

Supreme Court of Canada splits 5–4 to uphold a three-year sentence in an Indigenous intimate-partner violence case

In R. v. Cope, released Friday, the majority dismissed the Crown’s appeal and let stand the reduced three-year sentence of Harry Arthur Cope, a Mi’kmaw man with severe mental-health challenges who pleaded guilty in 2022 to aggravated assault against his partner, a Mi’kmaw woman. He had originally been sentenced to five years.

The court set out a three-step approach: sentencing judges must consider the Criminal Code provisions on the over-incarceration of Indigenous people (the Gladue principles) together with newer provisions recognizing Indigenous women’s heightened vulnerability to violence, rather than treating them as competing. The majority said denunciation and deterrence don’t automatically require a longer prison term. The four dissenting judges said the result fails to reflect Parliament’s direction toward tougher sentences for such abuse, The Globe and Mail reported.

Why it matters: The ruling now guides how judges across Canada, sentence intimate-partner violence cases involving Indigenous offenders and victims.

Sources (4)
  • Supreme Court of Canada — R. v. Cope
  • Supreme Court of Canada — Case in Brief (PDF)
  • The Globe and Mail
  • Law360 Canada
Community Note

The decision doesn’t create lighter sentences across the board: it says the two sets of provisions must be weighed together on the facts of each case, and a sentence can still be long where the circumstances call for it.

Sources: Supreme Court of Canada; The Globe and Mail.

FBI arrests a Canadian citizen it calls a key suspect in the ShinyHunters breach of its jobs website

FBI Director Kash Patel said Friday that agents had arrested “another suspected co-conspirator” of the ShinyHunters group, which the bureau believes is behind last month’s breach of FBIjobs.gov, a platform run by a third-party vendor. The New York Times reported the suspect is a Canadian citizen arrested in Pennsylvania and considered a primary co-conspirator, according to BleepingComputer.

The hackers claimed to have stolen 2 to 3 terabytes of data. Samples shared with media outlets confirmed exposed information on current and former FBI employees, including home addresses, Social Security numbers, sensitive job assignments and family members’ details, according to BleepingComputer. Patel said it was the latest of several arrests “in a matter of days” involving the network.

Why it matters: A Canadian accused in a major U.S. federal breach could face extradition questions and shows how far ShinyHunters, also blamed for corporate data thefts, reaches.

World

Magnitude 7.7 earthquake collapses buildings and roads in central Panama; at least 27 hurt, no deaths reported

The quake struck at 12:56 p.m. local time Friday (11:56 a.m. MDT), centred about 10 km from the small town of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, the U.S. Geological Survey said. The USGS first estimated magnitude 8.0, then revised it to 7.6 and 7.7. A magnitude 6.6 quake followed that afternoon, among dozens of aftershocks. A tsunami warning for the eastern Pacific was issued and later lifted.

Panama’s National Civil Protection System reported 27 people with minor injuries and no deaths, AFP reported. President José Raúl Mulino said “we are facing a major disaster,” with some roads “completely collapsed,” and went to Penonomé in Coclé province, where the bell tower of a 16th-century cathedral fell. Health Minister Fernando Boyd said Herrera, Coclé and Los Santos were hardest hit. The Panama Canal Authority said shipping was operating normally.

Why it matters: Panama is a popular destination for Canadian travellers and retirees, and the canal carries a significant share of Asia–North America trade.

Isaias makes landfall near Destin, Fla., as a Category 2 hurricane; hundreds of thousands lose power

The U.S. National Hurricane Center said Isaias came ashore near Destin on the Florida Panhandle at 8:30 p.m. CDT Friday (7:30 p.m. MDT) with sustained winds of 165 km/h after weakening from Category 3 before landfall. A gauge at Panama City Beach recorded water about 1.7 metres (5.6 feet) above normal high tide, and a gust of 187 km/h was measured near Santa Rosa Sound.

The storm weakened quickly as it moved north through Alabama and was declared post-tropical by early Saturday. Hundreds of thousands of people in Alabama and the Panhandle were without power, The Associated Press reported; FOX Weather put outages above 750,000 at the peak. Heavy rain and flash-flood risk continue across the U.S. Southeast through the weekend.

Why it matters: Many Canadian snowbirds own or rent along the Gulf Coast, and flights and travel through the region are disrupted this weekend.

What changed since yesterday: Isaias had peaked at Category 3 in yesterday evening’s edition; it has now made landfall at Category 2 and weakened over land.

BCE, Rogers and Telus shares slide as SpaceX buys low-band spectrum for Starlink Mobile

SpaceX agreed Thursday to buy Grain Management’s nationwide U.S. 800 MHz spectrum, up to 14 MHz of low-band airwaves that travel long distances and through buildings, for a planned Starlink Mobile network that would mix satellites and ground towers. The price wasn’t disclosed and the deal needs U.S. Federal Communications Commission approval. It adds to 65 MHz of mid-band spectrum SpaceX is buying from EchoStar, Ars Technica reported.

Telecom stocks fell Friday on fears of satellite competition. T-Mobile slid 9.1 per cent and AT&T 7.4 per cent, and the TSX telecom index was down 4.5 per cent at one point, with BCE down nearly 5 per cent at midday, Reuters reported. Rogers closed down 3.7 per cent at $42.46, according to MarketWatch data.

Why it matters: Canadian carriers’ share prices matter to many pension and retirement portfolios, and more satellite-to-phone competition could eventually affect what Canadians pay for wireless.

Science corner

“DNA Typewriter” records the family tree of 1.3 million cells in a developing mouse

In a paper published in Science this week, a team led by Jay Shendure of the University of Washington and Chengxiang Qiu of Dartmouth College inserted a molecular recorder, called DNA Typewriter, into the genome of a fertilized mouse egg. Each time a cell divides, the recorder uses gene editing to “type” a new mark onto a stretch of DNA, in order and without overwriting earlier marks, so every descendant cell carries a log of its ancestry.

Reading those logs out of about 1.3 million cells, the team reconstructed the cells’ family tree from the first division of the egg to day 13.5 of development, when organs are forming. A redesigned “tape” made the records easier to read from individual cells, the university said.

Why it matters: Tracing which early cells give rise to which tissues could help researchers understand birth defects, developmental disorders and cancer.

Sources (3)
  • UW Medicine — news release
  • Science — news coverage
  • bioRxiv — preprint of the study
Community Note

This was done in a single mouse embryo in the lab; it’s a research tool, not a medical treatment. The findings have been described by the team and the university; independent researchers will need to reproduce them.

Sources: UW Medicine; Science.

Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were pushed out for prioritizing safety; OpenAI says they mishandled sensitive data

Mikita Balesni, Tomek Korbak and Jasmine Wang, dismissed by OpenAI last week, published an open letter Thursday to the company’s Safety and Security Committee and advisory groups denying they broke company policy. They warned their firing has made former colleagues “afraid to speak” and could weaken work with outside safety evaluators. “I believe we were fired for prioritizing safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI as a corporation,” Balesni wrote on X.

Korbak said he had raised concerns that OpenAI was “losing the ability to monitor what AI agents think.” OpenAI has repeatedly denied the allegations, saying the three were fired for “accessing and handling sensitive company information” outside established procedures, reportedly involving sharing with a third-party AI safety organization, and that it hasn’t abandoned its safety commitments.

Why it matters: OpenAI’s models are used by Canadian governments and businesses, and outside testing by independent safety groups is one of the few checks on how they behave.
