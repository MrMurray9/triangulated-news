Alberta

Alberta unveils its first new licence plate in more than 40 years: Moraine Lake, and a “Strong and Free” motto

Albertans can pick up a newly designed standard licence plate at registry agents starting Monday, Nov. 9. The plate shows Moraine Lake in Banff National Park and swaps the old “Wild Rose Country” line for “Strong and Free.” It is the province’s first new official plate design in more than four decades (Alberta.ca, Edmonton Journal, CTV News Edmonton, CityNews Edmonton).

Switching is optional, and existing plates stay valid as long as they are readable. Drivers who want one before renewal pay about $35; at renewal it costs the regular registry service charge. A replacement comes with a new plate number, so drivers will need to update their insurer and any toll or parking accounts (Alberta.ca, StrathmoreNow, CityNews Edmonton).

Why it matters: Almost every vehicle in the province will eventually carry it, and anyone who switches early has to tell their insurer about the new number.

Eye surgeons ask Alberta to reverse a new rule letting trained optometrists do laser treatments and minor surgery; the minister says no

Since Nov. 1, Alberta optometrists who complete extra training and get approval from their regulatory college may perform selected office-based laser treatments for glaucoma and minor procedures such as removing benign eyelid lesions and treating styes. Alberta is the first province to allow it (The Canadian Press, Edmonton Journal, Alberta College of Optometrists).

The Canadian Ophthalmological Society, the Eye Physicians and Surgeons Association of Alberta and the AMA’s ophthalmology section say the training, including a 40-hour University of Waterloo course, is no substitute for medical school and residency. Primary Health Minister Justin Wright says he heard the concerns “loud and clear” but is going ahead, arguing it cuts travel for rural patients and that the province rejected many of the expansions optometrists wanted (The Canadian Press, CTV News Calgary, Canadian Ophthalmological Society).

Why it matters: It changes who can treat your eyes in Alberta, and the province hasn’t published data showing how much it will shorten waits.

Bill 39 rewrites how Alberta’s involuntary addiction-treatment law will work before it opens next spring, and ends funding for some lab tests

The province tabled Bill 39, the Health Statutes Amendment Act, 2026 (No. 2), on Wednesday. It changes the Compassionate Intervention Act before it takes effect: applications would be triaged by risk and by how many beds are open, registered psychiatric nurses could apply for an order, applications could be withdrawn, and the rules on giving medication without consent are spelled out (Alberta.ca, Edmonton Journal, Red Deer Advocate/The Canadian Press).

Mental Health and Addiction Minister Rick Wilson says the government is converting existing facilities and beds and wants compassionate intervention running early next spring. The same bill would stop public funding for lab tests deemed non-essential. NDP Leader Naheed Nenshi says forced treatment fails without housing for people once they leave (CityNews Edmonton, Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: Alberta is building one of Canada’s first systems for court-ordered addiction treatment, and this bill sets who can ask for it and how beds are rationed.

Canada

Most Canadians still want fewer newcomers in 2027: 52% back lower immigration levels, a Nanos poll finds

A Nanos Research survey for CTV News, taken Oct. 31 to Nov. 2, asked about Ottawa’s plan to admit 380,000 permanent residents and about 385,000 temporary workers and students. About 52% of respondents said Canada should accept fewer, a level Nanos says has barely moved since March (Nanos Research, CTV News).

The survey reached 1,058 adults by phone and online and is accurate to within about 3 percentage points, 19 times out of 20. It lands just as the federal budget sets spending for the next few years, while housing and job pressures keep immigration levels a political flashpoint (Nanos Research, Canadian Mortgage Professional, Immigration2Canada).

Why it matters: Ottawa sets immigration targets every year, and public opinion this lopsided makes it hard for any party to push the numbers back up.

Ottawa projects a $62.3-billion deficit in “Masters of our Destiny,” pairing a 50% grocery-benefit boost with $36 billion in business write-offs

Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne tabled Budget 2026, titled “Masters of our Destiny,” in the House on Thursday. It forecasts a $62.3-billion deficit this fiscal year, about $62.5 billion next year and $52.9 billion in 2030–31. Ottawa says stronger growth, high oil prices and cuts to the public service, which is on track to shrink by 10%, trimmed earlier projections, but roughly $146 billion in new measures soaked up most of the gain (iPolitics, Parliament Today, Department of Finance).

For households, the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit gets a 50% top-up in 2027, which Finance pegs at $4.1 billion for more than 12 million people. The Canada Workers Benefit is widened, the caregiver credit becomes fully refundable, and $11.2 billion is set aside over 10 years to end chronic homelessness. The biggest single item is the $36-billion Productivity Mega Deduction letting businesses write off equipment right away. The budget assumes 1% growth this year and 1.7% next (Department of Finance, iPolitics, Parliament Today).

What changed since the last edition: This morning we previewed budget day. Champagne has now tabled the actual plan: a $62.3-billion deficit for 2026–27, falling only to $52.9 billion by 2030–31, plus the first confirmed spending lines.

Why it matters: It is the plan behind your benefit cheques, business taxes and federal borrowing for the next five years, and it rests on oil prices that are already sliding.

Done in 6 hours 59 minutes: Kutryk and Menon bolt on the ISS’s eighth and final new solar array

Joshua Kutryk of the Canadian Space Agency and NASA’s Anil Menon wrapped up a 6-hour-59-minute spacewalk on Thursday after installing and deploying the last of eight roll-out solar arrays on the station’s 3B power channel. NASA says that completes a campaign begun in 2021 and lifts the station’s power-generating capacity from about 160 to about 215 kilowatts (NASA, Canadian Space Agency, The Globe and Mail).

It was Kutryk’s second outing in a week; the first, on Oct. 29, ran 7 hours 11 minutes. He is the fifth Canadian to work outside the ISS (NASA, Canadian Space Agency, The News International).

What changed since the last edition: This morning the spacewalk was still ahead. It has now finished, and NASA confirms the last roll-out array is installed and unfurled.

Why it matters: More power on the station keeps experiments running through the end of the decade, including Canadian ones, and a Canadian did a share of the work.

Paraguay’s president quits, then is impeached 78–0, after a bank tied to him collapses and freezes the savings of 3 million people

President Santiago Peña announced his resignation in a video on X on Thursday, apologizing “if at any point I failed.” Minutes later the lower house voted 78–0 to impeach him, sending the case to the Senate, with members of his own Colorado Party joining the opposition. Vice-President Pedro Alliana was expected to be sworn in to serve out the term to August 2028 (NPR/AP, The Guardian, Infobae).

The crisis began Monday when the central bank found Ueno Bank’s capital had fallen below half the legal minimum, then seized it on Tuesday. The digital lender said it had about 3 million customers, around 60% of Paraguay’s adults, and held pensions and up to US$850 million in public funds. Peña held a stake in a company linked to the bank until last year; prosecutors have named three officials to investigate (NPR/AP, Infobae, The Guardian).

Why it matters: A digital bank grew so fast it held most of a country’s savings, and its failure toppled a government in days. Regulators everywhere will be looking at what went wrong.

Nasdaq plunges 5.5%, its worst day since April 2025, as a Qualcomm warning sets off a chip-stock wipeout

U.S. stocks erased their post-midterm rally on Thursday. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% to 24,315, more than 10% below last month’s record, which puts it in a correction. The S&P 500 dropped 4.36% to 7,353.73 and the Dow shed 1,833 points, or 3.44% (CNBC, AP, Yahoo Finance).

Qualcomm and Nvidia each fell more than 9% after Qualcomm’s current-quarter outlook disappointed; Nvidia alone lost almost US$450 billion in market value. AMD, Micron and Applied Materials dropped by double digits. Traders cited worries that a Democratic Congress could slow the AI data-centre buildout or bring tighter AI rules (CNBC, AP, Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: Big tech and chip makers sit inside most Canadian index funds and pension plans, so a rout this size shows up in RRSPs, not just on Wall Street.

Bank of England raises rates to 4%, its first hike in more than three years, and warns another may follow

The Bank of England’s Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1 on Thursday to lift Bank Rate a quarter point, from 3.75% to 4%, citing energy-driven inflation from the Middle East war. Catherine Mann voted for a bigger jump to 4.25% (Bank of England, The Guardian, Reuters).

Governor Andrew Bailey said another increase could come if energy prices keep pushing inflation up, though he said more hikes are not inevitable. The Bank now expects U.K. inflation to peak around 5% next year. The next decision is due Dec. 17 (The Guardian, Reuters, AP, Bank of England).

Why it matters: It’s the clearest sign yet that the war’s energy shock is pushing central banks to raise rates again, which puts pressure on the Bank of Canada’s next moves and on mortgage rates.

Science corner

Science corner: Scentronic, an AI “e-nose” and a scent-puffing wristband that can send a smell over the internet

Ohio State materials scientist Jinghua Li and colleagues have built a two-part smell system. An electronic nose samples the air and a machine-learning model names the odour; the result is sent online to a small wearable, worn as a wristband, necklace or ring, that atomizes tiny amounts of stored scent liquid to recreate it at the other end. The study is in Science Advances (Ohio State News, Science Advances, CNA).

The team trained it on five food smells: coffee, beer, red wine, orange and chocolate cake. In trials, volunteers picked out the single scent the wearable released about 73% of the time and identified pairs about 71% of the time, although people varied a lot in how strongly they smelled each one. Li says the bigger goal is medical: tracking and possibly helping people who lose their sense of smell after illness or brain injury (Ohio State News, Audacy).

Why it matters: Smell is the one sense video calls and VR leave out, and losing it is an early sign of several brain diseases. A cheap way to measure and send it could help with both.

  • Science Advances: Scentronic — an AI-assisted olfactory interface

  • Audacy: Researchers develop electronic nose that can share scents over the internet

AI news and advances

Meta buys Conductor, a two-year-old startup that runs teams of AI coding agents side by side, and its cloud service shuts Nov. 20

Meta has acquired Conductor, a startup whose app lets software developers launch several AI coding agents at once, each working on a separate piece of a project in its own isolated workspace. Axios first reported the deal on Wednesday; Meta confirmed it but did not disclose a price (TechCrunch, Axios, The Verge).

Founder and CEO Charlie Holtz wrote that Conductor is joining Meta Superintelligence Labs, working with chief AI officer Alexandr Wang and product head Nat Friedman, and promised “something new” early next year. The local app keeps working until then, but Conductor Cloud closes on Nov. 20, after which user cloud data will be deleted. Conductor did not build its own models; it ran agents from the big labs. Meta launched its own coding agent, Muse Code, in August (Conductor, TechCrunch, The Verge).

Why it matters: Big tech firms are racing to own the tools programmers use to steer AI agents, and anyone using Conductor’s cloud has about two weeks to move their work.

OSNL UK’s “Nigerian Wicked,” a Nollywood-style spoof with Cynthia Erivo as Glinda, passes 7 million YouTube views

Seen on: YouTube, on the official SNL UK channel, where LateNighter reports the clip passed 7 million views, a record for the show.

In a three-minute pre-taped sketch from her Oct. 24 hosting turn on Sky’s Saturday Night Live UK, Cynthia Erivo swaps her movie role as Elphaba to play Glinda, opposite cast member Ayoade Bamgboye as the green witch, in a deliberately low-budget Nollywood take on Wicked. Instead of Oz, they head to “LagOz,” where Hammed Animashaun’s Wizard turns out to be much less rich than advertised (LateNighter, NME, Chortle).

The clip crossed 1.5 million views in two days and has since become the most-watched video in SNL UK history, nearly catching the U.S. show’s biggest sketches of its current season. Slate called it “the best SNL sketch in a long time,” and The Independent says it may have changed things for Erivo (LateNighter, The Independent).

Why it matters: It’s a sketch that works by loving the thing it spoofs, and it gave a British reboot of an American institution its first breakout hit.
