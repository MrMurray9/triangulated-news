Alberta

Alberta moves to set province-wide report-card standards and make daycares warn parents fast about abuse or neglect

Education and Childcare Minister Demetrios Nicolaides tabled the Securing Parental Involvement bill on Thursday. It would let the minister set province-wide report-card standards, starting in the 2027–28 school year, while school boards still choose the format. Boards would have to tell parents what happens on non-instructional and professional-development days, and public boards would need the minister’s approval before naming or renaming a school (Government of Alberta, CTV News Edmonton, The Canadian Press via CityNews).

For child care, licensed operators would have to notify parents quickly about critical incidents such as a death, neglect or abuse allegations, and the province could tell parents directly. The government links the change to a recent case at a Mill Creek daycare; the child-care rules are expected to take effect March 1, 2027 (Edmonton Journal, Government of Alberta, CTV News Edmonton).

Why it matters: Parents could get clearer report cards and faster warnings if something serious happens at their child’s daycare, two of the most common complaints the government says it heard.

Referendum results are now official: 70.4 per cent turnout, the highest in a provincial vote in at least 50 years, and 74.95 per cent chose to stay in Canada

What changed: The count finished last weekend, but those were unofficial results. Elections Alberta has now certified them and published the final turnout figure.

Elections Alberta released the certified results on Thursday. On the separation question, 1,556,316 people (74.95 per cent) voted to remain in Canada and 520,233 (25.05 per cent) voted to start the process toward a binding separation vote. The questions on provincial control over immigration passed, and the four constitutional questions were rejected (Elections Alberta, CTV News Edmonton, The Canadian Press via CityNews).

Turnout of 70.4 per cent is the highest for an Alberta-wide vote since at least 1975, though still below the 81.8 per cent record set in 1935. The government says immigration legislation would come next spring at the earliest (The Canadian Press via CityNews, Elections Alberta, CTV News Edmonton).

Why it matters: The certified numbers close the book on the count and set the timeline for what the province does next on immigration.

Canada

Ottawa will invest up to $1.6 billion for a stake in Toronto AI company Cohere, which promises to keep its headquarters and 1,000 new jobs in Canada

The federal government announced the equity investment on Thursday as part of a new funding round for Cohere, led by Germany’s Schwarz Group, that aims to raise about $7 billion and would value the company at roughly $36 billion. Nvidia and Bell are also investing (Prime Minister’s Office, The Canadian Press, Reuters).

In return, Cohere commits to stay incorporated and headquartered in Canada, create 1,000 Canadian tech jobs and spend $1.7 billion on computing in Canada by 2030. Ottawa’s money comes in stages, and only the first part of the round has closed (Prime Minister’s Office, Reuters, The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: It is the biggest direct bet Ottawa has made on a homegrown AI company, aimed at keeping key AI technology and talent in Canada instead of relying on U.S. firms.

Kutryk’s first spacewalk is a success: he and Anil Menon spend 7 hours 11 minutes outside and unroll a new solar array

What changed: This morning’s edition went out as the spacewalk was starting. It has now ended, on schedule, with the array unrolled.

Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and NASA’s Anil Menon finished their spacewalk at 3:08 p.m. Eastern (1:08 p.m. in Edmonton) after 7 hours and 11 minutes. They installed and deployed the seventh of eight roll-out solar arrays on the International Space Station (NASA, Space.com, CBS News).

Space.com notes it was the 100th U.S. spacewalk staged from the station. Kutryk, who grew up near Fort Saskatchewan, is the fifth Canadian to walk in space, and a second spacewalk to fit the last array is planned for Nov. 5 (Space.com, NASA, CBS News).

Why it matters: The new arrays keep the aging station powered through its final years, and Canada now has an Albertan with spacewalk experience.

Russian drone hits a crowded train station in Dnipro, killing 14 people

A Russian jet-powered drone struck a platform at the station in Dnipro, central Ukraine, at about 11 a.m. local time on Thursday as a commuter train and a passenger train bound for Zaporizhzhia were stopped there. Ukrainian officials say 14 people were killed and 14 injured, including an 11-year-old. Passengers had been told to leave about 10 minutes before the hit (AFP, Kyiv Independent, Interfax-Ukraine).

Other strikes on Thursday killed two 15-year-old girls in Chernihiv and three people in Kremenchuk, and about 25,000 homes lost power. The attacks came while a small local truce around occupied Oleshky was holding (DW, AFP, Kyiv Independent).

Why it matters: It is one of the deadliest single strikes on Ukrainian civilians in weeks, and it comes as U.S.-backed talks are testing whether limited ceasefires can grow.

Science corner

Science corner: engineers keep a promising chip material working even when it is just a few atoms thick

Multiferroic materials have linked electric and magnetic properties, so data could be written with a tiny voltage and read back magnetically, using far less power than today’s chips. The catch is the “dead layer” effect: shave them thin enough for a chip and they stop working. A Brown University team led by Lucas Caretta reports in Nature Materials that it beat the problem in bismuth ferrite (Brown University, TechXplore, Six State Signal).

They grew the film on a slightly narrower crystal base, dysprosium scandate, that squeezes it into shape, and added a metal layer that soaks up stray electric fields. The film stayed electrically and magnetically active down to a few ångströms and even became an “altermagnet,” a newly recognized type of magnet that could be fast and easy to read (Brown University, TechXplore, Six State Signal).

Why it matters: Low-power memory and logic chips could help rein in the fast-growing energy use of AI data centres.

AI news and advances

Mistral releases its trillion-parameter Large 4 model free to download, pitching it at cybersecurity work

Paris-based Mistral published the weights of Mistral Large 4, nicknamed “Le Chonk,” on Hugging Face on Thursday. It is a mixture-of-experts model with about one trillion parameters, of which about 52 billion are active for each word it produces (TechCrunch, The Verge, Mistral).

Mistral says it matches China’s GLM-5.3 while using 47 per cent fewer tokens, and that it tops Artificial Analysis’s coding-agent ranking among open models. Anyone can run and modify it under a modified MIT licence, but companies selling it as a hosted service need a separate licence (Mistral, Hugging Face, TechCrunch).

Why it matters: A top-tier model that organizations can run on their own servers matters for governments and businesses, including Canadian ones, that don’t want to send sensitive data to U.S. cloud providers.
