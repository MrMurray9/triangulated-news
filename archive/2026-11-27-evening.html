Edmonton’s rebuilt Wellington Bridge on 102 Avenue reopens to drivers three days ahead of schedule

The City of Edmonton reopened the Wellington Bridge on 102 Avenue to traffic Friday evening, three days earlier than planned (CTV News Edmonton; CityNews Edmonton).

The old bridge had reached the end of its service life. It was replaced with a single-span girder bridge, and the work, which started in 2025, was lined up with construction of the nearby Valley Line West LRT (CityNews Edmonton; City of Edmonton).

The new crossing has wider lanes, stronger traffic barriers, handrails, new street lights and separate space for people on bikes and on foot, including a new shared path on the north side. The pathway under the bridge stays closed to pedestrians until the end of 2026 (CityNews Edmonton).

Why it matters: 102 Avenue is a main route between downtown and the west end, so reopening the bridge should ease detours for drivers, cyclists and transit riders.

A former Legion accountant who stole more than $1.2 million and gambled much of it at casinos is sentenced to six years

A Calgary judge sentenced Janice Sugai, 50, to six years in federal prison for stealing $1,233,360.96 from the Royal Canadian Legion’s Alberta–Northwest Territories Command, where she worked as an accountant (Calgary Herald; CTV News Calgary).

Justice Craig Jones also ordered a fine equal to the full amount. If she doesn’t pay it within 10 years of finishing her sentence, she will serve five more years. The new term is on top of two years she is already serving for defrauding a Lethbridge employer (Calgary Herald).

The judge rejected the defence’s argument that a gambling addiction drove the crimes, saying there was no evidence of one, and noted she showed “a total lack of remorse.” Much of the money was gambled at Calgary casinos (Calgary Herald; FM947).

Why it matters: The Legion relies on donations and volunteers to support veterans, and the case shows how one trusted insider can drain a charity’s funds for years.

Canada

The Supreme Court upholds Quebec’s ban on religious symbols for some public workers, but says judges can still call out Charter violations

In a split ruling Friday, the Supreme Court of Canada upheld almost all of Quebec’s secularism law, known as Bill 21, which bars many public-sector workers, including teachers, from wearing religious symbols at work (Supreme Court of Canada; The Globe and Mail).

Six of the seven judges said Quebec used the notwithstanding clause properly. The one part struck down: the law can’t apply to members of the National Assembly, because that would breach the democratic right to run for office (Supreme Court of Canada; CTV News).

The court also ruled 4–3 that judges may declare that a law shielded by the notwithstanding clause still violates the Charter, though it chose not to make that declaration here. A 4–3 majority rejected the argument that the Charter’s gender-equality clause could strike the law down (The Globe and Mail; Supreme Court of Canada).

Why it matters: The ruling settles a seven-year fight over Bill 21 and sets the rules for how far provinces, including Alberta and Ontario, can go in using the notwithstanding clause to override Charter rights.

Sources (4)
  • Supreme Court of Canada: English Montreal School Board v. Quebec (Attorney General), case in brief
  • The Globe and Mail: Supreme Court upholds Quebec’s Bill 21 in landmark ruling
  • CTV News: Supreme Court of Canada upholds Quebec’s secularism law
  • Global News: Reaction to Supreme Court ruling on Quebec secularism law
Community Note

The notwithstanding clause only lasts five years at a time, so Quebec must keep renewing it. The ruling doesn’t stop future governments from changing the law.

Three dissenting judges said the law violates freedom of religion and equality rights. Those views don’t change the outcome but may shape future cases.

Ottawa fast-tracks an 800-km all-season highway down the Mackenzie Valley, the first northern project on its national-interest list

Prime Minister Mark Carney went to Yellowknife Friday to list the Mackenzie Valley Highway as a project of national interest under the Building Canada Act. It is the first project in the North to get the label, which puts one federal minister in charge of the permits instead of several (Prime Minister’s Office; Cabin Radio).

The planned 800-kilometre road would run from Wrigley, where the current all-season highway ends, to the Dempster Highway south of Inuvik. Tulita, Norman Wells and Fort Good Hope would get year-round road access for the first time (Global News; The Globe and Mail).

Ottawa has promised to pay for construction, with $3.1 billion in this year’s budget through 2031, and now puts the cost of the northern and southern sections at $4.2 billion. Carney also tied the road to Arctic defence, saying it would cut more than 1,000 km from the drive between NORAD airfields in Yellowknife and Inuvik (Cabin Radio; The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: It would be the first road link down the Mackenzie Valley for communities that now rely on winter ice roads, barges and planes, which keeps food, fuel and building costs high.

UN agencies now count 16 hunger hotspots, up from 13 in June, as El Niño adds new ones and Sudan faces famine

Hunger will get worse over the next six months in 16 places around the world, the UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Food Programme (WFP) said Friday in their twice-yearly Hunger Hotspots report, up from 13 in June (FAO; Al Jazeera).

Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, northern Nigeria and Yemen are of highest concern, and Sudan and South Sudan face a risk of famine. Up to 13 areas of North Darfur and South Kordofan in Sudan could be in famine by January (UN News; AP).

A strengthening El Niño is behind three of the new entries: southern Africa, Central America’s Dry Corridor and northeastern Uganda. Chad was added because of spillover from Sudan’s war, and Lebanon came off the list. Conflict is the main driver in 12 of the 16 hotspots (Al Jazeera; FAO).

Why it matters: The report is an early warning meant to get aid moving before famine sets in, at a time when aid budgets have been cut sharply.

Dutch customs find 26 critically endangered turtles taped up in a suitcase at Schiphol, nearly a tenth of the species left in the wild

Customs officers at Amsterdam’s Schiphol Airport found 26 live Vallarta mud turtles in the checked bag of a man travelling from Mexico to Hong Kong on Nov. 21, the Netherlands’ food and product safety authority (NVWA) said Friday (NVWA; DW).

Most were packed in a box with a toy car and a few were in a sealed sock, wrapped in duct tape so they couldn’t move. All survived and went to a specialist animal rescue centre. The man was arrested and remains in custody (DW; Reuters via The Independent).

Vallarta mud turtles, among the world’s smallest turtles, were only described by scientists in 2018, and about 300 are thought to remain in the wild in Mexico. A U.S. conservation group says they can sell for up to US$30,000 each in China (DW; Reuters via The Independent).

Why it matters: Losing even a couple of dozen animals matters for a species this small, and the case shows how wildlife smugglers use busy transit airports.

The CRTC rules Bell, Rogers and Telus broke its junk-fee ban and orders the new phone fees gone by Dec. 27

Canada’s telecom regulator ruled Friday that the Big Three carriers broke its ban on activation-style fees, and ordered them to stop charging them by Dec. 27 (The Globe and Mail via The Canadian Press; CRTC).

The CRTC found that Bell’s $40 device-handling fee, Rogers’ $40 device-setup fee and Telus’s $15 SIM and eSIM provisioning fee all broke rules that took effect June 12. Rogers’ shipping and SIM-replacement fees were allowed to stay (The Canadian Press; MobileSyrup).

Carriers that miss the deadline could face penalties of up to $10 million, and managers up to $25,000. In a separate decision, the CRTC said Bell broke consumer rules by locking phones for up to 60 days, and will consider a penalty (MobileSyrup; CRTC).

Why it matters: Anyone buying a phone with a new plan this holiday season has been paying these extra charges, and the ruling should take $15 to $40 off the cost of signing up.

Spain’s Puig, maker of Paco Rabanne and Charlotte Tilbury, pays €1.2 billion for 75% of luxury perfume houses Parfums de Marly and Initio

Puig said Friday it will buy 75% of Artessence, the parent of niche perfume brands Parfums de Marly and Initio Parfums Privés, from private-equity firm Advent International for €1.2 billion in cash, about US$1.3 billion (WWD; The Business of Fashion).

The deal values Artessence at about US$1.9 billion. Founder Julien Sprecher’s holding company keeps the other 25%, and the two sides agreed on an earn-out and options that could let Puig own all of it over time. Closing is expected in the second quarter of 2027 (WWD; EFE).

Artessence expects about US$420 million to $440 million in sales this year and roughly $180 million to $190 million in operating profit before interest, taxes and depreciation (WWD).

Why it matters: High-end “niche” perfumes are one of the fastest-growing parts of the beauty business, and big groups are paying up to own them.

Science corner

Science corner: a man-made protein “glue” keeps chromosomes paired in older mouse eggs, a possible new tool for IVF

As eggs age, a natural protein called cohesin, which holds paired copies of each chromosome together, wears away and isn’t replaced. Copies can then split too early, leaving eggs with the wrong number of chromosomes, a major reason fertility drops from the mid-30s (Max Planck Society; Nature).

Melina Schuh’s team at the Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Göttingen built a stand-in called TALhesin. Two programmable DNA-binding proteins latch on near the middle of each chromosome and lock together, and a built-in cut site lets the cell’s own “scissors” release them at the right moment (Science; Max Planck Society).

In eggs from 17-month-old mice, which counts as old for a mouse, the share of eggs with no early splitting rose from 46.7% to 74.1%, almost matching young mice at 74.7%. The proteins also attached to the right spots on chromosomes in donated human eggs (Nature; Max Planck Society).

Why it matters: It is the first attempt to replace cohesin’s job with an engineered system rather than just study its loss, which could one day help IVF patients in their late 30s and 40s.

Sources (5)
  • Science: An engineered cohesion system reduces errors in aged mammalian eggs
  • Nature: Chromosome ‘glue’ could boost fertility by stabilizing ageing eggs
  • Max Planck Society: An engineered “glue” for aged eggs
  • ThePrint: Can artificial glue reverse fertility decline? Scientists test it in mice
  • Science Media Centre Spain: Expert reactions
Community Note

This is lab work in mouse eggs. The study did not show more healthy embryos or pregnancies, and testing in human eggs only showed where the proteins attach. Any clinical use is years away and would need extensive safety testing.

Sources: Science (research paper); Nature news; Max Planck Society; ThePrint; Science Media Centre Spain.

Nvidia tells Dell and Super Micro to hold AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries while it checks U.S. export rules

Nvidia has told server makers including Dell and Super Micro to pause shipments of AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia for an unspecified time, according to reports first published by The Wall Street Journal and the Financial Times (TechCrunch; Malay Mail).

The company is checking whether its partners in the region meet new U.S. export requirements. Earlier this month, the U.S. Commerce Department moved to close a loophole that let Chinese companies rent Nvidia chips remotely from data centres in those countries (TechCrunch; Quartz).

Those remote data centres supply about 24% of China’s AI computing power, according to an estimate by the think tank Institute for Progress cited by the Journal (TechCrunch).

Why it matters: Southeast Asia has become a major hub for AI data centres, and the pause shows how U.S. efforts to limit China’s access to chips are now reaching U.S. allies and trading partners.

Ottawa opens $700 million more for AI computing and will cover 80% of the bill if companies buy from Canadian-owned providers

AI Minister Evan Solomon announced the next phase of the AI Compute Access Fund in Toronto on Thursday, opening the $700 million added earlier this year and bringing the program to $1 billion (BetaKit; The Globe and Mail).

Small and medium-sized Canadian companies spending $100,000 to $5 million on processing power can now get up to 80% of the cost covered if they buy from a sovereign, Canadian-owned provider, or up to 50% from other cloud providers. Before, the top rate was 67% (BetaKit; Government of Canada).

Funding now goes out sector by sector, starting with health and life sciences projects (Government of Canada; The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Big tech firms have bought up much of the world’s AI computing power, and the fund is meant to help smaller Canadian firms get access while steering money to homegrown data centres.

OA 12-year-old dog named Daisy rides a kayak down a flooded Jersey Shore street like it’s a normal afternoon

Seen on: Instagram and YouTube, where ABC7 New York shared owner Robin Shaffer’s footage; Pet Rescue Report says the clip “quickly went viral.”

When a powerful coastal storm flooded streets in Ocean City, New Jersey, Robin Shaffer put his senior corgi mix, Daisy, in an olive-green kayak to keep her safe and floated her down the street toward the boardwalk (ABC News; Pet Rescue Report).

His video shows Daisy sitting calmly and looking around as if she does this every day. Once they were back at their front steps, she got a treat (Pet Rescue Report; Outdoors.com).

Shaffer told WHYY the water rose “much, much faster” than he expected, with about three feet flooding under the boardwalk and onto his street. “It was like living in Venice, Italy, for periods of time,” he said (WHYY).

Why it matters: It’s a small, sweet moment from a storm that caused serious flooding and power outages along the U.S. East Coast.
