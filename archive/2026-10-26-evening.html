Alberta becomes the first province to let optometrists do minor surgery and some laser eye treatments, starting Nov. 1

From Sunday, Alberta optometrists with extra training can remove benign skin lesions around the eye, treat styes and perform certain laser procedures, including some for glaucoma and for clouding after cataract surgery. The province says it is the first in Canada to allow this (Edmonton Journal, CityNews, Government of Alberta).

Optometrists must complete more training, exams and supervised clinical work, then get authorization from the Alberta College of Optometrists before offering the procedures. The services will be publicly covered. “We’re removing unnecessary barriers to care, not lowering the standards to protect patients,” said Health Minister Justin Wright (CityNews, 650 CKOM/Canadian Press).

Why it matters: People in smaller towns often wait months or drive hours to see an ophthalmologist for simple procedures. This could shorten that wait, if enough optometrists sign up.

Sources (4)
  • Edmonton Journal — Alberta expands scope of practice for optometrists
  • CityNews Edmonton — Alberta to expand eye care services in November
  • 650 CKOM (Canadian Press) — Alberta allowing optometrists to perform minor laser procedures
  • Alberta College of Optometrists — Optometrist scope expansion
Community Note

This is not LASIK or vision-correction surgery. The procedures are minor ones, and not every optometrist will be allowed to do them on Nov. 1: each one needs separate authorization first. Eye surgeons’ groups elsewhere have raised safety concerns about similar changes in some U.S. states, so how well it works here is still to be seen.

Sources: Alberta College of Optometrists; Edmonton Journal; CityNews.

Smith joins the Conservative push to scrap the B.C. north-coast oil tanker ban, with a Commons vote set for Wednesday

The House of Commons debated Bill C-264 on Monday, a private member’s bill from Calgary MP David McKenzie that would repeal the Oil Tanker Moratorium Act. That law bars tankers carrying more than 12,500 tonnes of crude from B.C.’s northern coast. The Liberals asked for a recorded vote, now set for Wednesday (Canadian Press, Parliament of Canada).

Premier Danielle Smith backed repeal: “Canada cannot become a global energy superpower while federal policies continue to stand in the way of getting our energy to the world.” Conservatives point to a Strathcona Resources plan to move up to 250,000 barrels a day by rail to Prince Rupert. But Ottawa promised B.C. in July to keep the ban “without alteration,” and the planned Alberta–West Coast pipeline was routed south to respect it (Canadian Press, Western Standard).

Why it matters: Where Alberta oil can reach the ocean affects the price it gets and the province’s budget. A private member’s bill rarely passes without government support, so Wednesday’s vote is a test of where MPs stand.

Sources (3)
  • 650 CKOM (Canadian Press) — Federal Conservatives propose bill that would end B.C.’s tanker ban
  • Parliament of Canada (LEGISinfo) — Bill C-264, An Act to repeal certain restrictions on shipping
  • Western Standard — Smith, Poilievre push Carney to scrap oil tanker ban
Community Note

Repealing the ban would not approve any pipeline or terminal; projects would still need their own reviews. The Liberals signalled they don’t plan to support repeal, so passage is unlikely. Western Standard is an openly conservative outlet; the facts here are confirmed by Canadian Press and Parliament’s own records.

Sources: Canadian Press; Parliament of Canada; Western Standard.

Canada

Eric Bauza, the Toronto-born voice of today’s Bugs Bunny and Daffy Duck, has died at 46

Eric Bauza, who grew up in Scarborough, died at his home in Los Angeles. The Los Angeles County medical examiner listed the cause as hypertensive cardiovascular disease (AP, Variety, The Guardian).

Bauza was the main voice of Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, Porky Pig and Tweety for Warner Bros. Animation for years, with more than 400 credits and three Emmy wins, including for Looney Tunes Cartoons and Bugs Bunny Builders. He “followed in the footsteps of giants like Mel Blanc,” said Warner Bros. Television’s Channing Dungey. He had been working on a planned Looney Tunes musical (AP, Variety).

Why it matters: For a whole generation of kids, his was the voice of Bugs and Daffy. He was also a Canadian success story in an industry that is hard to break into.

Sources (3)

  • The Guardian — Eric Bauza, award-winning Looney Tunes voice actor, dies aged 46
Community Note

The cause of death comes from the coroner’s listing reported by AP and Variety; no further details have been released. Older Looney Tunes episodes still feature Mel Blanc and other actors, so not every Bugs voice you hear is Bauza’s.

Sources: AP; Variety; The Guardian.

Update: Carney promises a process to “move Canada beyond the Indian Act” after the first First Nations–first ministers meeting since 2005

What changed since the last edition: On Sunday morning we previewed this meeting. It has now happened, and Carney came out of it with a promise to start replacing or reforming the Indian Act, plus new money for policing and jobs.

Prime Minister Mark Carney told ministers to work with First Nations on a process to move past the 150-year-old Indian Act, whether that means reforming it or abolishing it. Manitoba Premier Wab Kinew held up his status card and said, “It’s time to abolish the Indian Act.” Assembly of First Nations National Chief Cindy Woodhouse Nepinak said that choice must rest with First Nations, because the act also contains some protections (PMO, iPolitics, Canadian Press).

Ottawa also announced $630 million over four years plus $250 million a year after that for First Nations and Inuit policing, $260 million for a workforce fund, $140 million for crime prevention, and a change letting the First Nations Finance Authority lend directly to Indigenous-owned project companies. More details are expected in the Nov. 5 federal budget (iPolitics, Chiefs of Ontario).

Why it matters: The Indian Act still governs status, reserve land and band governance. Any change would be the biggest shift in Ottawa’s relationship with First Nations in decades.

Sources (4)
  • Prime Minister of Canada — Opening remarks at the First Nations–First Ministers meeting
  • iPolitics — Carney pledges to start process that moves Canada beyond the Indian Act
  • Winnipeg Free Press (Canadian Press) — Carney, premiers meet First Nations leaders in Ottawa
  • Chiefs of Ontario — Response to commitments from the Prime Minister
Community Note

Carney promised a process, not a repeal: no bill or timeline was announced. Past attempts to replace the Indian Act, including the 1969 White Paper, failed partly because First Nations weren’t properly consulted. iPolitics uses some Canadian Press reporting, so they count as one source here.

Sources: PMO; iPolitics/Canadian Press; Chiefs of Ontario.

World

U.S. judge rules the Justice Department illegally fired prosecutor Maurene Comey, James Comey’s daughter

Federal Judge Jesse Furman in Manhattan ruled Monday that firing Maurene Comey in July 2025 broke the Civil Service Reform Act, because career prosecutors can only be removed for cause and with notice and a chance to respond. He called one of the department’s arguments one that “borders on frivolous” (court opinion, CNBC, AP).

Furman ordered back pay and reinstatement on paper from her firing until Dec. 20, 2025, when he found she had taken another job. The department admitted the firing “was not ‘for cause’ and was not based on her performance.” Comey had worked on the Jeffrey Epstein, Ghislaine Maxwell and Sean Combs cases. The Justice Department says it will appeal (CNBC, NPR, Washington Post).

Why it matters: The ruling says career government lawyers can’t be fired at will by political leaders. That matters for how independent prosecutors stay in the U.S.

The judge did not decide her claim that she was fired over her father or her politics; he ruled on civil-service and procedure grounds and set that First Amendment claim aside for now. She is not going back to work, since she has another job. An appeal could change the outcome.

Israel votes Tuesday, and the final polls show neither Netanyahu’s bloc nor Eisenkot’s reaching a majority

Israelis vote Tuesday, Oct. 27, with 37 parties on the ballot. The last polls show neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc nor the opposition bloc led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot reaching the 61 seats needed in the 120-seat Knesset. The opposition is polling at about 52 to 56 seats, Netanyahu’s bloc about 50 to 52, and Arab-led parties about 14 to 16 (Jewish Chronicle, Jerusalem Post, JTA).

Between 5 and 10 per cent of voters are still undecided, and parties need 3.25 per cent of the vote to win any seats, so a few small parties could swing the result (JTA, Israel National News).

Why it matters: It’s the first national vote since the Gaza war, and the result will shape Israel’s war policy, its relations with Washington and the region, and Netanyahu’s own future.

Update: Jordan arrests three suspects in the Karak church attack after a three-hour gunfight

What changed since the last edition: Last Friday’s edition covered the deadly attack on a funeral at a church in Karak. Police have now caught three suspects after a gunfight.

Jordanian security forces found two brothers and a relative hiding in an abandoned house in the Al-Jadeeda area. They refused to surrender, and all three were wounded, one critically, in a gunfight of about three hours. No security officers were hurt. Police said there was no sign so far that the men were linked to any other group (Al Jazeera/Reuters, AP).

The attack on a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in Hamoud, near Karak, killed nine people and wounded about 40; 27 were still in hospital. King Abdullah ordered a security review (AP, Al Jazeera).

Why it matters: Attacks on Christians are rare in Jordan, which prides itself on religious coexistence, and the arrests may answer whether this was a lone cell or part of a wider network.

Anthropic files to go public on the Nasdaq as “ANT,” showing $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, filed its S-1 with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission on Monday. It reported first-half revenue of $16.3 billion, up from $1.16 billion a year earlier, and its first positive operating cash flow, $3.9 billion. It still lost $80.7 billion on paper, mostly from a $72.4 billion non-cash charge tied to convertible notes (SEC filing, Fortune, The Verge).

The founders will keep about 50.1 per cent of the voting power through a special share class, and its top five customers make up about a quarter of revenue. The share price and number of shares haven’t been set. Stocks rose broadly on the news, with the Nasdaq up 0.8 per cent (Fortune, AP, Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: It could be one of the biggest stock listings ever, and the filing gives the public its first audited look at how much money AI models actually make and cost.

Japan’s Umios agrees to buy Nova Scotia’s High Liner Foods for $27 a share, and the stock jumps 77 per cent

Umios, the Japanese seafood company formerly called Maruha Nichiro, will pay $27 cash per share for the Lunenburg-founded frozen-fish company, about $804 million for the shares and $1.25 billion including debt. That is a 79 per cent premium over Friday’s price. High Liner shares closed Monday at $26.76 (Globe and Mail, company releases).

The Hennigar family’s holding company, which owns about 41 per cent, supports the deal. It needs 75 per cent shareholder approval in December and should close early next year. Umios says it will keep the Halifax head office, the Lunenburg plant and CEO Paul Jewer (Globe and Mail, Umios).

Why it matters: High Liner has been a Maritime business since 1899. Keeping its head office and plant matters for hundreds of jobs in Nova Scotia, and its fish sticks are in grocery freezers across Canada.

Science corner

MIT builds a paper-thin brain implant that uses ultrasound to push cancer drugs deeper into tissue

The device, called LUCID, is about as thin as cardstock. A tiny 3D-printed tube sits on an array of 24 ultrasound transducers, fed by a small drug reservoir. It’s controlled by Bluetooth and recharged wirelessly. The idea is to place it where a glioblastoma brain tumour was removed and keep dosing that spot to stop the cancer coming back (MIT News, Device).

In tests with gel copies of brain tissue and in mice, the ultrasound pushed a test dye noticeably farther than a pump alone. It hasn’t been tried in people yet (MIT News, Inside Precision Medicine, Medical Design & Development).

Why it matters: Glioblastoma almost always returns after surgery, partly because drugs struggle to get past the brain’s defences. Getting medicine to the right spot, for longer, could help.

Sources (4)
  • MIT News — Implantable device uses ultrasound to improve drug delivery to the brain
  • Device (Cell Press) — Research paper
  • Inside Precision Medicine — Brain implant could prevent glioblastoma relapse
  • Medical Design & Development — Implant uses ultrasound to improve drug delivery to the brain
Community Note

This is early lab work. The mouse tests measured how far a dye spread, not whether tumours were stopped, and human trials would take years. Trade outlets mostly drew on MIT’s own release.

Sources: MIT News; Device; Inside Precision Medicine.

AI news and advances

OpenAI’s new GPT-6.1 Terra halves API prices while scoring higher on coding and computer-use tests

OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra on Monday, a mid-range AI system aimed at everyday office work: reading images and drawings, filling in forms, controlling software and writing code. It costs developers $1 per million input words-pieces (tokens) and $5 per million output, half the price of the version it replaces. It’s in ChatGPT for paid plans, in Codex, through the API and on Amazon Bedrock (OpenAI, AWS, The Decoder).

Independent tester Artificial Analysis called it a new high for intelligence per dollar. It also found it guesses far less often when it doesn’t know an answer: 38 per cent of the time, down from 88 per cent for the previous Terra (Artificial Analysis, The Decoder).

Why it matters: Cheaper, more reliable AI is what let AI tools spread into ordinary apps and workplaces. Price cuts like this usually push rivals to cut too.

Sources (4)
  • OpenAI — Introducing GPT-6.1 Terra
  • AWS — OpenAI GPT-6.1 Terra on Amazon Bedrock
  • The Decoder — GPT-6.1 Terra halves OpenAI’s API prices
  • Artificial Analysis — GPT-6.1 Terra
Community Note

Most benchmark scores, like 50.3 per cent on Terminal-Bench 4.0, come from OpenAI itself. Artificial Analysis’s results are independent but early. OpenAI still recommends its bigger versions for the hardest coding work.

Sources: OpenAI; Artificial Analysis; The Decoder.
