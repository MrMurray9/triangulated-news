Carney confirms four Armed Forces members killed in the CFB Wainwright fire — 39 people in hospital, at least 19 critical — as early reports say a training munition sparked it

Prime Minister Mark Carney confirmed Sunday morning that four Canadian Armed Forces members died in Saturday’s fire at CFB Wainwright and that their next of kin have been notified, calling it “one of the worst tragedies for the Forces on Canadian soil in peacetime” (Prime Minister’s Office). He ordered flags on federal buildings to half-mast and said those affected came from bases across the country, including reservists and soldiers of Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry. Premier Danielle Smith said 39 people were in hospital, at least 19 in critical condition (AP, CTV News).

Military sources told Global News and The Canadian Press that a preliminary investigation suggests a munition from the live-fire exercise sparked a fast-moving grass fire. Internal military reports seen by The Globe and Mail describe a smouldering grass fire that a sudden wind shift drove more than a kilometre into a “wall of flame” that overtook the range’s administration area; one report said the fire-risk index had been low. Military officials told an afternoon briefing that the fire, which started about a kilometre away, reached troops “within 30 seconds” (CTV News). The Forces say there is no sign of terrorism, and the Alberta Energy Regulator said it had no reports of incidents involving energy infrastructure on the base (The Canadian Press, The Globe and Mail).

Alberta ended its Code Orange on Sunday afternoon. The response used 22 ground ambulances, six planes and two helicopters, with help from Saskatchewan, and patients were moved from Wainwright to the University of Alberta and Royal Alexandra hospitals in Edmonton, Foothills in Calgary and Red Deer Regional (The Canadian Press, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: It is one of the deadliest training accidents in the Canadian Forces’ peacetime history, and the wounded are being treated in Edmonton hospitals.

What changed since the last edition: This morning’s edition carried an unconfirmed “at least seven” toll from unnamed sources and no official numbers. New: the prime minister’s confirmed toll of four, the premier’s count of 39 in hospital (19-plus critical), the preliminary cause, the end of Code Orange and the hospitals now treating patients.

Science corner: Google grows “magic states” on its Willow quantum chip, cutting errors 40-fold in one of quantum computing’s biggest bottlenecks

To run useful programs, a quantum computer needs special ingredients called magic states, and making clean ones has been one of the field’s big bottlenecks (Google). Google Quantum AI has now shown on its Willow processor that “magic state cultivation” works in practice: a noisy state is placed in a small patch of qubits, checked again and again, and flawed attempts are thrown away, leaving a very pure state. Errors fell about 40 times compared with uncultivated states, reaching 99.99 per cent fidelity, the team reported in the journal Science (Google, New Scientist).

Google’s earlier theory work suggested the method could let quantum computers run important programs with about one-tenth as many qubits as expected (New Scientist). The team also moved a cultivated state from one error-correcting code to another inside the chip, a step toward a full design (Google).

Why it matters: Cheaper error correction could bring useful quantum computers — for chemistry, materials and medicine — years closer, with far smaller machines.

Sources (4)

  • Google — Bringing the magic: Google breaks open a bottleneck in quantum computing
  • New Scientist — Google showcases way to fix quantum computer errors more efficiently
  • Google Quantum AI — Demonstrating magic state cultivation on Willow (video)
Community Note

Only about 8 per cent of runs produced usable magic states, partly because of a strict testing method, and independent researchers caution that this is an early step (New Scientist). This is a hardware demonstration, not a working quantum application. The video is from Google’s own channel.

Anthropic will set aside 5 per cent of its shares — possibly more as it grows — for a “public benefit fund” that could pay people hurt by AI job losses

Anthropic, the maker of Claude, said Sunday it will create the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, a company-owned entity that would receive 5 per cent of its shares, subject to shareholder approval, plus another 2 per cent for every 50 per cent rise in the company’s value above a baseline it hasn’t yet disclosed, at the board’s discretion (Reuters, Anthropic). Possible uses include donations to nonprofits, direct payments to people, and responses to AI’s impact on jobs (Anthropic, TechCrunch). The fund would be set up as a wholly owned company.

The company, which is preparing to go public, says it will share details on governance and valuation thresholds in the coming months.

Why it matters: If AI eliminates jobs, someone has to help the people affected; this is one of the first concrete pledges by an AI company to share its own wealth to do that.
