World

Riyadh airport attack kills 12 and wounds 309, Saudi Arabia’s deadliest in more than a decade; coalition vows a “decisive” response

Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority said early Sunday that Saturday’s attack on King Khalid International Airport killed four Saudis, two Bangladeshis, and one person each from the U.S., Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Palestine and Sudan. Some of the 309 wounded are in serious or critical condition. It was the third attack on the airport this week, and Reuters reported it came with no warning from the kingdom’s threat-alert system. An eyewitness told AFP the strike hit the domestic terminal. Health officials called in doctors in a rare mass call-up.

The Saudi-led coalition blamed Yemen’s Houthis and called the strike a “full-fledged war crime.” The Houthis hadn’t claimed this attack but warned Sunday that Saudi airports and airspace, other than those serving Mecca and Medina, are “a theatre of operations.” Houthi media reported two Saudi strikes on Sanaa governorate on Sunday. U.S. President Donald Trump said the U.S. “may” join strikes on the Houthis. Prime Minister Mark Carney called the attacks “abhorrent,” and Canada’s embassy told Canadians to stay away from the airport, AFP and Arab News reported.

Why it matters: Canadians in Saudi Arabia have been told to avoid the airport, and the widening Yemen war threatens Red Sea shipping and the oil prices that matter to Alberta’s budget.

What changed since the last edition: Last evening’s edition reported many people wounded and no confirmed deaths. Saudi authorities have since reported 12 dead and 309 hurt, the coalition has vowed to respond, and the Houthis have threatened more attacks on airports.

U.S. jet disables a cargo ship trying to run the Iran blockade as a tanker catches fire in the Strait of Hormuz

U.S. Central Command said a fighter jet struck the stern of the Panama-flagged M/V Ocean Molica, also known as the Arika Sun, with a precision munition in the Gulf of Oman on Saturday. CENTCOM said the strike disabled the ship’s propulsion without harming the crew, after the crew ignored repeated warnings. The ship had left an Iranian port, CENTCOM said. Since resuming the blockade of ships entering or leaving Iranian ports on July 14, U.S. forces say they have disabled four commercial vessels and turned back 135.

Separately, the U.K. Maritime Trade Operations agency said an outbound tanker in the Strait of Hormuz was hit by an unknown projectile late Saturday and caught fire, and that its crew was reported safe. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said it struck a vessel for taking an “illegal route” and claimed a supertanker hit a naval mine. The UAE National Guard said it rescued 22 people from a tanker fire in the Gulf, The National reported. Reuters reported that traffic through the strait has fallen to its lowest level in more than two months.

Why it matters: Before the war, about a fifth of the world’s oil consumption passed through Hormuz, so disruptions there push up the crude prices that drive Alberta’s royalties and Canadians’ fuel costs.

What changed since the last edition: The Oct. 9 morning edition reported Trump saying the naval blockade stays in place. Since then, U.S. forces have fired on a ship to enforce it, the fourth they say they’ve disabled since July.

Drone strike on displaced families in Sudan’s Blue Nile state kills at least 31, UN says; a rights group puts the toll at 41

The strike on Friday hit a gathering of displaced people at Abu Shandi, in the Wad al-Mahi area near the Ethiopian border. Why it matters: Aid groups call Sudan’s war the world’s largest humanitarian crisis, and strikes on displacement sites hit people who have already fled once.

Science corner

NC State researchers use a laser to turn a thin beam of air into a working radio antenna

A team at North Carolina State University fired a laser to ionize a narrow column of air into a plasma filament. They then fed a radio signal into it without touching it, using a metal ring around the beam that acts as a capacitor. The plasma beam transmitted at 30 MHz in the VHF band, which the researchers say is the first demonstration that a plasma-filament antenna works. Changing the laser’s settings changes the filament’s length, so it could be tuned to different frequencies, and steering the laser changes the antenna’s direction.

“The plasma beam antenna looks like a lightsaber and is tunable,” said PhD student Prya Darshni, the corresponding author. The open-access paper, co-authored by Arthur Dogariu of Texas A&M and Princeton, appeared in the IEEE Journal of Microwaves.

Why it matters: Antennas that change length and direction without moving parts could make satellites lighter and easier to retune, the researchers say.

Sources (4)
  • NC State University (via EurekAlert!)
  • everything RF
  • Electronics Online
  • IEEE Journal of Microwaves — paper listing
Community Note

This is a lab proof of concept at one frequency. The team hasn’t yet shown the antenna receiving signals or working across a range of frequencies. Space use is a long-term idea. Co-author Paul Franzon said there’s enough air in low Earth orbit to form a plasma.

Sources: NC State University.

Nvidia is in early talks to buy or invest more in open-model startup Reflection AI

The Financial Times reported Saturday that Nvidia, which has already invested US$800 million in Reflection AI, is discussing either a deeper investment or an acquisition. Options include an “acqui-hire,” in which Nvidia would hire the staff and license the technology. That approach could avoid a long regulatory review, according to Reuters’ account of the FT report. Bloomberg, citing a person familiar with the matter, reported that the talks also cover giving the startup computing power, and that no terms have been agreed.

Reflection was founded in 2024 by former Google DeepMind researchers Misha Laskin and Ioannis Antonoglou. On Monday it released Beam, its first open-weight model, aimed at coding and AI-agent tasks.

Why it matters: Open-weight models, which anyone can download and modify, compete with cheaper Chinese models. A deal would tie one of the main U.S. developers of such models more closely to the world’s most valuable chipmaker.

Community Note
