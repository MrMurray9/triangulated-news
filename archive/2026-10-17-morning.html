“This is not a game. This is not poker”: Carney closes his Alberta tour before 5,000-plus in Edmonton as more than a million ballots are already cast

Prime Minister Mark Carney wrapped up two days of campaigning against separation with a Forever Canadian rally at the Edmonton EXPO Centre on Friday night, telling a hometown crowd the Edmonton Journal put at more than 5,000: “Don’t let anyone tell you this is already decided. Turnout will determine the outcome.” He called a vote to leave “a dangerous bluff that will lead to years of deep uncertainty and lost opportunities,” warned that “some outside our borders are working to break us so they can own us,” and acknowledged old grievances: “Growing up, I remember how Ottawa made Albertans feel like our resources weren’t our own” (AP, Edmonton Journal).

Mayor Andrew Knack urged the crowd to also reject the nine immigration and constitutional questions, drawing chants of “no to nine”; Oilers great Kevin Lowe and Forever Canadian leader Thomas Lukaszuk also spoke, and former premier Rachel Notley sent a video message (Edmonton Journal). Outside, independence supporters waved flags and held a sign reading “Say no to Carney’s new world order” (AP). Elections Alberta says more than one million Albertans had voted by Friday night — Elections Alberta tallies cited by CityNews show 529,504 in-person advance ballots over the first three days plus about 491,000 special ballots processed. Today (Saturday) is the last day of advance voting; on Monday, polls are open 9 a.m. to 8 p.m.

A final Abacus Data survey released Saturday found 68% would vote to remain a province of Canada, up from 64% in September, while 24% would vote to begin the legal process toward a binding separation vote and 7% were undecided.

Why it matters: With turnout the wild card both campaigns keep pointing to, the final weekend is about getting voters out rather than changing minds — and the nine policy questions remain far less settled than the separation question.

What changed since the last edition: Last evening’s edition covered the joint Smith–Carney appearance in Red Deer. New: Carney’s Edmonton rally speech, the million-ballot mark and Abacus’s final pre-vote poll.

Flu season arrives early in Alberta, so pharmacies can give flu shots now instead of waiting for Oct. 19

Alberta has told pharmacists they don’t have to wait for the planned Oct. 19 start of this year’s publicly funded flu campaign and can begin giving flu vaccines right away, because the season has started earlier and harder than officials expected (CTV News, CityNews). A Calgary Pharmasave owner told CityNews more than a dozen people came in Thursday morning after he got the notice by email.

As of Oct. 3, the province’s respiratory virus dashboard showed at least 411 flu cases this season, nearly 50 hospitalizations and one death, CityNews reported. Flu vaccine coverage in Alberta has fallen from 37 per cent in 2020–21 to 20 per cent in 2025–26. Alberta.ca says people aged 5 and older can be immunized at participating pharmacies, while children under 5 need a public health appointment; call ahead to check stock.

Why it matters: An early season means more people could get sick before the usual campaign would have begun — and with only about one in five Albertans vaccinated last year, officials want shots given sooner.

The vaccine itself hasn’t changed; only the start date for flu shots moved earlier. Dashboard counts reflect lab-confirmed cases, so actual infections are higher.

Health ministers leave Winnipeg with one-year “bridge” money but no long-term deal — they’ll meet again by December

The two-day meeting of federal, provincial and territorial health ministers in Winnipeg ended Friday without a long-term funding commitment from Ottawa. Federal Health Minister Marjorie Michel stood by a one-year extension of expiring mental-health, substance-use and home-care agreements, worth about $1.2 billion, but went no further. “The work is starting today. It’s not like a big announcement – it’s a bridging,” she said, adding that the federal budget will come this fall on a date not yet announced (Globe and Mail).

Manitoba Health Minister Uzoma Asagwara, the host, said provinces and territories want the Canada Health Transfer to grow at least 5 per cent a year, rather than the 3 per cent expected in 2028, and that ministers will meet again by December (Globe and Mail). Ontario’s Sylvia Jones said Thursday that a one-year extension of an expiring 10-year agreement “does not fill us with a lot of joy.” The Winnipeg Free Press also reported the meeting ended without a long-term commitment.

Why it matters: These agreements help pay for home care and mental-health services that provinces run; without longer-term money, ministers say they can’t plan staffing beyond next year.

What changed since the last edition: Yesterday morning’s edition covered the meeting’s start. New: it ended with only bridge funding and a promise to meet again by December.

Trump waives the new Russia sanctions Congress passed last month — the 500% tariffs and penalties on big buyers of Russian oil are on hold

U.S. President Donald Trump signed an executive order late Friday waiving key parts of the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, certifying to Congress that the move “is in the national interests of the United States” (White House, CNBC). The law, which he signed in September, required duties of up to 500 per cent on Russian goods and tariffs of up to 100 per cent on countries such as China and India that are among the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas; the deadline was two days away. Sanctions under earlier executive orders stay in place (CNBC).

Kirill Dmitriev, Vladimir Putin’s economic envoy, called it “an important and truly historic moment.” Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said lifting sanctions was “wrong” and “only helps the Russians,” calling it “effectively serving as a financial reward for Putin” (NBC News). Criticism came from Republicans too: retiring Sen. Thom Tillis called it “beyond irresponsible,” noting the bill passed the Senate 86–11. The waiver follows a temporary easing of sanctions on Russian diesel exports through April 2027; diesel prices are up almost 70 per cent since the Iran war began in February (NBC News, CNBC).

Why it matters: Penalizing Russia’s oil customers was Congress’s main new lever on Moscow’s war revenue; pausing it eases pressure on the Kremlin less than three weeks before the U.S. midterm elections, with fuel prices a top voter issue.

What changed since the last edition: Last evening’s edition covered Zelenskyy’s €70-billion request to the EU and Starlink. New: the U.S. sanctions waiver and Kyiv’s reaction.

Why it matters: Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu’s 2027 budget leans on spending restraint, including a public-sector pay freeze, while protesters want more money for schools and services — a collision heading into the 2027 presidential race.

Istanbul’s worst floods since 1942 kill four; searches continue for the missing in Sarıyer

Torrential rain on Wednesday night swamped Istanbul, a city of 16 million, which got more rain in 48 hours than the historical average for September and October combined, the state-run Anadolu Agency reported (AP). “We are trying our best to help our citizens, but we are also experiencing the biggest flood disaster since 1942,” Interior Minister Mustafa Çiftçi said Friday in the hard-hit Ayazağa neighbourhood of Sarıyer district.

The death toll rose to four on Friday when the body of a 66-year-old man was found beneath a bridge in Sarıyer; earlier deaths included a teenager who was electrocuted in the storm (AP, Türkiye Today). Çiftçi said there had been 9,850 reports of damage and other problems and that 46,000 personnel were clearing debris and helping residents. Environment Minister Murat Kurum, who will chair the COP31 climate summit in Antalya next month, said dealing with development close to waterways was “essential.”

Why it matters: The disaster puts a spotlight on building along flood channels in one of the world’s biggest cities, weeks before Türkiye hosts the UN climate summit.

Air Canada and United want to coordinate fares and schedules on 10 more cross-border routes; Ottawa opens a public review

Transport Canada says Air Canada and United Airlines have notified the transport minister that they want to update the joint venture they have run since 2022, which lets them coordinate flight schedules, ticket prices, marketing, sales and revenue management on certain Canada–U.S. routes. Officials will run a public interest assessment, including the effect on competition, and Canadians can comment at letstalktransportation.ca until Nov. 15.

The airlines want to add routes now excluded from the deal: Montréal–Chicago, Montréal–Washington, Ottawa–New York, Ottawa–Washington, Toronto–Cleveland, Toronto–Denver, Toronto–Houston, Toronto–San Francisco, Toronto–Washington and Vancouver–San Francisco (Travelweek; Bloomberg). Bloomberg noted the request comes as travel between the two countries has been hammered by geopolitical tension. Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon said the review aims to ensure “any expanded cooperation between these carriers benefits Canadians.”

Why it matters: On routes where the two big carriers would coordinate instead of compete, fares and choice could change — which is why the review weighs competition against connectivity.

Science corner: AI helps map 9,389 reactions on a copper catalyst, showing how CO₂ really turns into methanol

Turning captured carbon dioxide into methanol, a fuel and chemical building block, means reacting CO₂ with hydrogen on a catalyst. But thousands of tiny steps happen on the catalyst’s surface, far too many to simulate one by one with quantum mechanics, so models usually keep only a short list. Researchers at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) built a quantum-simulation database of 152 reactions, trained machine-learning models to estimate the energy barriers of others, and used automated tools to enumerate the possible steps among 105 surface species, growing the network to 9,389 reactions.

It mattered: the small network wrongly predicted formic acid as the main product and underestimated how much CO₂ gets converted, while the large one pointed to methanol, in line with experiments. It also revealed an unexpected way molecular hydrogen takes part directly. The study is published in Nature Communications.

Why it matters: More complete reaction maps could help chemists design catalysts that recycle CO₂ into fuels more efficiently — and the same method could apply to other industrial reactions.

Sources (3)
  • ScienceDaily (IISc release) — AI reveals a hidden chemical shortcut that could turn CO2 into fuel
  • Nature Communications — Data-driven massive reaction networks reveal mechanistic pathways underlying catalytic CO2 hydrogenation
  • DeepTech India — IISc maps 9,389 copper-catalyst reactions to explain how CO₂ becomes methanol
Community Note

This is a computational study checked against existing experimental results, not a new plant or a new catalyst. Independent coverage is still limited (the ScienceDaily story is the university’s release), so treat it as a promising modelling method rather than a proven industrial process.

Sources: IISc via ScienceDaily; Nature Communications; DeepTech India.

AI news and advances

A 25-year cryptography mystery solved: Anthropic’s Claude traced the NIST elliptic-curve seeds to a 1997 random-number generator

The seeds behind the NIST elliptic curves P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521, published in 2000 and used to secure much of the internet, were never explained, which fed suspicion that the NSA might have picked them to hide a weakness. Anthropic says an internal research model traced them in under half an hour to drand48/lrand48, an ordinary C random-number generator seeded with Unix timestamps from 1997 and 1998 (Anthropic, CoinDesk). The widely used P-256 seed is the 1,842nd candidate from a run seeded on Aug. 16, 1997.

Cryptographer Filippo Valsorda, who organized a bounty for the answer, wrote that he reimplemented the method in Go to verify it, and that the result leaves “barely 30 bits of freedom … not enough to embed any kind of NOBUS backdoor.” The Anthropic team donated the prize to ISRG, the nonprofit behind Let’s Encrypt, tripling it to US$36,864. In its recorded reasoning, the model wrote: “Wow, it’s a real discovery” (CoinDesk).

Why it matters: It’s a concrete case of an AI system answering a question experts chased for decades — and it strengthens confidence in curves that protect everyday web traffic.

Sources (4)
  • Anthropic — Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins
  • Filippo Valsorda — The NIST elliptic curves seeds bounty has been claimed
  • CoinDesk — ‘Wow, it’s a real discovery’: Claude cracks a 25-year NSA number mystery
  • Crypto Briefing — Claude traces NIST P-256 seed to a 1997 random number generator run
Community Note

Users of the NIST curves don’t need to change anything (Anthropic, via CoinDesk). Some questions remain open, including why roughly half of the valid candidate seeds were skipped. Bitcoin uses a different curve (secp256k1) that isn’t affected. The claim comes from Anthropic, but it was independently re-run and confirmed by Valsorda.

Sources: Anthropic; Filippo Valsorda; CoinDesk; Crypto Briefing.

“Sus” — short for suspicious or suspect

Sus (sometimes spelled suss) is slang for suspicious or suspect — something or someone that seems a little off. Example: “A text from ‘the bank’ asking for your password?
