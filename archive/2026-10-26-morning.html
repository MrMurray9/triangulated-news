Alberta

A U.S. developer wants to build an AI data centre campus five times the size of West Edmonton Mall in northeast Edmonton

Walton Global Investments, based in Arizona, has asked the city to change the plan for the Edmonton Energy and Technology Park so it can build a “hyperscale” AI data centre campus in the park’s medium industrial area. The request would also move about 250 hectares out of the area set aside for petrochemical plants. The city’s online survey on the idea closed Sunday, and city council will hold a public hearing before it decides (City of Edmonton, Taproot Edmonton).

Some people who live nearby are worried. “We don’t know how much water it’s going to use or how much power,” resident Blake Luce told CityNews. Coun. Ashley Salvador has brought a motion asking the city to study large data centres and possibly limit them. She warns that household power bills could rise by hundreds of dollars a year if the grid has to expand for them. University of Alberta engineering professor Yize Chen said one campus this size can use as much electricity as a small city (CityNews Edmonton).

Premier Danielle Smith said on Saturday that the province will release more rules this fall on how data centres connect to Alberta’s power grid (CityNews Edmonton).

Why it matters: Alberta is trying to attract data centre investment, but it is the people who live nearby, and possibly every power customer, who would live with the effects. This is one of the first big tests of that inside Edmonton city limits.

Sources (4)

  • City of Edmonton — Data centre development
  • CityNews Edmonton — Residents concerned about large AI data centre near community
  • Taproot Edmonton — Data centre campus proposed in northeast Edmonton

This is a request to rezoning land, not an approved project. No construction date, power supply or final size has been confirmed, and council can still say no. The power-bill estimate is Coun. Thieves broke into 30 Telus phone boxes in three weeks as copper-wire theft knocks out internet and street lights across Edmonton

Telus says thieves broke into 30 of its roadside equipment boxes in Edmonton over three weeks, cutting out the copper inside and leaving homes without phone and internet. Kaskitayo, Mill Woods, Twin Brooks and Castle Downs were among the hardest hit, along with the South-East Industrial area. “Alberta is the epicentre of this,” Telus spokesperson Anne Martin said. The company says wire theft has risen by more than 800 per cent across Canada in five years (Edmonton Journal, CTV News Edmonton).

City street lights are another target. The city is adding locks to light poles and switching some lines to aluminum wire, which is worth much less to thieves, city spokesperson Chelsea Keelan said. Lights in Chinatown went dark after wire was stolen, and Todd Janes of the Stony Plain Road business association says the outages make streets feel less safe. Coun. Reed Clarke says the city is looking at possible bylaw changes. Telus is calling for an end to cash payments at scrap yards (Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: An outage can cut off 911 calls on landlines and leave dark streets, and repair costs end up on phone bills and city taxes. Ideas like banning cash at scrap yards are aimed at removing the payday that drives the theft.

Sources (3)
  • Edmonton Journal — Copper wire thefts leave Edmonton lights dark
  • Edmonton Journal — Chinatown lights out after copper wire theft
  • CTV News Edmonton — Attempted copper theft leaves some Edmonton neighbourhoods with service interruptions
Community Note

Most of this reporting comes from two Postmedia stories plus CTV. The 800 per cent figure is Telus’s own national number, and police have not released a matching Edmonton total. Two Journal stories are not fully independent of each other.

Sources: Edmonton Journal; CTV News Edmonton.

Canada

Carney hosts EU leaders in Montreal this week; a draft text would set out what Canada’s ‘associate membership’ could mean by 2027

Prime Minister Mark Carney will host European Council President António Costa and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen at a Canada–EU summit in Montreal on Thursday and Friday, Oct. 29–30 (Prime Minister’s Office, Council of the EU).

A draft joint statement seen by Bloomberg says the “full scope of this associate membership will be defined by the 2027 Canada-EU Summit.” Options include joining EU programs such as the Erasmus student exchange or the Galileo satellite navigation system, and a seat on a proposed European security council (Financial Post/Bloomberg, AFP).

Reaction has been sharp. U.S. President Donald Trump called the idea “laughable” and “a hostile act.” Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre said Canada should “never be… the 28th state of the European Union.” The 2017 Canada–EU trade deal (CETA) is still not fully ratified, with 10 EU countries yet to approve it (AFP, Financial Post).

Why it matters: It’s the biggest move yet to tie Canada more closely to Europe while trade with the U.S. is strained. Students, defence companies and exporters could all feel the effects.

The RCAF receives its first two F-35 fighters in Arizona, even as Ottawa still reviews the full 88-jet order

The Royal Canadian Air Force formally accepted its first two CF-35A stealth fighters on Friday at Luke Air Force Base in Arizona. Canadian pilots and ground crews will train there, and eight Canadian jets will be based at Luke before the first ones arrive in Canada in 2028 (Global News/Canadian Press, Defence Industry Europe).

Ottawa has committed to buying 16 jets. The Carney government is still reviewing whether to buy all 88, and a mixed fleet that includes Sweden’s Gripen is still possible. The air force is aiming for the jets to be combat-ready by the end of 2030 (Globe and Mail, Defence Industry Europe).

Why it matters: The jets will replace CF-18s that are about 40 years old, at a cost of tens of billions of dollars. Cold Lake, Alberta, is one of the two planned home bases, so the decision has a direct Alberta angle.

Serbia’s student protest movement wins a parliamentary majority, and Vučić concedes: “It is clear we have lost”

The Student List, which grew out of a year of student-led protests, won 52.65 per cent of the vote in Serbia’s parliamentary election, according to the state electoral commission’s preliminary results. President Aleksandar Vučić’s “United Serbia” got 39.22 per cent. Turnout was 68.78 per cent, the highest in more than 30 years (Serbian government/RIK, AP).

Projections give the Student List about 140 of the 250 seats, comfortably more than the 126 needed to govern alone. “It is clear we have lost these elections,” Vučić said. The movement started after a station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad on Nov. 1, 2024, killing 16 people, and its campaign was led by Novi Sad cardiology professor Ilija Srdanović. Parliament must meet within 30 days of the final results to start forming a government (Al Jazeera, AP).

Lula wins a fourth term with 51.8 per cent; Flávio Bolsonaro refuses to concede and says he will ask courts to annul the vote

What changed since the last edition: The count is finished. With 100 per cent of polling sections counted, Lula has won, and Flávio Bolsonaro has refused to accept the result.

Brazil’s electoral court finished counting all 499,248 polling sections late Sunday, at 8:26 p.m. Edmonton time. President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva received 64,256,232 votes (51.79 per cent) and Sen. Flávio Bolsonaro 59,826,041 (48.21 per cent), a margin of about 4.4 million. Turnout was 80.78 per cent. Lula had trailed his rival in the first round (TSE via Brasil em Voto, The Guardian).

Bolsonaro, eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, told reporters in Brasília: “This result cannot be accepted. We are going to ask for the annulment of the elections.” He objects to a Supreme Court judge’s order keeping polls open two extra hours, though his own Liberal Party had joined that request. Members of the electoral court told Brazil’s G1 they expect the challenge to fail (El País, G1).

Science corner

A gel with no living cells moves on its own because DNA tells it which molecular motors to build

Scientists at Israel’s Weizmann Institute and the University of Minnesota have made a material that moves on its own, without any living cells. A thin sheet of microtubules, the protein fibres that act as tracks inside cells, sits in a well 3.3 mm wide along with “cell-free” protein-making machinery. When the researchers add DNA, the mix builds kinesin motor proteins, which pull on the fibres so the sheet buckles, blisters and flows (Phys.org, Nature Materials).

Different DNA does different jobs. Adding the gene for a protein called PRC1 acts as a brake, and a genetic feedback loop makes the motion pulse on and off. Depending on the dose, movement starts after about one to four hours and keeps going for more than 10. Rochelle Silverman and Alexandra Tayar led the work (Weizmann Institute, Nature Materials).

Why it matters: It points toward “programmable matter”: soft materials that could one day change shape, pump fluid or heal themselves according to genetic instructions, without needing living cells.

Sources (3)
  • Nature Materials — Gene-driven self-morphing microtubule-based active matter
  • Phys.org — DNA programs cell-free material to build its own motors
  • Weizmann Institute research portal — publication record
Community Note

This is a lab demonstration in tiny wells, not a product. Most coverage comes from the paper and institutional write-ups rather than independent experts. Proteins stop being made after about six hours, so the material can’t keep moving indefinitely yet.

Sources: Nature Materials; Phys.org; Weizmann Institute.

AI news and advances

UW and NVIDIA release a free AI protein designer they say succeeds 5 to 10 times as often, and Manchester used it to make a drug-making enzyme

The University of Washington’s Institute for Protein Design and NVIDIA have released RFD4-Proteina, an open-source AI model for designing new proteins, free under a BSD-3 licence. It combines RoseTTAFold Diffusion with NVIDIA’s Proteina-Complexa and adds a search step that ranks designs while generating them. The developers say it gets 5 to 10 times more working designs, 5 to 10 times faster (UW Medicine, NVIDIA Technical Blog).

Nobel laureate David Baker’s lab says graduate student Seth Woodbury used it to design a pesticide-breaking enzyme in days. At the University of Manchester, researchers designed enzymes for a step in making the HIV drug lenacapavir. Four of 67 designs worked, and the best converted 79 per cent of the starting chemical (University of Manchester, Phys.org).

Why it matters: Custom enzymes could make medicines cheaper and cleaner to manufacture. Because the model is open, smaller labs and universities can use it without paying licence fees.

Sources (4)
  • UW Medicine — Researchers achieve major advance in protein design
  • NVIDIA Technical Blog — Design proteins with RFD4-Proteina using reward-guided inference-time search
  • University of Manchester — AI-designed enzyme for drug synthesis
  • Phys.org — Scientists use AI to design enzyme for drug synthesis
Community Note

The 5–10× figures are the developers’ own benchmarks and haven’t been independently tested yet. The Manchester enzyme was a lab-scale result for one reaction step, not a factory process. Most coverage comes from the institutions involved.

Sources: UW Medicine; NVIDIA; University of Manchester.

OA 2.92-metre catfish: a German angler’s Instagram photos of a giant fish caught and released in Poland go viral

Seen on: Instagram — angler Oliver Schäfer (@olli_s_fishing)

Oliver Schäfer, 34, from Germany’s Eifel region, and his Dutch fishing partner Kevin Cremers landed a 2.92-metre wels catfish at a predator-fishing tournament on the Rybnik Reservoir in southern Poland on Oct. 10–11. It took more than 30 minutes to bring in. They measured it and let it go, and finished second in the competition (Euronews, Wochenspiegel).

Local Polish media think it is the same giant caught there in 2025, which also measured 292 cm. The reservoir is warmed by a nearby coal power plant, which helps catfish grow unusually large (rybnik.com.pl, Euronews).

Why it matters: It’s a jaw-dropping but true fish story, and the fish was released alive.
