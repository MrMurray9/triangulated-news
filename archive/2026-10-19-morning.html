The four soldiers killed at Wainwright are named — and the general says the grass fire was known for hours before wind drove it a kilometre in under a minute; Canadians line up to give blood

The Canadian Armed Forces has named the four soldiers who died in Saturday’s fire at CFB Wainwright: Sgt. Morgan Wong of Edmonton, a Loyal Edmonton Regiment reservist serving with 3rd Battalion, Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry, and Ptes. Shaun Farrell of Prince Albert, Sask., Aidan Goulding of North Vancouver and Hayden Lykkemark of Langley, B.C., all of 3 PPCLI at CFB Edmonton (National Defence, Global News). Thirty-nine members were hurt, 19 critically; Premier Danielle Smith says many have serious burns and will need “weeks and months” of specialized care (Reuters, The Canadian Press).

Maj.-Gen. Wade Rutland, commander of 1st Canadian Division, said 84 soldiers were on a grenade range and a simulated-attack range, training to become a rapid global-response force and to reinforce Canada’s mission in Latvia next spring. Officials had known about a grass fire roughly a kilometre away for hours and judged it no threat, so training carried on; winds that had been under 50 km/h then gusted to about 96 km/h and pushed the fire across that kilometre in 30 to 60 seconds (AP, Reuters). He said the cause hasn’t been determined, ruled out oil and gas infrastructure and “outside interference,” and said the investigation could take at least a month.

Defence Minister David McGuinty says Canadians are lining up to give blood, bringing meals to first responders and opening their homes to grieving families. Canadian Blood Services says donations in the coming days and weeks will help keep supplies strong (The Canadian Press, Canadian Blood Services). Prime Minister Mark Carney is scheduled to make a statement in the House of Commons at 11 a.m. Eastern — 9 a.m. in Edmonton (Prime Minister’s Office).

Why it matters: All four soldiers served out of CFB Edmonton, dozens of wounded remain in hospital, and the key question now is why training continued with a known fire nearby.

What changed since the last edition: The last edition had the official toll of four, 39 in hospital and a preliminary cause from unnamed sources. New: the soldiers’ names and hometowns, the general’s account that the fire was known for hours while training continued, the wind and speed figures, the timeline for the investigation, the rush of blood donors and today’s Commons statement.

Inflation edges up to 3.1 per cent as gas prices climb — a touch below the 3.2 per cent economists expected, nine days before the Bank of Canada decides

Canada’s consumer price index rose 3.1 per cent in September from a year earlier, up from 3.0 per cent in August, Statistics Canada said Monday; economists polled by Reuters had expected 3.2 per cent (Statistics Canada, The Canadian Press, Reuters, Bloomberg). Gasoline drove the increase, with prices up 27.8 per cent from a year earlier, compared with 22.8 per cent in August, as crude oil hit its highest level since May amid the wars in the Middle East. Excluding gasoline, inflation eased to 2.2 per cent from 2.4 per cent (Statistics Canada).

Grocery prices rose 1.7 per cent, the smallest yearly increase since May 2024, although they are still 33 per cent higher than in September 2020. Shelter costs rose 1.8 per cent and rent 2.8 per cent, and prices rose 0.4 per cent from August after seasonal adjustment (Statistics Canada). The Bank of Canada’s preferred core measures eased: CPI-median slowed to 1.9 per cent and CPI-trim was 1.8 per cent (Reuters, Bloomberg). This is the bank’s last inflation reading before its Oct. 28 rate decision. It has said it will look past the first effects of higher energy prices but is ready to raise rates if inflation spreads (The Canadian Press). Money markets expect a hold at 2.25 per cent next week and are pricing in a quarter-point hike in December (Reuters).

Why it matters: Gas is pushing up costs while grocery inflation slows, and the reading feeds directly into the Oct. 28 Bank of Canada decision on rates for mortgages and loans.

What changed since the last edition: Last edition previewed today’s release and the forecast of 3.2 per cent. New: the actual result, 3.1 per cent, and what drove it.

Science corner

Science corner: Binghamton engineers print a paper “wallpaper” that makes electricity from the humidity in your room — enough to run a wireless keyboard

Moist-electric generators turn water vapour in the air into a small electric current. A team led by Prof. Seokheun “Sean” Choi at Binghamton University has designed one as wallpaper for indoor rooms (Binghamton University, New Atlas). The work is published in the journal Advanced Energy Materials.

The wallpaper is made of 2-by-2-centimetre paper tiles. Each has a glycerol edge that draws in moisture, a PVP polymer middle and a wax-treated centre, so water moves one way across the tile and produces a current, with the wiring on the back (New Atlas, Binghamton University). One tile made about 0.34 volts at 80 per cent humidity and 10 tiles about 2.9 volts; a 35-tile array ran a humidity sensor for about 15 minutes, and 1,596 tiles made 3.5 volts at 38 per cent humidity and, with a capacitor, ran a wireless keyboard. The tiles also absorb moisture: 28 of them took a test chamber from 75 to 50 per cent humidity in about four minutes (New Atlas).

Why it matters: Walls that quietly power sensors and smart-home gadgets while helping control humidity could cut battery waste and some heating and cooling energy.

Sources (4)
  • Advanced Energy Materials — Moist-electric wallpaper with engineered unidirectional moisture transport
  • Binghamton University — Researchers develop wallpaper that generates power
  • New Atlas — Smart wallpaper turns your wall into a small indoor powerplant
  • Tech Explorist — Scientists create wallpaper that generates electricity from air

This is a lab proof of concept, not a product. The power is tiny, enough for sensors and small devices, not lights or appliances, and the study gives no total power figure for the wall-sized version. Long-term durability hasn’t been shown.

OA world first at Amsterdam’s Artis zoo: an injured hornbill gets 3D-printed upper and lower beaks — and picks up a mouse the next morning

Seen on: Instagram — Artis zoo’s video, per Vice; also covered by UPI, NL Times and NU.nl

A southern ground hornbill at Artis in Amsterdam had broken both parts of its beak and was getting by on the stumps. Joëll Magré of UMC Utrecht designed two 3D-printed prostheses, Amnovis made the titanium parts, and Utrecht veterinary surgeons Bjorn Meij and Lucinda van Stee attached them (NL Times, Artis).

He used his new beak the same day, picked up a mouse on his own the next morning and was preening within days. The zoo calls it a world first for a bird to get both upper and lower beak prostheses (NL Times, UPI).

Why it matters: Clever engineering gave a bird its life back — and could open new options in veterinary medicine.

Sources (5)

  • UPI — Injured hornbill gets prosthetic beak at Netherlands zoo

Community Note

“World first” is the zoo’s claim, not an independent ruling. How well the prostheses hold up over the long term isn’t known yet. The video is from Dutch broadcaster RTL Nieuws and is in Dutch.
