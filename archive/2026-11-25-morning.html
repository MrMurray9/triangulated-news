Alberta

Alberta drops its order for a second flagpole at every school after boards warn it would cost millions

Education Minister Demetrios Nicolaides announced Tuesday that schools won’t have to install extra outdoor poles to fly both the Canadian and Alberta flags. A school with one pole will fly the Canadian flag outside and display the Alberta flag “prominently” indoors. Schools with two poles fly both outside, and schools with none hang both inside (Edmonton Journal, CityNews).

The province announced the flag rules on Oct. 23. The Calgary Board of Education estimated it would need 223 more poles at a cost of up to $4 million, and Edmonton Public Schools counted at least 170 schools that would need one (CityNews). New schools will still be built with two outdoor poles, and the amended rules take effect in January (Edmonton Journal).

“There’s nothing wrong with listening to partners and making changes,” Nicolaides said. NDP deputy leader Rakhi Pancholi said it will be “the third time, actually, that the flag regulations will be changed” (Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: Edmonton Public alone had 170 schools facing a bill it hadn’t yet tallied. That money now stays in classrooms, and every school still has to show both flags.

The rest of the policy hasn’t changed. Schools still need provincial approval to fly any other flag, and the minister has said exemptions won’t be granted for social or political causes (CityNews).

The Calgary board’s cost figure is its own estimate, and reports differ slightly on the count of poles. Edmonton Public hadn’t put a dollar figure on its share.

Deaths in Alberta jails and police custody would no longer automatically get a public inquiry under a new justice bill

Bill 44, tabled Monday by Justice Minister Mickey Amery, would end the rule that a judge-led fatality inquiry must be held whenever someone dies in prison or police custody. Inquiries would go ahead only where they “would best serve the public interest,” using criteria set later in regulation (The Globe and Mail, The Canadian Press).

Amery says Alberta starts about twice as many fatality inquiries as it finishes each year, some take as long as 14 years, and 180 are waiting to be heard. “We can’t allow families to be waiting years, sometimes a decade or more, to get an answer,” he said. The bill gives the chief medical examiner more access to personal and health records and requires that office be told of deaths at involuntary addiction-treatment centres (The Canadian Press, KaCheng).

NDP justice critic Irfan Sabir said bringing the medical examiner’s office under the minister “points to more control over these processes which were arm’s length” (The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: Inquiries are often the only public look at why someone died in state custody. Fewer of them could clear the backlog faster, but it also means fewer public recommendations.

Sources (4)

  • The Canadian Press via Global News: Alberta moves to reduce mandatory inquiries of deaths in custody
  • KaCheng: Alberta proposes ending mandatory public inquiries for deaths in police custody or prison
  • Government of Alberta: Protecting personal information and public safety
Community Note

This is the same omnibus bill that would limit which parties can get the voter list, covered in Monday evening’s edition. The inquiry change is a separate part of it and hadn’t been reported here.

The bill was tabled the same day Calgary police officers were charged with manslaughter in Jon Wells’ 2024 death. The Globe notes no fatality inquiry has been called in that case. Bill 44 hasn’t passed yet.

Canada

Macklem admits the Bank of Canada was “too slow” on inflation and brings back an old yardstick, CPIX, to track prices

Speaking to the Economic Club of Canada in Toronto on Tuesday, Governor Tiff Macklem said the Bank is keeping its 2% inflation target through 2031, but changing how it reads price trends. CPIX, which leaves out eight volatile items like gasoline, mortgage interest and fruits and vegetables, returns as a key core measure alongside CPI-trim. CPI-median moves to a new public inflation dashboard (Bank of Canada, BNN Bloomberg/The Canadian Press, Canadian Mortgage Trends).

Macklem said policymakers were “too slow to recognize the persistence of inflationary pressures” after the pandemic, and that “we heard a lot of anger” in consultations. He admitted the Bank’s talk about core measures confused people: “Canadians found it confusing when we discussed measures that strip out volatile prices such as food and gasoline” (BNN Bloomberg/The Canadian Press).

“Total CPI inflation is our target, and it will be front and centre in all our communications,” he said. The Bank says it may act sooner or harder when a supply shock is large, lasting or widespread (The Globe and Mail, Bank of Canada).

Why it matters: The Bank’s reading of inflation sets the rates on mortgages and loans. Changing its yardsticks changes how fast it may react the next time prices jump.

The Canadian Press notes the 2026 agreement drops the 2021 wording about supporting “maximum sustainable employment,” as well as an earlier reference to climate change. This was not a rate decision, and the next one is scheduled for December.

Squamish declares a local emergency and builds sandbag walls as king tides and storms threaten B.C.’s coast

Environment Canada says a run of king tides, the highest tides of the year, is lining up with storm surge and big waves. That could push water 20 to 50 cm above the highest normal tides, rising each day and peaking Thursday. Coastal flood warnings or statements cover Metro Vancouver, Vancouver Island, Haida Gwaii and the central, Sunshine and north coasts (The Globe and Mail/The Canadian Press, EmergencyInfoBC).

The District of Squamish declared a state of local emergency Tuesday. Crews are building sandbag walls and berms along Loggers Lane, with a sandbag station near Copper Coil, after Howe Sound flooded downtown in December 2022 (Squamish Chief). The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation near Tofino has an evacuation alert for some shoreline properties (EmergencyInfoBC).

Up to 225 mm of rain is forecast for parts of Vancouver Island through Thursday, with gusts up to 90 km/h in Greater Victoria. The province warns of sudden road closures on coastal highways (The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: An ECCC scientist told the Squamish Chief that El Niño is adding roughly 30–40 cm to sea levels right now. That leaves less room for error than in 2022, when a storm surge hit during a king tide and set a record.

China flies its first foreign astronaut to space as Pakistan’s Muhammad Zeeshan Ali lifts off for the Tiangong station

A Long March 2F rocket launched Shenzhou-24 from Jiuquan at 8:18 a.m. Beijing time Wednesday (6:18 p.m. Tuesday in Edmonton). On board were commander Tang Hongbo, flight engineer Wu Yi and payload specialist Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, a former Pakistani military pilot and the first person from Pakistan to reach orbit (AP, Reuters, Space.com).

It is the first time China has launched a foreign national. Ali is due to stay only about a week, running joint China-Pakistan experiments, before returning with two members of the Shenzhou-23 crew. Tang becomes the first person to visit Tiangong three times (Space.com, Xinhua, AFP).

The third Shenzhou-23 astronaut, Zhu Yangzhu, will stay behind and is set to become the first Chinese astronaut to spend a year in orbit, practice for future long missions to the moon (Space.com, citing the South China Morning Post).

Why it matters: Tiangong is now open to astronauts from partner countries. China is building space ties outside the U.S.-led International Space Station partnership.

Solomon Islands signs a treaty making Australia its main security partner, and Chinese police are expected to leave by mid-2027

Prime Ministers Anthony Albanese and Matthew Wale signed the Australia–Solomon Islands Treaty in Canberra on Wednesday, days after Honiara said it would end its 2022 security pact with Beijing when it expires in April (The Guardian, AP, Reuters).

Solomon Islands agrees not to allow foreign military bases or major security infrastructure. About 14 Chinese police officers are expected to leave by the middle of 2027. Why it matters: The 2022 China pact alarmed Australia and the U.S., who feared a Chinese base in the South Pacific. This treaty closes that door, at least for now.

A U.S. jury convicts Huawei of stealing trade secrets and lying to banks about Iran, the case that began with Meng Wanzhou’s 2018 arrest in Vancouver

A federal jury in Brooklyn on Tuesday found Huawei and three subsidiaries guilty on 10 of 12 counts. They include conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank and wire fraud, money laundering conspiracy and violating U.S. sanctions on Iran. Huawei was acquitted of the most serious charge, racketeering conspiracy, and of conspiracy to obstruct justice (The Guardian, NBC News, U.S. Justice Department).

Prosecutors said Huawei copied Cisco router source code, took confidential antenna work from Quintel, and that an employee walked out of a T-Mobile lab with a piece of its “Tappy” testing robot in his backpack. They said Huawei also helped Iran’s government monitor and censor the internet (Justice Department, The Guardian).

Huawei says the charges are “without legal or factual merit,” and its lawyer says it will appeal. Sentencing is set for Sept. 14, 2027, and the company faces fines, forfeiture and restitution. China’s foreign ministry said it “firmly opposes the U.S. suppression and containment of Chinese companies” (NBC News).

Why it matters: Canadians know this case. Meng Wanzhou’s arrest in Vancouver started a nearly three-year standoff with China, including the detention of Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor.

Science corner: a peptide built backwards by mistake, then left in a freezer for years, grabs rare earth metals 10 times harder

Chemists at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf were copying the metal-grabbing parts of lanmodulin, a bacterial protein that latches onto rare earth elements. By mistake they assembled their first short peptide in reverse order. They put it in a freezer and forgot about it (Interesting Engineering, HHU).

Years later, Lena Daumann and Sophie Marie Gutenthaler-Tietze tested it with colleagues at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf. Three of four reversed peptides bound lanthanides about 10 times more strongly than the natural versions. The fourth bound very strongly once the team swapped a single amino acid. The work was published in Angewandte Chemie (Interesting Engineering, peer-reviewed paper).

The German Chemical Society gave the team this year’s €10,000 Mario Markus Prize for playful science, to be presented Dec. 15 in Frankfurt (GDCh, Interesting Engineering).

Why it matters: Rare earths go into phone speakers, EV motors and wind turbines, and getting them out of old devices is dirty, hard work. Molecules that pick out specific rare earths could make recycling cleaner.

Sources (5)

AI news and advances

Google DeepMind’s Gemini teams find quantum programs up to 12 times cheaper for a fusion problem, and prove each one correct

Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI set teams of Gemini 4 AI systems to work improving known quantum algorithms. Every four hours, the teams met at a “virtual conference” to trade and debate ideas. Each improvement had to be proved correct in Lean, a tool that checks math proofs, because the circuits are far too big to test on today’s machines (Google DeepMind, The Next Web).

For a calculation used in laser-driven fusion research, the agents cut the computing cost 11.8-fold and the memory needed from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. They also made a light-sensitive-molecule simulation about eight times cheaper, and work on Cytochrome P450, an enzyme that breaks down most medicines, 2.5 times cheaper. One widely used math routine got 290 times cheaper (Google DeepMind, TechTimes).

DeepMind has open-sourced its Lean library and verified results. The Next Web reports an outside checker re-ran every proof so the agents couldn’t game the tests.

Why it matters: Useful quantum computers will be small at first. Cheaper programs mean real science problems could fit on them sooner, and proof-checked AI results are easier to trust.

Sources (5)
  • Google DeepMind: Finding faster quantum algorithms with agents and formal verification
  • The Next Web: DeepMind’s AI agents found cheaper quantum algorithms, and proved them
  • TechTimes: DeepMind AI agents cut fusion simulation cost nearly 12x
  • The Neuron: Everything that happened in AI today, Nov. 24
  • arXiv: Discovering formally verified quantum programs with AI
Community Note

DeepMind itself says these simulations would still need more resources than the first error-corrected quantum computers are likely to have. This is a step forward, not something anyone can run today.

The paper is a preprint on arXiv and hasn’t been peer-reviewed yet. The numbers are DeepMind’s, though the Lean proofs and code are public for others to check.

A brain implant that speaks for a woman with motor neuron disease needs a cart of computers, and Nvidia aims to shrink it to a box on her wheelchair

Austin-based Paradromics announced Monday it will use Nvidia’s low-power Jetson modules and Holoscan software to decode brain signals into speech. Its Connexus implant has more than 400 electrodes and lets a Michigan woman named Mary, who struggles to speak because of motor neuron disease, talk through a synthesized voice (Wired, Business Wire).

Right now the decoding runs on a cart of electronics that has to be plugged into the wall. CEO Matt Angle told Wired the goal is a small box that sits on her wheelchair within about a year, and eventually something phone-sized. Paradromics earlier reported she used the system to talk with her grandchildren on a live phone call (Paradromics, Anadolu Agency, The Neuron).

Running AI models on the device itself would mean users don’t need an internet connection, and could make the voice sound more natural, the company says. Nvidia says it will not have access to patients’ brain data (Wired).

Why it matters: Speech implants only help if people can use them away from a lab cart. Smaller, faster computers are a big step toward that.
