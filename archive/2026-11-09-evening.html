Alberta

Alberta puts $39 million toward land and planning for nine new urgent care centres, including two in Edmonton

Hospital and Surgical Health Services Minister Adriana LaGrange announced on Monday in Sylvan Lake that the province will spend $39 million over three years to plan and buy land for nine new urgent care centres. That would take Alberta’s network from six centres to 15. The sites are west Edmonton, south Edmonton, Stony Plain–Spruce Grove, east Calgary, Lethbridge, Medicine Hat, Cold Lake, Fort McMurray and Sylvan Lake (Global News, CityNews, rdnewsnow).

Urgent care treats same-day problems that aren’t life-threatening, such as broken bones, cuts, infections and asthma attacks. The province says the new centres could take up to 300,000 visits a year away from emergency rooms. Five of today’s six centres are in the Calgary region, and Edmonton has only one, in the central city, open evenings and weekends. LaGrange said she hopes each centre opens within three years of shovels going in the ground, but timing depends on when land is bought (Global News, rdnewsnow, CityNews).

Why it matters: Edmonton’s south and west sides would get their own urgent care for the first time, which could mean shorter ER waits for minor injuries once the centres are built.

Four Calgary friends who named their group chat “Manifesting Millions” win Canada’s biggest lottery prize ever, $90 million

Eidelson Elchico, Lovelyn Malvar, Hazeline Ong and Velna Ramirez were revealed as the winners of the $90-million Lotto Max jackpot from the Oct. 20 draw, the largest lottery prize in Canadian history. The friends, who met through their families more than five years ago, bought the ticket on Play Alberta and will split it evenly, $22.5 million each (Calgary Herald, Global News, CityNews).

They decided to buy in early October in a text group chat called “Manifesting Millions,” started as the jackpot kept rolling over. Elchico found the winning email at about 3 a.m. and first thought it said $9,000 or $900. “When I saw the email I was like ‘we definitely got scammed,’” Malvar said. All four are still working, plan to support their families and say they’ll donate part of the money to the community (Global News, Calgary Herald, CityNews).

Why it matters: Lottery wins in Canada are tax-free, so the full $90 million goes to the four winners, and it’s a rare bit of pure good news from Calgary.

Canada

The Supreme Court of Canada starts hearing the challenge to Ottawa’s 2020 ban on about 1,500 “assault-style” gun models

The country’s top court began two days of arguments on Monday over whether the federal cabinet overstepped its powers when it banned the use, sale and import of about 1,500 firearm models and variants in May 2020, including the AR-15 and Ruger Mini-14. The list has since grown to more than 2,500 types. Gun owners, businesses, hunters and sport shooters lost in the Federal Court in 2023 and the Federal Court of Appeal in 2025 (The Canadian Press via Global News, The Globe and Mail, Supreme Court of Canada).

The key question is whether cabinet ignored a Criminal Code limit that stops it from banning guns it considers “reasonable for use in Canada for hunting or sporting purposes.” Federal lawyers say the banned guns were owned by only 3% to 5% of gun owners and that thousands of alternatives remain legal. The Canadian Coalition for Firearm Rights and other appellants say the lower courts never asked whether the guns were actually used for hunting or sport. Several judges asked probing questions on Monday, and arguments continue Tuesday (The Canadian Press, The Globe and Mail, Canadian Constitution Foundation).

Why it matters: The ruling decides whether hundreds of thousands of Canadians must give up or deactivate guns they bought legally, and how much power cabinet has to ban products by regulation.

Sources (4)

  • The Globe and Mail: Gun owners to battle Ottawa’s firearms ban at Supreme Court
  • Supreme Court of Canada: Hearings 41858, 41859, 41860 and 41861
  • Canadian Constitution Foundation: CCF argues at Supreme Court for clear rules when firearms become prohibited property
Community Note

The court won’t rule this week; a decision is expected by next year. The amnesty for owners, which was due to end Oct. 30, now runs until 90 days after the ruling. The case is about cabinet’s legal authority, not a Charter right to own guns, which Canada doesn’t have.

Russia’s Novaya Gazeta holds its last editorial meeting and closes its Moscow newsroom after three decades

Novaya Gazeta, one of the last independent newspapers in Russia, ended operations on Monday and left its central Moscow office, saying state pressure had made it impossible to keep reporting. The paper was labelled a “foreign agent” in September, and it says banks then stopped processing the reader donations it depended on. Its editor-in-chief, Sergei Sokolov, had his home searched in October, and another editor was arrested in April (AP, Al Jazeera with AFP and Reuters, DW).

Co-founder and Nobel Peace Prize winner Dmitry Muratov told journalists gathered outside that constitutional guarantees of political and civil rights had “effectively ceased to function.” The paper was started in 1993 with help from Mikhail Gorbachev’s Nobel prize money, and several of its reporters were killed, including Anna Politkovskaya in 2006. The Kremlin called the closure part of “a normal cycle of change” (AP, Al Jazeera, DW).

Why it matters: It removes one of the few remaining Russian newsrooms that openly reported critically on the war in Ukraine and the Kremlin.

COP31 opens in Antalya with a push for action over new targets, as the UN says 2026 will be one of the four hottest years on record

Nearly 200 countries opened the UN climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, on Monday. It runs to Nov. 20, and more than 90,000 people are expected. Türkiye’s environment minister, Murat Kurum, took the gavel as COP31 president, while Australia’s climate minister, Chris Bowen, runs the negotiations under an unusual shared presidency. UN climate chief Simon Stiell told delegates that progress is “nowhere near far enough or fair enough” and urged them to “supercharge solutions” (UN News, AP, AFP, Climate Home News).

Bowen said he wants to shape a political decision from the first day instead of in a last-minute crunch. Money for poorer countries to adapt and switch to clean energy is expected to be the hardest fight. The same day, the World Meteorological Organization said 2026 is on track to be one of the four hottest years on record, helped by a strong El Niño, and Singapore’s environment minister said no big new emissions targets are expected (UN News, Reuters, Climate Home News, The Guardian).

Why it matters: Canada and Alberta’s oil and gas sector will be watching what countries agree on methane, clean energy and climate finance, which can shape future rules and trade.

A fire in a 40-storey building in Taiyuan, China, kills 14 people and sends 38 to hospital

Fire broke out at about 5 p.m. Sunday local time in a mixed-use high-rise on a busy downtown road in Taiyuan, the capital of Shanxi province. Xinhua said 14 people died and 25 were still in hospital Monday, nine of them in serious condition. Reuters-verified video showed smoke pouring up the 40-storey tower as firefighters on cranes fought flames that spread up from a lower annex (Reuters via CNA, Xinhua, China Daily).

State television said the fire appears to have started around the fourth floor of the annex and spread to the eighth and ninth floors, where a hotel operates. The building also holds short-term rental apartments, small venues and tutoring centres. The fire was out after about two hours. Authorities have taken the building owner’s managers and the head of property management into custody while they investigate the cause (Xinhua, China Daily, Caixin).

Why it matters: High-rise fires in China have led to nationwide safety inspections before, and this one is likely to renew scrutiny of crowded mixed-use towers that combine hotels, homes and classrooms.

Nvidia shares slide about 4% on a day the market soared, after its board opens an investigation into CEO Jensen Huang’s affair with an alleged Chinese spy

Nvidia said Sunday that independent directors and outside lawyers will investigate Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, a Chinese businesswoman whom U.S. intelligence agencies believe is a spy. The two aren’t related. Huang admitted the affair in an email to staff, writing that colleagues and shareholders “deserve better from me,” and said he will co-operate. The board says it has “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.” China called the spying claims smears (NBC News, CNBC, Reuters).

Nvidia fell about 4.2% Monday, erasing roughly US$200 billion in value, and was the worst performer in the S&P 500 and the Dow even as both indexes jumped more than 2% and other chipmakers rallied. Lead independent director Stephen Neal, a veteran Cooley LLP lawyer, is expected to play a central role. The company hasn’t given a timeline. Nvidia says Huang intends to stay on as CEO, and President Trump said he should (NBC News, CNBC, Reuters via StreetInsider).

Why it matters: Nvidia is one of the biggest holdings in index funds, pensions and RRSPs, and any doubt over the founder who has run it since 1993 hangs over the whole AI trade.

Science corner

Science corner: a 3D printer that spreads thick paste into thin sheets and hardens it with light, printing four times faster with no support scaffolding

3D printers usually make you choose. Nozzle printers can squeeze out almost any material, but they trace one line at a time and struggle with fine detail. Light-based printers are fast and sharp, but they only work with thin, clear resins. Northwestern University engineers report in Science Advances a hybrid called direct ink additive lithography, or DIAL. A slot nozzle lays down a thick, toothpaste-like ink, a blade spreads it into a layer 50 microns thick, and a projector hardens just the pattern it needs, layer after layer (Northwestern University, Science Advances, 3DPrint.com).

The trick is in the ink. Fumed-silica particles make it flow while the blade passes and then stiffen again, so the unhardened ink holds up overhangs and bridges by itself. Afterwards it simply washes off and can be reused. In tests, DIAL printed a grid in 30 minutes that took a normal nozzle printer 124, made lattices with walls just 150 microns thin, and printed interlocking chainmail. The team also printed battery-electrolyte lattices and hydrogel cell-culture dishes (Northwestern, VoxelMatters, 3DPrint.com).

Why it matters: Faster, finer printing with functional materials could speed up custom medical devices, soft robot parts and new battery designs.

Sources (4)

  • Northwestern University: New method DIALs up new capabilities for 3D printing
  • 3DPrint.com: A novel AM process: DIAL 3D printing

AI news and advances

Anthropic ships Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, its first frontier models since Amodei called for slowing AI down, 30% faster and 20% cheaper

What changed: On Nov. 7 we reported that Anthropic’s IPO talk had cooled to about $1.2–$1.3 trillion. Since then it has released two new frontier models, and CNBC reports the offering could come as soon as next week at a $1–$1.5 trillion target.

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2, available to everyone, and Claude Mythos 5.2, the same underlying model limited to vetted groups in its cyber and life-sciences programs. The company says both reply 30% faster and cost 20% less than the 5.1 versions, at US$8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens. It says they improve coding, reasoning and vision, and that a pre-release Fable helped solve two open problems in theoretical physics (CNBC, TechCrunch, The Verge, Anthropic).

It is the company’s first frontier release since CEO Dario Amodei called in September for the industry to slow down. “We called for pacing, not pausing,” product lead Dianne Penn told CNBC. Anthropic’s own safety notes say the models suspect they’re being tested more often than earlier ones, which makes them harder to evaluate, and that Mythos 5.2 showed stronger hidden-reasoning abilities on some tests, though the company says it can’t act covertly against users (TechCrunch, CNBC, Anthropic).

Why it matters: Cheaper, faster top-tier models cut the cost of AI tools for businesses, and the safety caveats show how hard it is getting to test the newest systems before release.

OAbout 250 Chicagoans answer a viral flyer to push The Bean “seven inches to the left” — and the 110-ton sculpture doesn’t budge

Seen on: Instagram, Reddit’s r/chicago and an anonymous Partiful invite, as reported by Block Club Chicago and UPI.

A flyer that took off online Saturday told Chicagoans, “we need to move The Bean 7 inches to the left,” with the first push set for exactly 12:47 p.m. Sunday. The Partiful page explained that a hidden latch door might sit under the sculpture, so moving it could let people “give the man inside The Bean a pickle and other resources,” a nod to a long-running joke that someone is trapped inside (Block Club Chicago, UPI).

Hundreds swarmed Anish Kapoor’s Cloud Gate in Millennium Park and pushed together. It didn’t move. Local promoter Taylor Bennett jokingly declared “at least one inch” in a video, then clarified it hadn’t moved at all. “In the back of everyone’s minds, we knew it wasn’t really gonna move,” art student Saniyah Whaley said. “It brought a lot of joy to a lot of people there” (Block Club Chicago, UPI, CBS Chicago, ARTnews).

Why it matters: A completely pointless group project that got strangers out of their dorms and laughing together, which is the best kind of internet.
