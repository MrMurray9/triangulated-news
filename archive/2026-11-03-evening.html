Alberta

Alberta tables Bill 38: universities must stay officially neutral on political issues, and the 2% tuition cap ends after next year

Advanced Education Minister Myles McDougall introduced Bill 38, the Advanced Education Statutes Amendment Act, in the legislature on Tuesday afternoon. It would require Alberta’s public universities and colleges to adopt “institutional neutrality” policies, so the schools themselves can’t take official positions on political, social or ideological matters unrelated to their academic mission. Students, faculty and staff would keep the right to voice their own views, and the bill also sets minimum standards for student codes of conduct (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail, The Gateway).

The bill also lets the minister set maximum tuition by ministerial order. Domestic tuition increases stay capped at 2% for 2027–28 while the province works out program-by-program tuition ceilings and a new funding model for next year’s budget. Separately, McDougall said parental income will no longer count in provincial student-aid assessments for 2026–27. Penalties for schools that break the neutrality rules will be set later by regulation (The Globe and Mail, Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: It changes what the U of A, MacEwan, NAIT and other schools can say officially, and it opens the door to bigger tuition increases for some programs after 2028.

Sources (3)

  • The Globe and Mail: Alberta tables bill to end tuition hike cap, bar schools from expressing ideological positions
  • The Gateway: Minister outlines legislative agenda for post-secondary education
Community Note

This is a bill, not law yet, and the details (what counts as a violation, and the penalties) will come later in regulations. All of Alberta’s public post-secondary schools had already adopted similar neutrality principles, according to the 2025 Mintz panel report. The Gateway is the U of A student newspaper.

Sources: Edmonton Journal; The Globe and Mail; The Gateway.

Enoch Cree Nation breaks ground on 166 homes to bring members back, backed by a $30-million federal infrastructure loan

maskêkosihk Enoch Cree Nation, just west of Edmonton, has started building 166 affordable single-family homes for members, aimed at a housing waiting list of about 350 families. The Canada Infrastructure Bank has closed a $30-million, 27-year loan for the roads, water and sewer lines, utilities and solar installations the homes need, the bank’s largest investment in an Indigenous housing project so far (Canada Infrastructure Bank, Edmonton Journal, Enoch Cree Nation via Newswire).

The Nation says the first homes should be ready around spring 2027, with most of the rest finished later that year and the final lots in early 2028. Leaders say the goal is to let members who moved away for lack of housing come home (Edmonton Journal, Enoch Cree Nation, Taproot Edmonton).

Why it matters: Housing shortages on reserves push many families into Edmonton’s tight rental market; this is one of the largest on-reserve housing builds in the region.

Canada

Nearly one in three Canadians skipped or shrank meals this year because food cost too much, Salvation Army survey finds

The Salvation Army’s 2026 Canadian Poverty and Socioeconomic Analysis, released Tuesday, found 31% of Canadians skipped meals or cut portions because they couldn’t afford groceries, up from 27% last year. Use of food banks and other community food supports rose to 15% from 11% (The Salvation Army, Global News, CityNews, Le Devoir).

Young adults were hit hardest: 41% of 18- to 34-year-olds said they’d skipped or reduced meals and 23% had used food supports. Almost nine in 10 respondents said they were worried trade disputes with the U.S. would push food prices higher, and half said they had dipped into savings to cover basics (The Salvation Army, Global News, CityNews).

Why it matters: It lands two days before Thursday’s federal budget, adding pressure on Ottawa to show what it will do about grocery costs.

U.S. hits Iran’s Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, calling it a limited defensive strike, as Iran sets terms to reopen the strait

The United States carried out strikes on Iran’s Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz, on Monday. U.S. officials, speaking anonymously, said the strikes were limited, aimed at Iranian forces firing surface-to-air missiles at U.S. aircraft, and were not a return to major combat. Iranian state media had reported several explosions on the island. U.S. Central Command didn’t comment (Reuters, The Associated Press, Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty, Al Jazeera).

President Donald Trump said he was “in no rush” to decide whether to resume fighting. Iran’s foreign ministry said the strait could reopen within two days if Washington accepts its demands, and a Qatari delegation arrived in Tehran to keep mediating. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signalled more diplomacy (Reuters, AP, Al Jazeera).

Why it matters: About a fifth of the world’s oil normally moves through Hormuz, so any flare-up there feeds straight into fuel prices, including at Alberta pumps and in oil company earnings.

Two UN-backed reports warn that passing 1.5°C of warming would do lasting damage to glaciers, ice sheets and permafrost

Two reports released Monday by the UN Environment Programme and the International Cryosphere Climate Initiative say that overshooting 1.5°C of global warming, even temporarily, will bring “large-scale and in many cases irreversible” losses to the world’s ice: glaciers, polar ice sheets, permafrost, sea ice and polar oceans (AFP via France 24, The Guardian, ICCI).

The scientists say glacier loss and sea-level rise caused during an overshoot would last for centuries even if temperatures came back down, and that a safe long-term level for Earth’s ice is closer to 1°C. They call for faster emission cuts plus removing carbon from the air. (The Guardian, France 24, ICCI).

Why it matters: Western Canada’s rivers, including the North Saskatchewan through Edmonton, depend on Rocky Mountain glaciers and snowpack for late-summer flow.

Sources (3)
  • France 24 (AFP): UN reports warn of large-scale damage to Earth’s ice from climate change
  • The Guardian: Cryosphere faces permanent damage when world overshoots 1.5C, scientists warn
  • ICCI: State of the Cryosphere Report 2026 – The Cost of Overshoot
Community Note

These are scientific assessments summarizing existing research, not new measurements. The world is already close to the 1.5°C mark; how far and how long an overshoot lasts depends on future emissions.

Sources: AFP; The Guardian; ICCI and UNEP.

Business

Marathon Petroleum sells its Minnesota and North Dakota refineries to Icahn’s CVR Energy for US$4.2 billion as war-time margins soar

Marathon Petroleum, the largest U.S. refiner, agreed Tuesday to sell its St. Paul Park refinery in Minnesota and its Mandan refinery in North Dakota, plus a fuel-marketing business, to Carl Icahn-controlled CVR Energy for US$4.2 billion. Marathon gets US$3.45 billion and its pipeline arm MPLX gets US$750 million for related assets. The two plants can process 177,000 barrels a day; the deal is expected to close by year-end (Marathon Petroleum, CVR Energy, Reuters via Investing.com, Oil & Gas Journal).

Marathon also beat third-quarter forecasts, earning US$26.93 a share on an adjusted basis. Its refining margin more than doubled to US$45.70 a barrel as the wars in Iran and Ukraine disrupted fuel supplies, and its refineries ran at 100% of capacity. Trafigura will be the exclusive crude supplier to the two plants under CVR (Reuters, Oil & Gas Journal).

Why it matters: It shows how much money refiners are making from war-driven fuel shortages, which is part of why prices at the pump, including in Alberta, have stayed high.

Science corner

Science corner: a McGill computer made of living bacteria that solves puzzles by multiplying through a maze

Ordinary computers check possible answers to a hard puzzle one after another, which can take impossibly long. A team led by McGill University bioengineer Dan Nicolau tried another approach: they etched a tiny network of channels in which each path stands for one possible answer to a classic maths problem, called subset sum, then let swimming bacteria loose inside (McGill University via TechXplore, Nanowerk, Microsystems & Nanoengineering).

As the bacteria divide, there are more and more of them exploring routes at the same time, like adding processors on the fly; the researchers tune the temperature to keep them from overcrowding. Cameras and image software then trace where the bacteria end up, revealing which exits match a correct answer. The work, 20 years in the making with partners in Europe, the U.K. and Australia, is published in Microsystems & Nanoengineering (TechXplore, Nanowerk).

Why it matters: Some problems, from protein shapes to code-breaking, blow up too fast for ordinary chips; a computer that grows its own processors is a new route to them.

Sources (3)
  • TechXplore (McGill University): Biocomputer can explore multiple solutions at once as bacteria multiply
  • Nanowerk: A biocomputer that runs on living bacteria
  • Microsystems & Nanoengineering: Amplification of computational power by the multiplication of bacteria exploring microfluidic networks
Community Note

This is a proof of concept on small versions of the problem, and it isn’t faster than a laptop yet. The researchers say it isn’t meant to replace electronic or quantum computers. Coverage is based mainly on McGill’s release; the peer-reviewed paper is open access.

Sources: McGill University via TechXplore; Nanowerk; Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

AI news and advances

Anthropic’s updated IPO filing shows almost US$17 billion in revenue in one quarter, and US$895 billion promised for computing power

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, filed an amended IPO prospectus on Monday adding its third-quarter results. Revenue for the three months to Sept. 30 was about US$17 billion, roughly 12 times a year earlier, bringing nine-month revenue to US$33.24 billion (CNBC, Business Insider, Moneycontrol).

The filing also lists about US$895.4 billion in commitments for computing power and data-centre leases, with roughly US$69.8 billion due within a year. Spending on computing pushed the company to a small operating loss for the quarter, after an operating profit earlier in the year, and accounting changes on its convertible notes made the net loss much larger (CNBC, Business Insider).

Why it matters: It is the clearest public look yet at how fast AI revenue is growing, and how much more is being spent to keep up, ahead of what could be one of the biggest stock listings ever.
