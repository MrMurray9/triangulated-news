Wet snow causes more than 60 crashes in Calgary before 8 a.m., and jackknifed semis close Highway 1 to the east

Heavy, wet snow made roads in southern Alberta treacherous on Wednesday. Calgary police said they responded to more than 60 collisions between midnight and 8 a.m.: 16 with injuries, eight hit-and-runs and 37 with no one hurt. The worst was a four-vehicle crash on southbound Deerfoot Trail near Cranston Avenue shortly before 7 a.m., which left one person with serious, life-altering injuries (Global News, CityNews Calgary).

Several jackknifed semis forced RCMP to close westbound Highway 1 between Highway 56 and Gleichen, and Mounties reported vehicles in ditches across the south. Calgary Transit put all its snow detours in place. Environment Canada’s snowfall warning called for 15 to 30 centimetres in total before the system tapers off Wednesday evening (Global News, Lethbridge News Now, CityNews Calgary).

Why it matters: Edmonton is staying mostly dry, but anyone driving south on Highway 2 or east on Highway 1 tonight should check 511 Alberta first.

Now the province steps in on data centres: Bill 42 would bar big ones from within 600 metres of a home, from top farmland and from irrigated land

What changed since the last edition: On Tuesday evening we reported that Calgary council had voted 13-2 to start building its own legal tools for a pause on giant AI data centres. Today the province went further and tabled legislation that would set the rules everywhere in Alberta.

Technology Minister Nate Glubish tabled Bill 42, the Energy, Environment and Utilities Statutes Amendment Act, 2026, on Wednesday. If it passes, data centres drawing 10 megawatts or more couldn’t be built within 600 metres of the nearest home, on Class 1 or 2 farmland (or Class 3 land where a municipality has none better), or on irrigated land. The province says that protects about 14 million acres (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail, The Albertan).

The Alberta Utilities Commission would become the main regulator for those projects, not just their power plants. Designated industrial zones such as Alberta’s Industrial Heartland, brownfield sites and existing power plants are exempt from the setback, and municipalities can ask for bigger buffers. The bill also lets the province set a water-efficiency limit on cooling, with the actual targets to come later in regulations (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail, Daily Hive).

Why it matters: More than 70 data centre projects are in talks with the province, and this decides which rural neighbourhoods could end up next to one.

Sources (4)
  • Edmonton Journal: Alberta tables bill restricting where AI data centres can be built
  • The Globe and Mail: Alberta sets limits on where AI data centres can be built amid backlash
  • The Albertan: Olds mayor applauds newly proposed data centre rules
  • Daily Hive: New rules could change where data centres are built in Alberta
Community Note

The rules would apply only to new projects. Projects already approved by the AUC, or with applications in front of it, aren’t covered.

In August the AUC turned down a 1.4-gigawatt power plant tied to a data centre in Olds because it was too close to homes. On the same day this bill was tabled, the AUC approved a different data-centre power plant at Indus, near Calgary.

Sources: The Globe and Mail; Edmonton Journal; The Albertan

The military pauses almost all cadet activities until April, leaving only weekly parade nights, to save money

An internal Nov. 16 message obtained by the Ottawa Citizen says that “due to the current fiscal environment, all cadet activities are paused” for the rest of the fiscal year, which ends March 31, 2027. The only exception is weekly parade nights. The halt to weekend activities takes effect Nov. 21, and every region is getting similar orders from the vice-chief of the defence staff (Ottawa Citizen, The Globe and Mail).

In Kelowna, families of 93 RCSCC Grenville sea cadets were told that rock climbing, band holiday concerts, marksmanship and the year-end mess dinner are off. National Defence says the program got an extra $27 million in the spring economic update, but more young people signing up means the money has to be spread further. National Defence’s plan for 2026-27 already calls for about $459 million in spending cuts this year (Castanet, Ottawa Citizen, National Defence).

Why it matters: About 72,000 youth aged 12 to 18 are in cadets or Junior Canadian Rangers across Canada, and most weekend trips, competitions and band concerts are now off.

Interest on the federal debt is now Ottawa’s fastest-growing expense, rising to $85.5 billion a year by 2030, budget officer says

Parliamentary Budget Officer Annette Ryan’s four reports on the Nov. 5 budget project public debt charges rising from $60.2 billion this fiscal year to $85.5 billion in 2030-31. That’s growth of more than nine per cent a year, faster than any other federal expense, and works out to about 13.6 per cent of federal revenue (PBO, The Globe and Mail, The Logic).

Ryan warned that the deficit alone understates the cost of borrowing. The budget plans about $750 billion in new borrowing over five years, including loans to Crown corporations that don’t count toward the deficit. Her office still judges current federal fiscal policy sustainable over the long term, though that finding excludes the new defence spending pledges (Canada’s National Observer, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Every dollar spent on interest is a dollar that can’t go to health transfers, defence or tax cuts.

Voyager 1 becomes the first spacecraft ever to reach one light-day from Earth, 49 years after launch

NASA calculated that Voyager 1 crossed one light-day from Earth at 5:16 a.m. Eastern (4:16 a.m. Edmonton time) on Wednesday, Nov. 18. That’s about 25.9 billion kilometres, the distance light travels in 24 hours, so a command sent from Earth now takes a full day to arrive and the reply takes another (NASA, Space.com, EarthSky).

The probe launched in 1977, flew past Jupiter and Saturn, and entered interstellar space in 2012. Engineers have been switching off instruments and heaters one by one to stretch its shrinking nuclear power supply, and it is still sending back data (Caltech, The Verge, Space.com).

Why it matters: It’s the farthest anything humans have built has ever travelled, and the spacecraft is still working.

Sources (4)
  • NASA Science: Voyager 1 — what is a light-day
  • Space.com: NASA’s Voyager 1 probe hits huge milestone in interstellar space
  • EarthSky: Voyager 1 reaches 1 light-day from Earth
  • The Verge: NASA’s Voyager 1 probe is now 1 light-day away
Community Note

The crossing time is NASA’s calculation from Voyager’s known trajectory. Nobody “saw” it happen. Because Earth moves around the Sun, the distance swings slightly over the year.

Voyager 1 hasn’t left the solar system in every sense. It’s past the heliosphere, but still far inside the Oort Cloud of icy bodies that orbit the Sun.

Sources: NASA; Space.com; EarthSky; The Verge; Caltech

Science corner: drones that fly through humpback whales’ breath collect enough DNA to read whole genomes, with no darts needed

Until now, reading a whale’s full genome meant a biopsy dart, which requires a boat to get close, or a stranded or museum animal. A University of St Andrews-led team instead taped sterile petri dishes to a small off-the-shelf drone and flew it through the spout of one humpback at a time off northern British Columbia, usually catching one to six breaths per surfacing (University of St Andrews, Popular Science).

From 58 blow samples from 26 whales, about 84% of the DNA turned out to be the whale’s own. That was enough for low-coverage nuclear genomes and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes that matched biopsies from the same animals, correctly identified every whale’s sex, and turned up three mitochondrial variants never described before. More than a third of the flights launched from shore with no boat at all. The study ran this week in Molecular Ecology Resources (University of St Andrews, Popular Science, Global News).

Why it matters: It gives scientists a gentle way to track inbreeding, family ties and health in whales they can’t or shouldn’t dart.

Sources (3)
  • University of St Andrews: Whole genome sequencing using whale snot
  • Popular Science: Whale snot helps scientists sequence an entire genome
  • Global News (The Canadian Press): Researchers on B.C. coast use drones to collect whale snot for genome sequencing
Community Note

The rest of the DNA, about 16%, came mostly from microbes in the whales’ airways, so one breath may also say something about respiratory health.

The work was done in Gitga’at First Nation territory under agreements with the Gitga’at and the North Coast Cetacean Society, with a Fisheries and Oceans Canada permit. Resting whales weren’t sampled, and flights stopped if a whale reacted.

Sources: University of St Andrews; Popular Science; The Canadian Press

Google releases Gemini 4 Neon, a faster mid-size model it says comes close to its flagship at much lower cost

Google DeepMind launched Gemini 4 Neon on Wednesday, its fourth new Gemini model in about a month. It’s priced at US$3 per million input tokens and US$15 per million output tokens, with a one-million-token context window. Google pitches it as sitting between the top-end Gemini 4 Carbon and the quick Hydrogen model (Google, 9to5Google, The Decoder).

Why it matters: Cheaper near-top-tier models are what make AI practical for everyday tools at schools and small businesses.
