Alberta

Alberta’s wildfire season ends with less than 3% of the usual forest burned, after a record-wet June

Alberta Wildfire counted 785 fires in the season that ran from March 1 to Oct. 31. Together they burned just under 18,000 hectares, compared with a five-year average of more than 660,000 hectares and an average of 996 fires a season (Government of Alberta, Red Deer Advocate, CJWE, CochraneNow).

The province credits mostly the weather: record or near-record rain early in the summer, including Edmonton’s wettest June in 112 years of records. In mid-August Alberta had no active wildfires at all, which officials say last happened 40 years ago. Forestry and Parks Minister Todd Loewen said “wildfire doesn’t keep a calendar,” and pointed to two new hoist-equipped helicopters, quicker initial attack and about 1,000 hectares of new firebreaks. More than 1,000 Alberta firefighters were sent to help in Ontario, Quebec, the Northwest Territories, B.C., the U.S. and Australia (Red Deer Advocate, CJWE).

Why it matters: After several years of evacuations, including Jasper in 2024, a quiet season spared communities and the provincial budget; it also gave crews time to cut firebreaks before next spring.

Revealed: Alberta’s proposed 91-riding map splits Lethbridge four ways, and two panel members say it breaks the panel’s own mandate

What changed since the last edition: This morning we reported the justice minister’s refusal to delay the election while the report was still private. The report itself was tabled Monday afternoon, so its riding-by-riding proposals and the minority’s objections are now public.

The O’Ferrall panel’s report was tabled in the legislature Monday afternoon. It proposes 91 ridings, up from 87. Lethbridge’s two city ridings would become four: one mostly urban seat and three that pair parts of the city with large stretches of the surrounding county. A similar urban–rural mix is proposed for parts of northwest Calgary, and two northern rural ridings that an earlier commission had dropped would come back (Canada’s National Observer, Western Standard, The Globe and Mail).

Chair Brian O’Ferrall, a retired judge, wrote that the proposals are constitutional. The two NDP-appointed members, Gerald Kennedy and Brent Robinson, signed a minority response: they back restoring the northern seats but say every change south of Airdrie goes beyond what the panel was asked to do, and that it favours rural voters at the expense of urban ones. University of Alberta political scientist Jared Wesley called it “politicians picking voters.” The NDP repeats it will go to court if MLAs pass the map (National Observer, Western Standard).

Why it matters: This map decides how Lethbridge, Calgary and rural southern Alberta are represented in the Oct. 18, 2027, election, and a court fight could add to the time pressure Elections Alberta already warned about.

St’át’imc chiefs take Ottawa to Federal Court over fast-tracking the Pacific Link oil pipeline

The St’át’imc Chiefs Council, representing 11 St’át’imc communities near Lillooet, B.C., said Monday it has filed for a judicial review of Ottawa’s October decision to list Pacific Link as a project of national interest under the Building Canada Act. The pipeline would carry about one million barrels of crude a day from Bruderheim, Alta., to a deepwater port near Delta, B.C. (APTN News, Richmond News, Global News).

Chief Bonnie Adolph of Sekw’el’was said the Major Projects Office refused to consult her community as a First Nation because its land is not directly on the route, even though the line would cross the Fraser River system that salmon use twice in their lives. The Canadian Press counts eight separate court challenges from nine B.C. nations so far, including Tsartlip on Vancouver Island, which says the consultation was squeezed into less than three months over the summer, and Boston Bar, Cheam, Kwantlen and Lower Nicola. The council says the route involves more than 500 river crossings; the pipeline is estimated to cost $35.2 billion to $42.7 billion (APTN News, The Canadian Press via Global News, Richmond News).

Why it matters: Pacific Link is meant to be Alberta’s new export route to the West Coast; court cases over consultation have stalled pipelines before, including Northern Gateway and the Trans Mountain expansion.

A judicial review asks a court whether the government followed the law in making a decision; it does not by itself stop construction. Ottawa has said consultation will continue as the project moves through review. The claims about consultation are the nations’ allegations and have not yet been tested in court.

Carney lands in Antigua for his first Commonwealth summit, pitching 56 countries on trade beyond the U.S.

Prime Minister Mark Carney arrived Monday in St. John’s, Antigua and Barbuda, for the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting, his first as prime minister. He is spending one day there, including a lunch for new leaders and a dinner hosted by King Charles, and plans a working breakfast with Caribbean leaders before Tuesday’s opening ceremony (The Canadian Press via The Globe and Mail, CTV News, Prime Minister’s Office).

Ottawa says Canada already does about $100 billion a year in trade with Commonwealth countries. Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand, who is travelling with Carney, pointed to the group’s target of $2 trillion in trade among members by 2030. Antigua’s high commissioner to Canada says Prime Minister Gaston Browne will propose a bilateral deal on joint ventures. King Charles is expected to address the legacy of slavery, a live issue for Caribbean members seeking reparations (The Canadian Press, CTV News).

Why it matters: It’s another stop in Carney’s push to diversify trade away from the United States, coming days after the Canada–EU summit and as Canada–U.S. talks restart.

Supreme Court turns down the Trump administration’s bid to wall off 20,000 California mail ballots on the eve of the midterms

The U.S. Supreme Court on Monday rejected an emergency request from the Trump administration to make California set aside the mail ballots of more than 20,000 people the federal government says are likely not citizens. The court gave no explanation. A federal judge and an appeals court had already refused the request (AP, NBC News, SCOTUSblog, CBS News).

The administration filed its appeal Monday morning, a day before Tuesday’s midterm elections. California officials disputed the accuracy of the federal list and warned that separating the ballots could keep eligible citizens’ votes from being counted (AP, NBC News, SCOTUSblog).

Why it matters: It settles, for now, a fight over how ballots are handled in the most populous state, where several close House races could help decide control of Congress.

Hackers hijack both Ukraine’s and Russia’s emergency-alert apps, pushing fake nuclear and chemical warnings overnight

Late Sunday, Ukraine’s Air Alert app, which people rely on to find shelter from Russian missiles and drones, sent false nationwide warnings of high radiation, a chemical threat and street disturbances in Kyiv, including one message saying “the Oreshnik is incoming,” a Russian missile. Ukraine suspended the app and had it running again Monday (AP via PBS, The Record, The Washington Post, CBS News).

Hours later, Russia’s Emergency Situations Ministry said hackers used its own app to send a fake alert to 33 of Russia’s 89 regions at about 2:45 a.m. Moscow time. A Russian Telegram channel said a group called Hitcore claimed the Ukrainian hack, which could not be verified, and it is unclear whether the two attacks are linked. Ukraine’s air force said military systems were not affected (AP, The Record, The Washington Post).

Why it matters: False alarms can push people to ignore real ones, and both countries depend on these apps to warn civilians in a war fought largely with missiles and drones.

Oracle files to raise US$20 billion in new shares and bonds for AI data centres, and its stock falls almost 9%

Oracle filed with U.S. regulators Monday to sell US$10 billion of common stock and US$10 billion of senior notes, in one floating-rate and five fixed-rate tranches maturing between 2029 and 2046. The two offerings are separate, and Oracle says the money is for general purposes, including capital spending and paying down debt (CNBC, SEC filing, U.S. News & World Report, The Next Web).

Oracle previewed the raise at last week’s investor day and said it would be the last new capital-market money it needs through fiscal 2030, yet shares fell 8.8% Monday. The stock is down 33% this year. Oracle carries about US$125 billion of debt, S&P rates it BBB-, one notch above junk, and its credit-default swaps, a gauge of default risk, have climbed sharply over the past year (CNBC, U.S. News).

Why it matters: Oracle is one of the biggest borrowers in the AI build-out, so how investors treat its debt is a test of how much more money markets will put into AI data centres.

Science corner

Science corner: tiny silk needles that vaccinate baby fish underwater, then melt away

Vaccinating farmed fish today usually means sedating them, lifting them out of the water and injecting them one at a time, and it doesn’t work on fish smaller than about 30 grams. Engineers at MIT and the U.S. Department of Agriculture have made a patch of microneedles from food-grade silk that can do the job in the water, on rainbow trout weighing just 1 to 2 grams (MIT News, Salmon Business, ACS Nano).

Silk normally softens as soon as it gets wet, so the team coated hollow silk tips with shellac, the food-safe resin used to glaze candy and pills. The coated needles pierce wet fish skin, stay put while the fish swims, and release a bacterial or DNA vaccine over anywhere from 15 minutes to 24 hours before breaking down. Dry, they kept about half of a DNA vaccine intact for four weeks without refrigeration. The work is published in ACS Nano (MIT News, Salmon Business).

Why it matters: More of the world’s seafood now comes from farms than from the wild, and a gentler, needle-free way to protect the youngest fish could cut disease losses and make the work safer for farm workers.

Sources (3)
  • MIT News: MIT engineers design a safer, more efficient vaccination system for farmed fish
  • Salmon Business: MIT researchers test underwater vaccination in trout weighing just two grams
  • MIT J-WAFS: Precise fish vaccine injection using silk-based biomaterials
Community Note

This is lab-scale work in one species. The researchers say more development is needed before farm-scale use, and the trials don’t show how well it works in other species such as Atlantic salmon. Coverage leans on MIT’s own release; the peer-reviewed paper is in ACS Nano.

Sources: MIT News; Salmon Business; ACS Nano.

AI news and advances

Meituan, China’s food-delivery giant, launches a 1.6-trillion-parameter AI model built to code for hours on its own

Meituan released LongCat-2.5 on Monday. The model has 1.6 trillion parameters in total, but only about 48 billion are switched on for each word it processes, a design that keeps it cheaper to run. It reads images and text together and is aimed at long jobs that AI agents carry out step by step, such as working through big codebases or finishing whole software projects (Meituan, TechNode, AI Weekly).

The company says it trained the model on long task sequences with large-scale reinforcement learning, and turned failures by real agents into practice tasks. LongCat-2.5 is available now in Meituan’s chat app and API; Meituan says releases on GitHub and Hugging Face will follow, and it highlighted running the model on Chinese-made chips (TechNode, Meituan, AI Weekly).

Why it matters: It shows how even companies best known for delivering dinner are now building frontier-scale AI in China, adding to price pressure on U.S. AI firms.
