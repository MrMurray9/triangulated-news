Edmonton council opens up to 10 days of budget debate Monday, with 264 people signed up to speak on a plan for 4.9% higher property taxes

Edmonton city council starts deliberating its 2027–2030 budget on Monday, Nov. 23, beginning with a public hearing. As of Friday, 264 people had registered to speak. Meetings are booked for Nov. 23 and 24, then Dec. 3, 4, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16 and 18 if council needs them, after which councillors will move and vote on spending changes (Edmonton Journal, City of Edmonton).

As proposed by city staff, residential property taxes would rise 4.9% in 2027, 4.7% in 2028, 4.5% in 2029 and 3.9% in 2030. For a typical home assessed at $492,500, that is about $212 more in civic tax next year, for a total of $4,025. Bus fares would go to $3.15 from $3. The plan includes a $4.1-billion operating budget, with policing the biggest line, and a $6.3-billion capital budget focused on finishing Yellowhead Trail and Valley Line West LRT and on about $2.9 billion in road, bridge and other renewal (Edmonton Journal, Global News).

Why it matters: The decisions over the next few weeks set what Edmontonians pay and which services they get for the next four years.

Two reports find Alberta’s waits getting worse, with Edmonton hospitals’ ER stays the province’s longest and surgery waits up to 50 weeks, and Smith says “we have to try new things”

A Montreal Economic Institute report says the median emergency-room stay in Alberta was four hours last year, 36 minutes longer than five years ago. Edmonton had the longest waits: a median of nine hours at the University of Alberta Hospital, 8 hours 36 minutes at the Misericordia and eight hours at the Royal Alexandra. Calgary’s longest was Foothills, at 7 hours 19 minutes (MEI, CityNews Calgary, Calgary Herald).

A separate Fraser Institute report says Canadians waited a record median 30.7 weeks from a family doctor’s referral to specialist treatment in 2026. Alberta’s wait was 50 weeks, up from 36 a year earlier and among the worst in the country. On her Saturday radio show, Premier Danielle Smith pointed to triage doctors added at several ERs, nine planned urgent-care centres and more surgeries, 330,000 last year, up from 295,000 in 2019–20 (Fraser Institute, Calgary Herald).

Why it matters: If you or your family end up in an Edmonton emergency room, these are the waits you’re likely to face, and they’re heading the wrong way.

Canada

Canada becomes the first North American member of NATO’s Innovation Fund, putting in about $100 million to back defence startups

Defence Minister David McGuinty announced at the Halifax International Security Forum on Saturday that Canada has joined the NATO Innovation Fund as its 25th member and its first in North America, committing €64.59 million, about C$100 million. The Business Development Bank of Canada will manage the stake (Department of National Defence, BDC, The Globe and Mail).

Joining makes Canadian defence and dual-use startups eligible for the fund’s money and pushes its total commitments past €1.1 billion, Defense One reported. The fund’s president, Ari Kristinn Jónsson, said there is “no pro rata,” so there’s no guarantee the money comes back to Canadian firms. Asked about reported U.S. limits on military contacts, McGuinty said: “As far as we’re concerned, it’s business as usual” (Defense One, CPAC).

Why it matters: It gives Canadian defence startups a new pot of venture money and a door into Europe’s defence market as Ottawa tries to rely less on the U.S.

COP31 ends without a deal: the EU and others reject the “Antalya Pact” over its silence on fossil fuels, the first such collapse since Copenhagen in 2009

Science corner

Science corner: a McMaster microneedle patch tracks a heart-damage protein for days without a single blood draw

Most heart injuries after ordinary surgery are silent, so the only way to catch them is a troponin blood test, hard to do once a patient goes home. Engineers at McMaster University, with the University of Waterloo, built a wearable patch whose painless polymer microneedles reach just under the skin, sip the fluid between cells and pass it to a sensor that reads cardiac troponin continuously over several days (McMaster News, Science Advances).

Troponin is hard to track because there’s so little of it, and even less under the skin. The team pairs antibodies, which grab the protein, with aptamers, short engineered DNA strands that produce an electrical signal and can reset, so the readings rise and fall with the real level. In mice with heart disease the patch followed troponin up and down; more testing, with federal funding, comes next (Science Advances, Hospimedica, TWN).

Why it matters: About 300 million people have non-cardiac surgery each year, and roughly 20% of higher-risk patients suffer a heart injury afterward, about 90% with no symptoms. A patch like this could flag it before it turns into a heart attack.

Sources (4)
  • McMaster News: New wearable sensor could help detect hidden heart damage after surgery
  • Science Advances: A wearable biointerface integrating antibody and aptamer probes for real-time tracking of proteins in vivo
  • Hospimedica: Wearable patch enables continuous troponin monitoring for postoperative cardiac injury
  • TWN: Patch instead of blood draws: microneedles to detect heart injury after surgery
Community Note

So far the patch has been tested only in mice; it hasn’t been shown to detect injuries in people. The researchers have federal (CIHR) funding for more testing and a startup, Aptec Health, to commercialize it.

Continuous glucose monitors already work this way for sugar; doing it for a protein at such low concentrations is the new part.

Sources: McMaster University; Science Advances; Hospimedica; TWN

AI news and advances

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, a free open model that “thinks in loops” and runs on a 32 GB laptop

Mira Murati’s Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini on Nov. 20 under the permissive Apache 2.0 licence. It is a mixture-of-experts model with 26.4 billion parameters that handles text and images and has a one-million-token context window. Its trick is “looping”: 12 of its 24 layers run three times for every token, so it can reason longer internally without writing out more words (The Decoder, Hugging Face, Thinking Machines).

The company says it leads open models under 100 billion parameters on agentic coding tests, and independent benchmarker Artificial Analysis ties it with much larger rivals on its intelligence index at a fraction of the cost. It runs locally on Macs with at least 32 GB of memory and on Nvidia’s DGX Spark (The Decoder, Wccftech, Hugging Face).

Why it matters: Strong AI that runs on your own machine means private, offline use with no subscription, and looping is a new way to make small models smarter.

Sources (4)

  • Hugging Face: Welcome Inkling 2 Mini
  • Wccftech: Thinking Machines unveils Inkling 2 Mini
  • Thinking Machines Lab: Introducing Inkling 2 Mini
Community Note

The coding-benchmark leadership is the company’s own claim; the Artificial Analysis score is independent. Reported active-parameter counts differ (3.4 billion per pass, about 5.4 billion counting the loops).

“Open weights” means anyone can download and run the model, but the training data isn’t published.

Lean and Mathlib groups certify the Claude-assisted Riemann hypothesis proof as correct after a day of independent checks

What changed since the last edition: Friday night’s edition reported the claim and early checks. Since then the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative have formally certified both the statement and the proof as correct.

On Saturday the Lean and Mathlib communities, backed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, issued a joint statement saying they have “full confidence” that Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge’s proof is correct. They say the 17-million-line Lean library compiles for dozens of people, proves exactly Mathlib’s vetted statement of the hypothesis, and passed several independent proof checkers, two of which are themselves formally proven unable to accept a false proof (Lean community blog, The Guardian, The Decoder).

The 1859 conjecture about how prime numbers are spread out is one of the Clay Institute’s seven US$1-million Millennium Prize problems. Alpöge says Claude helped him reach the proof over about six weeks. Mathematicians now have to work through the ideas in the proof itself (The Guardian, Scientific American).

Why it matters: If it holds, one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics has been cracked with an AI’s help, and checked by computer rather than by years of peer review.
