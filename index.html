Alberta

Advance polls open at 9 a.m. Tuesday: Elections Alberta urges off-peak visits as it expects record turnout for the Oct. 19 referendum

In-person advance voting for Alberta’s 10-question referendum starts Tuesday, Oct. 13, and runs through Saturday, Oct. 17, at about 310 stations, most open 9 a.m. to 8 p.m.; Elections Alberta says some locations’ days and hours vary. Voters must use a station in their own electoral division. “Look for the off-peak times like mid-morning after lunch,” Elections Alberta spokesperson Michelle Gurney told Global News. “We tend to see more of a rush after dinner.” Bringing your voter card speeds things up, but any registered voter can vote with ID.

Elections Alberta forecasts turnout of 80 to 85 per cent, versus an average of just over 55 per cent in the last five provincial elections, the Edmonton Journal reports. An Ipsos poll for Global News in September found 81 per cent of Albertans plan to vote. Ballots must be hand-counted within 48 hours of polls closing; Question 10 on separation will be counted first, with a final result expected between 1 a.m. and 4 a.m. on Oct. 20 and the other nine questions sometime on Oct. 21. Mailed special ballots must reach Elections Alberta in Edmonton by 5 p.m. Friday, Oct. 16.

Why it matters: If you want to avoid election-day lineups, the next five days are the window, and mid-morning or early afternoon is the quietest time to go.

What changed since the last edition: This morning’s edition covered readiness and mail-in numbers. New today: Elections Alberta’s advice on off-peak times and ID, the hand-count timeline (Question 10 first, other questions Oct. 21), and the Ipsos turnout figure.

Economics Nobel goes to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford for the models central banks use to tame booms and busts

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences to Robert E. Hall of Stanford and Michael Woodford of Columbia “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation.” After the 1970s oil shocks, when older Keynesian models seemed to fail, the two built models based on how forward-looking households and businesses actually make decisions.

“With them, we are better equipped to deal with shocks in the economy, such as energy crises or sudden changes in how much households consume,” said committee chair John Hassler. Those models underpin how central banks such as the Bank of Canada set and communicate interest rates. The two share 12 million Swedish kronor. Woodford told the Nobel Foundation the call was “a tremendous honor” and “a surprise.”

Why it matters: When the Bank of Canada explains where rates are headed to steer mortgages and prices, it’s using the approach these two economists helped build.

Science corner

Science corner: NC State engineers use a laser to turn a thin beam of air into a “lightsaber” antenna that transmits radio

Researchers at North Carolina State University fired a laser to ionize a narrow channel of air into a plasma filament, then used it as an antenna to transmit radio waves at 30 MHz. To get a signal into the plasma without touching it, they built a contactless feed: a metal ring acting as a capacitor that the laser passes through. A radio-frequency generator drives the ring, and the filament radiates the signal.

Because the laser’s settings control the filament’s length, and steering the beam changes its angle, the antenna could in principle be tuned across many frequencies and pointed without moving parts. “It is the first time anyone has ever demonstrated that plasma-filament antennas can work,” said lead author Prya Darshni. The open-access paper appears in the IEEE Journal of Microwaves, with Arthur Dogariu of Texas A&M and Princeton as a co-author.

Why it matters: Antennas you can resize and aim with light could cut weight and moving parts on satellites, and the researchers note low Earth orbit has enough air to form the plasma.
