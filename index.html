Edmonton police say a phone-delivery scam run from Pakistani call centres has cost Edmontonians an estimated $20 million

The Edmonton Police Service says callers posing as Rogers, Telus and Virgin Mobile offered free or deeply discounted phones and plans. Victims ordered real devices through their own carriers. Then a second call claimed the wrong phone had been shipped and asked them to send it to another address, where people tied to the ring collected the phones and resold them. Police link the scheme to several call centres in Lahore, Pakistan, and say it has run since at least October 2024 (EPS, Edmonton Journal, CityNews Edmonton).

About 100 Edmonton reports have come in this year. In early October officers searched a home near 97 Street and 113 Avenue and seized suspected forgery equipment and fake IDs. Johnathan Cesar, 31, is charged with fraud over $5,000, trafficking in property obtained by crime and possessing forgery equipment. EPS passed what it learned to international partners in late October, and police in Pakistan then shut down several of the call centres (EPS, CTV News Edmonton, Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: The scam uses real carrier orders, so it looks legitimate. Police say to hang up on any surprise phone offer and call your provider back on a number you already trust. Report it to EPS at 780-423-4567 or #377 from a mobile.

Ottawa backs De Havilland with up to $2.5 billion in loans to double water-bomber output, adding about 1,200 jobs

Calgary-based De Havilland Aircraft of Canada says Export Development Canada and the federal government will provide up to $2.5 billion in financing. The money will pay for a second production line for its DHC-515 amphibious firefighting plane. Final assembly happens in Calgary for now and will move to De Havilland Field, the plant and airfield being built in Wheatland County, west of Strathmore. The company says the deal could put planes in customers’ hands as early as 2030; it had been sold out until about 2034 (Calgary Herald, De Havilland Canada, The Globe and Mail).

De Havilland expects about 1,200 more direct jobs at its sites in Alberta, B.C. and Ontario, on top of roughly 1,400 people already working on the program. It has orders for 38 planes: 24 from six European countries and 14 from Canadian provinces, including five Alberta bought for about $400 million. The first plane is due to go to Greece in early 2028. The company says the financing is a loan, not a grant, and details should be finalized by the end of March (Calgary Herald, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: It means hundreds of manufacturing jobs near Calgary, and it could get Alberta and other provinces newer firefighting aircraft sooner as wildfire seasons get worse.

Canada

China keeps tariffs off Canadian canola meal, peas and lobster until the end of 2028, and Canada extends a steel break in return

After the first ministerial-level meeting of the Canada–China Joint Economic and Trade Commission in Beijing, Trade Minister Maninder Sidhu says China will keep suspending its 100% tariffs on canola meal and peas and its 25% tariffs on lobster and crab through the end of 2028. The relief had been set to expire on Dec. 31, 2026. In return, Canada is extending tariff remission on some Chinese steel and aluminum products that aren’t made in Canada (Global Affairs Canada, The Globe and Mail, BNN Bloomberg).

Ottawa says exports have rebounded since the relief began in March. Canola meal sales to China rose from $146 million to $390 million, peas from $107 million to $345 million and lobster from $128 million to $200 million. China’s 100% tariff on canola oil is still in place, and the separate duty on canola seed isn’t part of this deal (Global Affairs Canada, The Globe and Mail, BNN Bloomberg).

Why it matters: Canola and peas are major Prairie crops. Two more years of certainty helps Alberta farmers plan, although canola oil remains shut out of China.

The export figures come from the federal government and compare periods before and after the March relief. Prices and harvests also affect those numbers, not only tariffs. The deal is an extension of temporary relief, not a permanent end to the tariffs.

A mother and daughter are killed in a suspected grizzly attack near Bella Coola, B.C., and officers say more than one bear may be involved

Jennifer Balcarras, 48, and her daughter Selah Reed were reported missing on Sunday after going for a walk on Saturday on the Snooka West Trail near their home outside Bella Coola, on B.C.’s central coast. Searchers found Balcarras on Monday and Reed on Tuesday (The Guardian, The Globe and Mail, CBS News).

B.C. Conservation Officer Service Insp. Jeff Tyre says more than one bear may have been involved. Officers have set traps, but no bears had been caught as of Tuesday, and any bear linked to the deaths will be killed. The Nuxalk Nation is recommending a voluntary 8 p.m. curfew and running a shuttle, and RCMP and conservation officers are patrolling at night. A grizzly attacked a group of schoolchildren in the same area in November 2025, injuring 11 people (The Guardian, The Globe and Mail, CBS News).

Why it matters: It’s a rare, tragic double fatality. It’s also a reminder that bears are still out in November before denning, including in Alberta’s mountain parks, so carry bear spray and hike in groups.

Serbia detains elite police officers accused of torturing protesters in a government garage during last year’s anti-corruption demonstrations

Serbia’s interior ministry says it detained serving members of an elite police unit and one former officer on Wednesday. They’re suspected of using force and threats against several people held in the garage of the government compound in central Belgrade on Aug. 14 and 15, 2025, to get information from them and to intimidate and punish them. The ministry released only the suspects’ initials. Serbian media, including the public broadcaster RTS, report that the commander of the SAJ special anti-terrorist unit is among them (The Guardian, B92, RTS).

These are the first major arrests connected to violence against the protest movement that began after a railway station canopy collapse in Novi Sad killed 16 people in November 2024. The arrests come less than two weeks after the Student List defeated the long-ruling Serbian Progressive Party in a snap election on Oct. 25 (The Guardian, Government of Serbia, B92).

Why it matters: It’s an early test of whether Serbia’s new political landscape will bring accountability for police abuse, which the EU, Serbia’s would-be partner, has been watching closely.

Ebola reaches a British patient for the first time in this outbreak as a health worker is flown from Congo to the U.K.

What changed: Since this outbreak was last covered here on Oct. 31, a British aid worker has become the first U.K. case and been flown home, and the death toll has passed 5,300.

A British health-care worker infected with Ebola in the Democratic Republic of Congo has been evacuated to the U.K. for treatment in a specialist high-level isolation unit. Officials describe the worker as stable and say the risk to the British public is low (The Guardian, The Independent, Al Jazeera).

Africa CDC now counts more than 5,300 deaths, almost all in Congo, with a few in Kenya and Uganda, from about 10,400 cases. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, which has no approved vaccine or treatment, although trials of an existing Ebola vaccine have started. Earlier medical evacuations went to Germany, the Netherlands and Switzerland (Al Jazeera, The Guardian, The Independent).

Why it matters: Every evacuation of a foreign aid worker shows how stretched the response is. Public health officials stress the risk to the public in Canada and Europe remains very low.

CVS will break itself in two, splitting Aetna and Caremark from its 9,000 drugstores a decade after the Aetna megamerger

CVS Health says it will separate into two publicly traded companies by the first half of 2028. CVS Health, led by CEO David Joyner, will keep the Aetna insurer, the Caremark pharmacy benefit manager, specialty pharmacy, Oak Street Health and Signify Health. CVS Pharmacy, led by group president Prem Shah, will take the roughly 9,000 stores, mail-order pharmacy, MinuteClinic and the Health100 platform. Shareholders will own stock in both (CVS Health, Axios, Healthcare Dive).

The move reverses years of buying, including Caremark in 2007 and the US$78-billion Aetna deal in 2018. Joyner says the company is acting “from a position of strength” and that the split isn’t driven by regulators, though some U.S. states are trying to bar PBMs and pharmacies from sharing an owner. CVS also posted a US$2.2-billion third-quarter profit on US$108.4 billion in revenue and raised its 2026 outlook. Its shares fell about 2% (Healthcare Dive, CNBC, Axios).

Why it matters: It’s one of the largest corporate breakups ever and a sign the “one company does everything” model in U.S. health care is falling out of favour. Canadians won’t see a direct effect, but it’s a big shift in how the largest U.S. health companies are built.

Science corner

Science corner: a software “twin” of the Webb telescope pulls a faint planet out of the starlight in two-year-old data

Northwestern University astronomers built FLIP, short for Physics-based Learning for Imaging Planets. It models how the James Webb Space Telescope’s optics wobble and blur light, then tunes millions of settings so it can subtract a star’s glare and reveal faint planets beside it. Published in Nature on Nov. 4, the method was 2 to 15 times more sensitive than the standard technique on test targets (Nature, Northwestern University, Scientific American).

Running FLIP on two-year-old Webb data, the team recovered the young giant planet 51 Eridani b at a wavelength where carbon dioxide absorbs light, a band useful for studying planet atmospheres. Northwestern astronomer Jason Wang, part of the team, says the approach borrows optimization tricks from machine learning but isn’t an AI model, because it’s built on the physics of the telescope (Northwestern University, Scientific American).

Why it matters: Better glare removal gets more out of telescopes that are already flying, and it could relax the extreme stability NASA’s planned Habitable Worlds Observatory needs to look for Earth-like planets.

Sources (3)
  • Nature: Physics-based learning for high-contrast imaging with JWST
  • Northwestern University: Hidden planet emerges from the glare
  • Scientific American: Webb is wobbly. Scientists have a fix that could reveal new alien worlds
Community Note

The 2-to-15-times gain was measured on a handful of test stars. Independent groups haven’t yet shown how well FLIP works across many targets. 51 Eridani b was already known; what’s new is seeing it at this wavelength in old data.

Sources: Nature; Northwestern University; Scientific American

Google will switch Gemini and even Google Search off by default for K-12 student accounts, but schools can’t turn on Search without its AI

Google says Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will all be off by default for K-12 student accounts until school administrators turn them on. Vice-president Chris Phillips announced the change Tuesday. After pushback, Google said schools can turn on Search by itself, but it keeps AI Overviews and AI Mode, which is gaining a step-by-step “guided learning” option. That means there’s no AI-free Google Search for students (NBC News, AP, Education Week).

American Federation of Teachers president Randi Weingarten and Fairplay executive director Josh Golin criticized the approach as handing schools a hard choice. Google presents the new defaults as letting administrators decide when students get AI tools (NBC News, Education Week, The Next Web).

Why it matters: Many Alberta schools use Google Workspace for Education. If these defaults roll out here, IT staff and parents may notice Search switched off on student accounts until schools decide how to handle AI.
