Alberta

Smith tells UCP members to “get their papers in order”: a law making voters prove citizenship is likely coming in the spring

At a virtual town hall for United Conservative Party members on Saturday, Premier Danielle Smith said her government will “probably” act in the spring sitting of the legislature on October’s referendum question about proof of citizenship. Voters in the Oct. 18, 2027, provincial election would need a passport, a birth certificate or one of Alberta’s new driver’s licences that can show a citizenship marker (The Canadian Press, via The Globe and Mail and CityNews).

About 58% of referendum voters said yes to the idea. Smith said voting now runs on an “honour system” and suggested mail-in special ballots may need more limits, too. Her office said Sunday it stands by her comments.

Critics say the rule would make voting slower and harder and could open the door to racial or ethnic bias at polling stations. The UCP’s annual meeting starts Friday in Calgary.

Why it matters: Every Alberta voter could need new paperwork before next fall’s election, and many people already struggled to get citizenship documents for the new licences this summer.

Sources (3)
  • The Globe and Mail (The Canadian Press): Alberta to introduce citizen voter ID law, Smith says
  • CityNews Calgary: Alberta premier says proof-of-citizenship voting law likely for spring 2027
  • Culture Alberta: Alberta plans a proof-of-citizenship voting law for 2027
Community Note

Only Canadian citizens can vote in Alberta already. Elections Canada and Elections Alberta have reported very few cases of ineligible voting.

A Canadian passport can’t be used alone to vote in Alberta today because its address is handwritten, Culture Alberta notes, so many voters may still need a second piece of ID showing where they live.

No bill has been tabled yet. Smith’s wording was “probably.”

Sources: The Canadian Press (via The Globe and Mail and CityNews); Culture Alberta; Elections Alberta

Rocky Mountain Icehouse pours its last drink on Jasper Avenue after 12 years, blaming rising costs and downtown’s struggles

The downtown Edmonton bar and restaurant at 10516 Jasper Ave. closed for good on Sunday, Nov. 22. It opened in 2014 and announced the closure late Friday on Facebook (Daily Hive, CTV News).

The owners said rising operating costs, ongoing problems in Edmonton’s downtown core and money pressures across the restaurant industry made it “no longer sustainable.” Staff are calling everyone with a booking, including Christmas parties, to move them to Lunar (Daily Hive, HighOnCity).

The bar sits in a 105-year-old heritage building. Its owners, Susan Forsey and Wayne Jones, also run Solar Brunch and The Cask & Barrel, which stay open (HighOnCity).

Why it matters: It’s the latest in a string of downtown closures, including Tiger Sugar’s downtown store this month, as council opens its budget debate.

Canada

More Canadians under 50 now use cannabis every day than drink alcohol every day, a new CMAJ study finds

The study, published today in the Canadian Medical Association Journal, used answers from almost 100,000 people in Statistics Canada’s 2023–24 Canadian Community Health Survey. It estimates about 1.8 million Canadians, or 5.7% of adults, use cannabis daily or almost daily, compared with 5.2% for alcohol (CMAJ, EurekAlert).

Adults 18 to 49 were more likely to use cannabis than to drink every day. For adults under 35, daily cannabis smoking is now about as common as daily cigarette smoking. From age 65, alcohol is the more common daily habit (The Canadian Press, EurekAlert).

Most daily users (68%) still smoke it rather than eat or vape it. About 41% of daily users said they started after legalization in 2018, including 85% of daily users aged 18 to 24 (EurekAlert, Medical Xpress).

Why it matters: Lead author André McDonald calls it a “generational shift,” and the researchers say public health efforts that focus mostly on alcohol and tobacco need to catch up.

Ottawa starts hiring the commissioners who will review possible wrongful convictions, almost two years after the law passed

Justice Minister Sean Fraser announced Monday that the federal government is recruiting a chief commissioner and four to eight commissioners for the new Miscarriage of Justice Review Commission. Each will serve up to seven years (APTN News, The Canadian Press via CityNews).

The commission will work independently from government, police, prosecutors and courts. It can’t overturn a conviction, but it can send a case back with a new trial or appeal. Courts will still decide guilt or innocence (APTN News).

Parliament passed the law, known as David and Joyce Milgaard’s Law, in December 2024. Until the commission is running, the justice minister’s current review system keeps handling cases. In 24 years it finished 236 applications and gave remedies in 36 cases (APTN News, Justice Canada).

Why it matters: Advocates say the current process is too slow and too small, and the new body is meant to make it easier, especially for Indigenous people, to get a wrongful conviction looked at again.

Sources (3)
  • APTN News: Canada begins recruiting for wrongful convictions review body
  • CityNews (The Canadian Press): Ottawa recruiting commissioners for wrongful-conviction review body
  • Justice Canada: Questions and answers on miscarriage of justice reviews
Community Note

No date has been given for when the commission will start taking cases.

The rules say at least a third of commissioners, including the chief, must be lawyers with 10 years of criminal-law experience.

Sources: APTN News; The Canadian Press; Justice Canada

Ukraine says seven sea drones sank Russia’s newest corvette, the Merkury, while it was docked in Libya

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Monday that Ukrainian forces carried out Sunday’s attack at the naval base in Benghazi, in eastern Libya. He said seven Ukrainian-made sea drones sank the Merkury and destroyed a Ka-27 helicopter on board, and that a second Russian ship, the cargo vessel MV Angara, was also hit (AP, ABC News, The Guardian).

Ukraine’s security service, the SBU, said the drones were launched from a “mother ship” and released video of them hitting the warship at its berth. Satellite images published after the attack show the corvette had sunk, with only its bow above water (The Independent).

Eastern Libya’s government said the ship had stopped to refuel and was heading home to Russia. It called the attack a violation of its sovereignty and opened a joint investigation with Russia. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov declined to comment (ABC News, AP).

Why it matters: The attack happened about 2,400 km from the front line, a sign the war at sea is spreading well beyond the Black Sea, a day after drones also set a Russian cargo ship on fire in the Gulf of Finland.

Armenia publishes a new draft constitution that drops a key line Azerbaijan objects to, and Pashinyan says he’ll resign if voters reject it

The draft, posted online Monday for public comment, removes the current constitution’s reference to Armenia’s 1990 Declaration of Independence. That declaration cites a resolution on “reunification” with Nagorno-Karabakh, and Azerbaijan says the line amounts to a territorial claim. Dropping it is one of Baku’s conditions for signing a peace treaty (Meduza, Türkiye Today).

In a televised address, Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan said Armenia would sign and ratify the peace deal once the new constitution is adopted. He said he would resign if voters rejected it, and would not run again for prime minister if they approved it (Public Radio of Armenia, Türkiye Today).

His Civil Contract party is six votes short of the 70 needed to call a referendum, and both opposition groups say they won’t help (Meduza, Public Radio of Armenia).

Why it matters: This could unlock a formal end to one of the longest conflicts left from the Soviet breakup, but only if Pashinyan can win over opposition MPs and then the public.

CrowdStrike agrees to buy Zscaler for about US$43.9 billion in stock, one of the biggest cybersecurity deals ever

Zscaler shareholders will get 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each share they own, worth about US$260.41, a 34% premium over Friday’s close. CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84% of the combined company (CrowdStrike, CNBC).

CrowdStrike, best known for protecting laptops and servers, gets Zscaler’s network security business. CEO George Kurtz will lead the combined company, and Zscaler CEO Jay Chaudhry will keep running the Zscaler business and join CrowdStrike’s board. The deal needs Zscaler shareholders and regulators to approve it (CrowdStrike, Bloomberg Law).

Before the opening bell, Zscaler shares jumped more than 13% and CrowdStrike’s fell more than 8%. Zscaler also reported first-quarter revenue up 20% to US$949.3 million (CNBC).

Why it matters: AI is helping attackers find weak spots faster, and companies want device and network protection from one supplier. The deal creates a company with more than US$10 billion in yearly recurring revenue.

Tata Steel will pay US$1.67 billion for a quarter of India’s biggest steel-pipe maker, and both companies’ shares tumble

Tata Steel said Monday it will buy a 26.4% stake in APL Apollo Tubes from its founders for 155.1 billion rupees, or 2,100 rupees a share. It will then offer to buy another 26% from other shareholders at 2,301.40 rupees, which would give it control with 52.38% (CNBC, Tata Steel, Moneycontrol).

First, Tata Steel will sell its own steel-tube business and Tata Steel Colors to APL Apollo for 280 billion rupees. The combined company plans to more than double its capacity to 15.5 million tonnes a year by 2035 (CNBC, Tata Steel).

Investors weren’t impressed: Tata Steel shares fell more than 9% in Mumbai, and APL Apollo’s dropped more than 19%. One analyst told CNBC the deal’s structure was “very complex” and gave minority shareholders little clarity.

Why it matters: Steel makers are moving into finished products like pipes and coated sheets, where profits are higher, and India’s building boom is the prize.

Science corner

Science corner: a superconductor sandwiched between two magnets becomes a memory cell that holds its bit with no power, a step toward super-cold computers that waste far less energy

Researchers at MIT’s Plasma Science and Fusion Center layered an ultrathin superconducting film between two magnetic layers. When the magnets point in opposite directions, current flows with no resistance. When they point the same way, the film resists. That difference is read as a 0 or a 1 (MIT News, Physical Review Letters).

To write a bit, a tiny electrical pulse warms one cell for an instant while a small magnetic field flips its magnet. Once set, the cell keeps its value without any power, and the team showed it could flip one cell without disturbing the one next door.

Colder still, the same sandwich works as a one-way valve for current, called a superconducting diode, so one device could both store bits and steer signals. “Integrating these devices can lead to processors operating at high speed and low energy,” says senior scientist Jagadeesh Moodera.

Why it matters: Superconducting computers could use much less power than today’s chips, which matters as AI data centres burn more electricity, but they’ve lacked good memory that works at very cold temperatures.

Sources (3)
  • MIT News: A tiny superconducting sandwich could help computers use less energy
  • Physical Review Letters: Magnetic exchange-coupled superconducting devices with broken symmetry for cryogenic memory
  • arXiv: Magnetic exchange coupled nonreciprocal devices for cryogenic memory (preprint)
Community Note

The cells only work when cooled to extreme cold, so this is for special computers, not phones or laptops.

It’s a single-cell lab demonstration. The next steps are making cells smaller, building arrays and measuring how much energy they really save.

This item is backed by MIT’s release, the peer-reviewed paper and its preprint. We didn’t find independent press coverage yet.

Sources: MIT News; Physical Review Letters; arXiv

AI news and advances

OpenAI’s chief scientist asks every AI lab to agree on a shared “speed limit” for their most powerful models, and Altman backs him

In an essay called “Perplexity and Intelligence,” published Sunday, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki proposed that labs measure progress with perplexity, a score of how well a model predicts the next piece of text, where lower is better. Labs would then agree not to pass set limits until tools to understand and monitor AI behaviour reach concrete milestones (OpenAI, TechCrunch).

OpenAI also published its own models’ scores for the first time. They fell from about 0.99 for GPT-3.5 to under 0.8 for its newest models, and Pachocki says each drop brought new skills, most recently in cybersecurity and math (The Next Web, TechCrunch).

CEO Sam Altman called it “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.” Pachocki didn’t say where the first limit should be set (TechCrunch, The Next Web).

Why it matters: If rival labs accepted a common yardstick, it could become the basis for slowing the race to build ever-stronger AI. It follows Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s call to “pace the frontier.”

Sources (4)

  • The Next Web: OpenAI’s chief scientist calls for industry-wide limits on AI models
  • The Indian Express: OpenAI’s chief scientist has a new way to track AI progress
Community Note

This is a proposal, not an agreement. No other lab has signed on.

Perplexity scores depend on the test text, so labs would also have to agree on how to measure them.

Sources: OpenAI; TechCrunch; The Next Web; The Indian Express

OpenRouter drops three AI hosting companies after researchers prove attackers were quietly copying customers’ prompts for months

The security firm Dragos sent about two million test prompts to AI services from July to November, each carrying a unique fake web link or cloud password. When someone used one, it proved they had read the prompt. Attackers were caught inside three hosting providers, the earliest since mid-July, and none of the three knew (Dragos research reported by The Hacker News and The Next Web).

OpenRouter, a service that sends customers’ AI requests to many providers, said it removed three providers, took 125 endpoints off its “Zero Data Retention” list and told affected customers. It said its own systems weren’t breached (OpenRouter, The Next Web).

Dragos also found a Chinese AI lab opening secret links from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds marked as not to be stored (The Hacker News, The Next Web).

Why it matters: People paste passwords, code and private documents into AI tools, and this shows a “don’t store my data” setting is only as good as every company in the chain.

Sources (3)
  • OpenRouter: Data processing incidents on inference providers
  • The Hacker News: OpenRouter drops 3 providers after researchers catch months of prompt theft
  • The Next Web: OpenRouter drops three AI providers after Dragos catches prompt theft
Community Note

OpenRouter hasn’t named the three providers it removed. Some of the 125 endpoints were pulled as a precaution, not because they were caught.

Dragos’s advice is to run models in-house where you can, or to go straight to a major provider.

Sources: OpenRouter; The Hacker News; The Next Web
