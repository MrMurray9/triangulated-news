Alberta

Alberta tables Bill 40 to lock in its 91-seat riding map, and the NDP promises a court fight if it passes

Justice Minister Mickey Amery introduced the Electoral Divisions Act in the legislature on Monday. It would replace today’s 87 ridings with the 91-seat map drawn by a government-appointed advisory panel, which mixes some Calgary and Lethbridge neighbourhoods with nearby rural areas in “hybrid” ridings. The new map would take effect for the election set for Oct. 18, 2027 (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail, The Canadian Press via Global News).

NDP Leader Naheed Nenshi called it an attempt to “rig the election” and said his party will go to court once the bill gets royal assent. Amery called that “sour grapes.” The government threw out an independent commission’s 89-seat map in April. Alberta’s chief electoral officer warned in October that elections normally take 12 to 18 months to plan, but Elections Alberta told a committee on Nov. 5 it can run the vote on the new lines (Edmonton Journal, Global News).

Why it matters: Riding lines decide how much each vote counts, and a court challenge could leave it unclear which map is used less than a year before Albertans vote.

TC Energy proposes a 370-km gas pipeline across Alberta to feed the oilsands, new power plants and data centres

Calgary’s TC Energy announced the Alberta Growth Connector on Monday: a 48-inch pipe running roughly 370 kilometres west to east that would add about one billion cubic feet a day of natural gas capacity toward the oilsands in the province’s northeast. It’s part of a wider plan to grow TC’s NOVA Gas Transmission (NGTL) network, which has run across Alberta and into northeastern B.C. for almost 70 years (TC Energy, The Canadian Press via Global News, Gas Processing & LNG).

TC now expects demand on the system to climb about a quarter, to 18 billion cubic feet a day by 2031, with more than 60% of the growth coming from the oilsands, power generation and data centres. The project still needs customer contracts, consultation with Indigenous rightsholders and landowners, regulatory approval and the company’s own go-ahead (Global News, Boursorama/Reuters).

Why it matters: Alberta wants to double oil output and lure data centres that must make their own power, and both plans depend on moving a lot more gas around the province.

Canada

Canada’s inflation stays at 3.1% in October as gas and grocery prices climb, but cheaper phone plans help

Statistics Canada said Monday that consumer prices were 3.1% higher than a year earlier in October, the same as September and a bit under the 3.2% economists expected. Gasoline was up 30.9% from a year ago, partly because prices had dropped sharply in October 2025, though gas actually fell 2.5% from September (Statistics Canada, The Canadian Press via BNN Bloomberg, Reuters).

Leave out energy and inflation was 2.0%. Grocery prices rose 2% after four months of slowing, and clothing jumped 3.4% in one month, the most since 2019. Cellphone plans were 6.2% cheaper than a year ago and rent growth slowed, especially in B.C. BMO’s Benjamin Reitzes said nothing in the numbers “really screams rate hike” for the Bank of Canada in December (BNN Bloomberg, Statistics Canada).

Why it matters: The Bank of Canada raised rates in October, and this report makes another hike in December less certain, which matters for anyone with a mortgage renewal or variable-rate loan.

Ukrainian drones hit the bridge linking Russia to Crimea during a barrage of more than 1,300 drones

Russia’s defence ministry said Ukraine fired 1,336 drones overnight into Monday, and Crimea’s Russian-installed governor said one hit the Kerch Strait bridge, Russia’s road and rail link to the occupied peninsula. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the damage “minor” and said traffic kept moving, while admitting “no system in the world can provide 100 percent protection” against strikes this big (AFP, The Guardian, Reuters).

Moscow’s mayor said about 600 drones were shot down heading for the capital. Ukraine says the strikes answer Russia’s attacks on its cities and power grid; Russian strikes left 50,000 people without power in the Odesa region on Monday. The attack comes three days before Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin meet in Shenzhen (AFP, Reuters).

Why it matters: The bridge is Russia’s main supply route to Crimea and a symbol of its 2014 annexation, and the strike shows how far Ukraine’s drones can now reach.

Business

Quebecor bids $31 billion for Telus, and Telus says the deal has “serious” regulatory problems

Quebecor, owner of Videotron and Freedom Mobile, said Monday it has offered Telus shareholders $6.35 in cash plus 0.17 of a Quebecor share for each Telus share, about $19.45 a share or a 60% premium to Friday’s close. It first approached Telus in June and sent an improved offer on Nov. 9. Telus shares jumped about 13% to $13.70, while Quebecor’s fell about 15% (Quebecor, The Canadian Press via BNN Bloomberg, The Globe and Mail).

Telus set up a committee of independent directors to study the bid but said it carries “significant regulatory and execution risks” and doesn’t reflect the company’s value. CEO Victor Dodig said “every single point of their offer has serious complications.” Quebecor CEO Pierre Karl Péladeau says the deal is about savings and investment, not higher prices. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said the Competition Bureau and CRTC would review any deal “rigorously” (Telus, BNN Bloomberg, Mobile World Live, Government of Canada).

Why it matters: A merger would cut Canada’s big wireless carriers from four to three, and Freedom was handed to Quebecor in 2023 specifically to keep a fourth competitor.

Science corner

Science corner: UCLA puts tiny lids on cell-sized “test tubes,” so hundreds of thousands of single-cell experiments can run at once

Tonight’s technology is the capped nanovial. Nanovials are bowl-shaped gel particles, each small enough to hold one or a few cells, that UCLA bioengineers already use to catch cells and measure what they release. In a study in Science Advances, Dino Di Carlo’s team added gel caps that snap on with a “molecular Velcro” of vitamin B7 and a bacterial protein. Spin the bowls and caps together in a centrifuge and up to nine in ten end up sealed (UCLA, Science Advances, Scientific Frontline).

The lids are porous, so food and waste pass through while the cells stay put. That let the team trap bacteria and yeast that normally float away, grow tiny colonies for hours or days, and sort them by how fast they grew. They also paired antibody-making cells with T cells that glow green when an antibody works, a way to spot useful drug candidates. The particles run on equipment many biology labs already own (UCLA, Scientific Frontline).

Why it matters: Running huge numbers of tiny experiments cheaply could speed up drug discovery and produce the large data sets AI models need to predict how cells behave.

Sources (3)
  • UCLA: Scientists put a lid on tiny test tubes, opening new frontiers for cell research
  • Science Advances: Massively parallel self-assembly of suspendable microcompartments for functional cell screening
  • Scientific Frontline: Capped nanovials expand cell research capabilities
Community Note

Di Carlo co-founded Partillion Bioscience, the company that sells nanovials.

Coverage so far comes from the university, the peer-reviewed journal and a science news site, not from independent experts.

AI news and advances

The U.S. moves to stop Chinese companies renting top AI chips from data centres in Southeast Asia and Brazil

The U.S. Commerce Department issued a rule on Monday to close a loophole that let firms in China, Russia and Iran use restricted American AI chips without buying them, by renting computing time from data centres abroad. Companies in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil will need a licence, strict customer checks and monthly reports on who’s using high volumes of advanced chips. It takes effect 90 days after it appears in the Federal Register (AP, Ars Technica, The Hill).

The move follows reports that Singapore data centre firm Megaspeed became a major Nvidia customer and moved restricted chips toward China, which Megaspeed denies. Nvidia shares fell 4.1% on Monday. President Trump said he doesn’t expect China to retaliate, days before he meets Xi Jinping in Shenzhen (Ars Technica, CNBC).

Why it matters: Renting computing power abroad has been one of the main ways Chinese labs could train advanced AI despite U.S. export bans, and closing it could push China to rely more on its own chips.
