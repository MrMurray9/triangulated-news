Alberta

An NDP private member’s bill would ban Alberta doctors from billing medicare and charging patients privately at the same time

Sarah Hoffman, the NDP’s critic for hospital and surgical health services, introduced Bill 214, the Alberta Health Care Insurance (Protecting Public Health Care) Act, in the legislature on Nov. 5. It would amend the Alberta Health Care Insurance Act to undo the government’s move to let physicians work in the public system and a private, patient-pay system at once. Doctors could still opt out of medicare entirely, but they couldn’t pick case by case who pays (Red Deer Advocate, Insurance Business, Legislative Assembly of Alberta).

“The UCP’s American-style, dual-practice system undermines equitable access to care and creates a two-tier system where people who can afford to pay can move to the front of the line,” Hoffman said. Supporters at the launch included Canadian Doctors for Medicare, the Alberta Union of Provincial Employees and Friends of Medicare. The bill passed first reading but hasn’t had a second-reading vote, and the NDP has been pitching it this week as the legislature sits (Red Deer Advocate, Medicine Hat News, Legislative Assembly of Alberta).

Why it matters: Alberta is the only province that lets doctors work both sides at once, and whether it stays that way decides how wait lists work for patients who can’t pay out of pocket.

Canada

Carney calls Stellantis’s sale of its idle Brampton car plant to armoured-vehicle maker Roshel a “betrayal” and vows to claw back public money

Stellantis confirmed on Monday that it sold its Brampton, Ont., assembly plant to Roshel, a Brampton-based maker of armoured vehicles, for an undisclosed price. The plant stopped making cars in late 2023 to retool for a new vehicle, but that work was paused in 2025 after U.S. tariffs on Canadian-made cars, and the planned Jeep Compass moved to Illinois. About 2,200 workers are still on layoff (The Globe and Mail, CP24, The Trillium).

“It’s a betrayal of the workers as it is a betrayal of the obligations to Canada and by extension to all Canadians,” Prime Minister Mark Carney said in Montreal on Tuesday. “We will fully enforce our rights.” Ottawa agreed in 2022 to put up to $529 million toward retooling Stellantis plants in Brampton and Windsor. Stellantis says it has offered to repay the support for Brampton but won’t say how much. Roshel’s CEO, Roman Shimonov, said his company will give Brampton residents priority but won’t take on Stellantis’s commitments to its unionized workers. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly meets Unifor, Stellantis and Ontario officials in Toronto on Thursday (The Globe and Mail, CP24).

Why it matters: It’s one of the biggest job losses from the U.S. tariff fight, and how hard Ottawa pushes will show whether its auto subsidies come with conditions it can actually enforce.

Roshel says it will create “thousands” of jobs at the site but hasn’t given numbers or timelines. Parts-industry head Flavio Volpe told CTV that an armoured-vehicle plant would employ a fraction of what car assembly did.

Thick fog sets off pile-ups involving about 86 vehicles on Bulgaria’s main Sofia–Burgas motorway, killing 11 people

The crashes began around 7:10 a.m. local time on Tuesday on both sides of the Trakia motorway near Yambol, in southeastern Bulgaria. Officials say a bus and a truck collided in dense fog, with visibility down to about 30 metres, and dozens of cars and trucks piled in behind them. Rescuers had to cut people out of crushed vehicles, including tanker trucks (Euronews, DW, BTA).

Authorities first gave a toll of 12 dead, and the Health Ministry later corrected it to 11. About 50 people were taken to hospitals in Yambol and Sliven, and 13 are seriously hurt. The state news agency BTA reports that 86 vehicles were involved in all and that a truck driver has been detained over one of the pile-ups. Prime Minister Rumen Radev cancelled his schedule, and the motorway was closed in both directions (BTA, Xinhua, Euronews).

Why it matters: Bulgaria already has the highest road death rate in the European Union, and this is one of the deadliest crashes in its recent history.

The U.S. House votes 211–204 to shelve Thomas Massie’s bid to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth

The House voted Tuesday to send the impeachment resolution to the Judiciary Committee, which effectively kills it. Massie, a Kentucky Republican who lost his primary this year and leaves Congress in January, was the only Republican to side with Democrats. His eight articles accused Hegseth of waging the war with Iran without approval from Congress, ignoring rules meant to limit civilian deaths, ordering deadly strikes on suspected drug boats, the seizure of Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro, and retaliating against Sen. Mark Kelly (NBC News, CBS News, U.S. House Clerk).

Hegseth called the effort “a farce” and “a stunt” at a Pentagon news conference that morning, and President Donald Trump called the vote an “EXONERATION.” Several Republicans who have criticized Hegseth said his conduct didn’t rise to “high crimes and misdemeanors.” Democrats, who take control of the House in January, say it isn’t over. “This is not the end of it. It’s just the beginning,” said House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (CBS News, NBC News).

Why it matters: Hegseth is safe for now, but with Democrats taking over Congress in January, the fight over how the U.S. has run its wars is likely to come back.

Science corner

Science corner: a Caltech robot puts together part of an antenna frame in orbit, the first structure built in space by a machine

ASTRA, short for Autonomous In-Space Tensioned Reflector Assembly, is a box about one cubic metre in size holding a robotic arm, dispensers full of carbon-composite struts and aluminum joints, and an onboard computer. It launched in March on a SpaceX rideshare mission. In orbit it unfolded two sections of truss built on the ground, then picked parts out of its dispensers and snapped together a third section on its own (Caltech, Interesting Engineering, New Atlas).

Each finished section gets a reflective sheet of perforated Kapton film, co-developed with the University of Pennsylvania, and is pushed outward so the arm can start the next one. The full design calls for 11 sections forming a 1.4-metre antenna, but limited contact time meant only one new section was built. Ground teams got about 15-minute windows a few times a day, so they tested fixes on a spare unit in the lab first. The work is funded by DARPA’s NOM4D program, which wants to eventually build structures hundreds of metres or even kilometres wide in orbit (DARPA, Space.com, Interesting Engineering).

Why it matters: Anything launched today has to fold up to fit inside a rocket. Robots that build in orbit could make much bigger telescopes, antennas and solar arrays possible.

Sources (5)

  • DARPA: NOM4D program demonstrates automated in-space assembly
  • Interesting Engineering: US robot builds space structure without human assistance for first time

“Autonomous” here means the robot did the assembly itself. Engineers on the ground still checked photos and sent revised commands between steps, as New Atlas points out.

Robots have helped assemble things in orbit before, such as Canada’s Canadarm2 on the space station, but those joined parts built on Earth rather than building a structure piece by piece.

AI news and advances

Two more custom AI chips in one year: TPU 8tx keeps data encrypted while it’s being processed, and TPU 8i Max doubles up for huge prompts

Google added two systems to its eighth generation of Tensor Processing Units, its in-house AI chips, on Monday. TPU 8tx uses the same silicon as the training-focused TPU 8t, paired with AMD EPYC processors, and adds hardware-level confidential computing, so sensitive data and model weights stay protected even while the chip is working on them. Google says it’s aimed at governments and regulated businesses, and that some customers will be allowed to install it in their own data centres (Google, Data Center Knowledge, The Verge).

TPU 8i Max puts two TPU 8i inference chips in one package, doubling compute, on-chip memory and memory bandwidth, with 384 GB of high-bandwidth memory. It’s meant for AI agents working through very long inputs, such as a whole codebase or hours of video, and fits twice the silicon in the same rack space. Google didn’t give pricing or availability dates. IDC analyst Dave McCarthy said protecting data while it’s in use could give Google “a little bit of an edge” over rivals (Data Center Knowledge, MarkTechPost, Google).

Why it matters: Hospitals, banks and governments are wary of sending private data to AI in the cloud, and chips that keep it encrypted during processing remove one of their biggest objections.

Sources (4)

  • Data Center Knowledge: Google adds TPU 8tx, 8i Max for enterprise AI
  • The Verge: Google announced two new versions of its eighth-gen AI chips
  • MarkTechPost: Google unveils TPU 8i Max and TPU 8tx

These are new system configurations of chips announced in April, not brand-new chip designs, as Google itself explains.

XPunch, the orphaned zoo monkey who once clung to a stuffed orangutan, is now filmed gently watching over a newborn

Seen on: X, in a Nov. 9 post by @Melissa_WongMT and a Nov. 8 keeper video from the zoo’s official account, @ichikawa_zoo, per India Today and NDTV.

Punch is a Japanese macaque at Ichikawa City Zoo, near Tokyo. He became famous online in February, when photos showed the tiny monkey, rejected by his mother, carrying a big orange plush orangutan everywhere. Staff had given him the toy for comfort and to help him build the strength to cling, as baby macaques do to their mothers (India Today, NDTV, Moneycontrol).

A new clip shared on X on Nov. 9 shows a grown-up Punch slowly approaching a newborn monkey, lying down beside it and keeping watch. “Punch is all grown up now and has taken on a little one and watches out for him,” the post reads. A day earlier, the zoo posted its own keeper video of Punch playing happily with the troop and thanked its staff for his healthy, lively life (India Today, NDTV).

Why it matters: The once-bullied baby the internet worried about is now a settled member of his group, which is about as happy an ending as a viral animal story gets.
