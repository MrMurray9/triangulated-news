Alberta

MLAs return Tuesday to about 14 new bills — cheaper auto insurance, ticket-resale rules and limits on AI data centres among them

Government House Leader Joseph Schow said Friday the United Conservative government plans to table “13 or 14” bills in the fall sitting, which opens Tuesday, Oct. 27, and is scheduled to run 19 sitting days to Dec. 3 (Edmonton Journal, The Canadian Press via CityNews, Daily Hive).

The list includes bills to lower auto-insurance costs and protect people on their utility bills, consumer protections for resale event tickets and defective used vehicles, new rules for developing artificial-intelligence data centres, standard student report cards, public-sector hiring based on “merit,” tougher penalties for illegal dumping and roadway rules that cover the future of bike lanes. Schow said details would come from each ministry as bills are tabled. The Opposition NDP said the plan misses affordability and health care.

Why it matters: It is the government’s last fall sitting before the October 2027 election, so these bills show what it plans to campaign on — and several, like ticket resale and insurance, hit household budgets directly.

  • Daily Hive — Alberta eyes new legislation targeting resale tickets
Community Note

These are announced plans, not tabled bills: none of the wording, costs or penalties is public yet, and bills can change or die before third reading. The government said it isn’t bringing in new immigration laws this sitting.

From Jan. 4, every Alberta school must fly the Canada and Alberta flags — and other flags will need a specific exemption or ministry approval

The province published its Flag Display Regulation on Friday. Starting Jan. 4, 2027, public, separate, francophone, charter and accredited independent schools must fly the Canadian and Alberta flags on separate poles during the school year. Other flags are allowed only in listed cases or with written approval from the Ministry of Education and Childcare (Government of Alberta, Discover Airdrie, Todayville).

Allowed without approval: Alberta emblems, municipal flags, the Franco-Albertan flag, the Vatican City flag and sports-team flags. First Nations and Métis flags may go up inside schools around National Indigenous Peoples Day, Truth and Reconciliation Day and Louis Riel Day, and during related lessons, visits or ceremonies, for up to 14 school days. Requested flags must not be a “political, social or ideological statement.” The rules also cover school websites and social media, but not flags on students’ own clothing or belongings.

Why it matters: It turns this spring’s school “neutrality” law into concrete rules every school board must follow, and it settles which flags students will and won’t see at school.

Sources (3)
  • Discover Airdrie — New rules for school flag displays take effect in January
  • Todayville — Province clarifies flag regulations on school properties
  • Alberta Teachers’ Association — Neutrality legislation in force, flag policy delayed
Community Note

Education Minister Demetrios Nicolaides says the flags “unite us”; NDP education critic Amanda Chapman called it a distraction from crowded classrooms. The regulation expires May 29, 2029, unless renewed after a review. Pride flags are not on the permitted list, so schools would have to apply.

Sources: Government of Alberta via Discover Airdrie; Todayville; Alberta Teachers’ Association.

Canada

About 120,000 federal public servants will vote on whether to strike, starting Nov. 16

The Public Service Alliance of Canada says members of its three largest Treasury Board units — the PA, TC and EB groups — will hold strike votes from Nov. 16 to Jan. 22. The union says the government’s offer averages raises of less than one per cent a year and refuses to bargain on remote work, job security and protections against artificial intelligence (PSAC, The Globe and Mail, Ottawa Citizen).

“The government has left us no other choice,” said PSAC national president Sharon DeSousa, while calling a strike a last resort. The votes come as Ottawa moves to shrink the public service by about 10 per cent by 2028–29; Parks Canada says 2,917 employees are getting notices that their jobs are affected.

Why it matters: A walkout by this many workers would slow passports, tax returns, benefits and border services across the country, including in Edmonton.

Ottawa says its plan to keep kids under 16 off social media fits the Charter — while admitting it limits free expression

In a Charter statement tabled for Bill C-34, the proposed Digital Safety Act, the Justice Department says the under-16 ban on social-media accounts touches freedom of expression, equality and privacy, but is consistent with the Charter because of the risks platforms pose to young people — from content that can never be erased to grooming and cyberbullying (Justice Canada, The Canadian Press, Michael Geist).

Young people could still browse some content without an account, and platforms could be exempted if they show adequate safeguards. Legal experts, including Dalhousie’s Michael Karanicolas and University of Ottawa law professor Michael Geist, say a court challenge is close to certain. The bill was set for second-reading debate this week; it would still need committee study and Senate approval.

Why it matters: If it passes, millions of Canadian teenagers would lose their accounts — and how platforms check ages could affect adults too.

Sources (3)

  • Michael Geist — What the Bill C-34 Charter statement reveals
Community Note

A Charter statement is the government’s own view, not a court ruling. The statement was released earlier this week; it is new to this site. Geist is a critic of the bill, so his analysis is opinion.

Sources: Justice Canada; The Canadian Press; Michael Geist.

Gunmen open fire on a funeral inside a Greek Orthodox church in southern Jordan, killing seven

Three attackers arrived by car at a church in the village of Hmoud, in Karak governorate about 180 km south of Amman, during a funeral on Friday and opened fire before fleeing. Jordan’s police chief, Maj. Gen. Obaidullah Al-Maaytah, said seven people were killed and 34 injured; AP, citing authorities, put the injured at 40 (Petra, AP, Al Jazeera, The Times of Israel).

Interior Minister Mazen Al-Faraya called it a terrorist attack by “a group of extremists” and said the attackers would be caught soon. King Abdullah II vowed “the harshest” response. No group has claimed responsibility, and Amman’s prosecutor general has banned media from publishing details of the investigation.

Why it matters: Attacks on Jordan’s Christian minority are very rare, and the country is a key Western ally in a tense region.

Russia and Ukraine agree to stop shooting in one occupied town, Oleshky, so aid can get in and residents can leave

The White House said Friday that first lady Melania Trump brokered a local ceasefire for Oleshky, a Russian-occupied town across the Dnipro River from Kherson, so humanitarian aid can be delivered and civilians evacuated along a safe corridor. About 1,700 to 2,000 people remain, mostly elderly, down from about 24,000 before the war (White House, AP, RTÉ/Reuters).

Start dates differ: the White House said Oct. 29, while Russia’s army chief Valery Gerasimov and Ukraine’s General Staff said fighting in the area would stop early on Oct. 27. Ukraine’s human-rights ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets said the mission has stages — demining roads, delivering food, then letting people leave to Russia or Ukraine as they choose. Separately, the two countries swapped 270 prisoners each.

Why it matters: It is a small, local truce, but one of the few deals both sides have signed onto — and residents there have reported a deepening food crisis.

American Express will cut about 3,500 jobs, 5% of its staff, even as it raises its profit forecast

AmEx reported third-quarter revenue of US$20.1 billion, up 9 per cent, and adjusted earnings of $4.68 a share, ahead of analysts’ estimates. Cardholder spending rose 8 per cent to $454 billion, with travel and entertainment up 10 per cent. It raised its full-year forecast to $17.60–$17.90 a share (Reuters via Investing.com, Investing.com, 24/7 Wall St.).

It also announced a restructuring that eliminates about 5 per cent of its global workforce. CEO Stephen Squeri said the company needs to be more “agile and efficient.” Shares fell roughly 3 per cent in morning trading, with investors pointing to expenses up 9 per cent and revenue just short of forecasts.

Why it matters: AmEx’s wealthy customers are still spending freely — a contrast with lower-income households — while big companies keep trimming jobs in the name of efficiency and AI.

Rogers earns $482 million but its wireless revenue slips after Ottawa’s ban on some fees — and its stock drops

Rogers Communications reported third-quarter revenue of $5.44 billion, up 2 per cent, and net income of $482 million, or 88 cents a share; adjusted earnings of $1.29 a share beat estimates. Free cash flow jumped 65 per cent to $1.37 billion as capital spending fell (Rogers, The Globe and Mail, BNN Bloomberg).

Wireless service revenue fell 1 per cent to $2.03 billion, and average monthly revenue per phone customer dropped to $55.22, which Rogers tied to price competition and the CRTC’s ban on certain fees. It added 17,000 mobile customers, 6,000 of them postpaid. Shares fell more than 3 per cent (Investing.com, BNN Bloomberg).

Why it matters: Falling revenue per customer is good news for phone bills: Canada’s carriers are competing harder on price.

Science corner

Gene editing’s precision tool is used inside a patient for the first time — rewriting liver DNA to treat a copper disorder

Prime editing is a newer, more precise form of CRISPR that can insert, delete or rewrite short stretches of DNA instead of just cutting a gene. Prime Medicine says it has dosed the first person in a trial of PM577a for Wilson’s disease, delivering the editor in fat-based nanoparticles straight to the liver — the first known use of prime editing inside the human body (Nature, The Independent, Prime Medicine).

Wilson’s disease comes from faulty copies of the ATP7B gene, so the liver can’t clear copper; it builds up and damages the liver and nervous system. The treatment aims to fix the most common mutation, H1069Q, in one dose, in place of daily copper-clearing drugs for life. Earlier prime-editing work edited blood cells outside the body and put them back in. First results are expected next year.

Why it matters: Editing genes inside the body could make cures cheaper and faster than custom-made cell therapies — a path to treating many rare inherited diseases.

Sources (3)
  • Nature — Prime editing alters DNA directly in the human body
  • The Independent — Scientists edit human DNA inside the body for the first time
  • Prime Medicine — First patient dosed in phase 1/2 trial of PM577a
Community Note

This is an early safety trial with no results yet; we don’t know if it works or is safe long-term. Only one gene-editing therapy (Casgevy) is approved, and none for use inside the body. The first dose was announced Oct. 5; Nature’s report this week is why it is in the news now.

Sources: Nature; The Independent; Prime Medicine.

AI news and advances

A start-up’s new open coding AI runs on a laptop — and the company says its own AI system did much of the building

Recursive has released Flint, an open-weight coding model with about 40 billion parameters, of which only about 2.5 billion are active for each word it writes, so it runs on a well-equipped laptop (around 48 GB of memory). It is built to read code, fix bugs, add features and write tests (TechCrunch, The Decoder, The Next Web).

Recursive says its automated research system, the “Eureka Machine,” handled much of the work — model design, training, compression and speed tuning — letting a small team finish in under four months. The weights are free to download on Hugging Face.

Why it matters: Coding help that runs on your own machine keeps code private and avoids cloud fees — and “AI building AI” is the claim every big lab is chasing.

Sources (3)

  • The Decoder — Recursive shows first result of its AI-driven research process
  • The Next Web — Recursive releases Flint
Community Note

Flint’s benchmark scores come from Recursive’s own tests and haven’t been independently checked, and how much the “Eureka Machine” really did can’t be verified from outside. Released Oct. 21.

Sources: TechCrunch; The Decoder; The Next Web.

OSix newborn puppies napping on a 24/7 livestream are Finland’s biggest TV hit — and viewers get to name them Saturday

Seen on: Other — Yle Areena livestream and the Yle app, as reported by AP and The Guardian

Finnish public broadcaster Yle’s Pentulive (“Puppy Live”) streams a lagotto romagnolo named Hilkka and her six puppies, born Oct. 10, around the clock for eight weeks. In its first two weeks it has been watched for more than a million hours by 1.85 million unique viewers, and it is on track to break the show’s records (AP, The Guardian, Yle).

There are evening chats with the breeder, a podcast and alerts for milestones like weigh-ins and first nail trims. On Saturday, the four males and two females will be named after a viewer vote. The stream is also airing in Sweden, and the format has been sold to the Netherlands. In mid-December the puppies leave for their new families.

Why it matters: In a heavy news week, millions are choosing to unwind by watching puppies sleep — and it’s free to watch from Canada.
