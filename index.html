Alberta

Alberta will charge one education property tax rate everywhere and freeze it for three years, trimming about $99 million

Starting with Budget 2027, Alberta will replace its town-by-town education property tax calculation with a single provincial rate: $2.65 per $1,000 of assessed value for homes and farmland, and about $3.98 for non-residential property. The rate is locked for three years, then reset and frozen again (Government of Alberta, CochraneNow, LiveWire Calgary).

The province expects to collect about $98.7 million less in 2027-28. Where the new rate is higher than a community’s current one, a one-time transition credit applies in 2027. Calgary figures suggest a $700,000 home would save about $70 a year (LiveWire Calgary, Red Deer Advocate, CochraneNow).

Why it matters: Education tax is the provincial slice of every property tax bill in Edmonton, so the change shows up on homeowners’ statements next year.

Sources (4)

  • CochraneNow — Province plans new education property tax calculation, projects $98.7M in savings
  • LiveWire Calgary — Province sets, freezes municipal education property tax rate
  • Red Deer Advocate — Alberta freezes school taxes for three years
Community Note

The freeze applies to the rate, not your bill: if your assessment rises, you can still pay more. City property taxes are set separately and are not frozen. Finance Minister Jason Nixon did not rule out higher rates after the three years.

Sources: Government of Alberta; CochraneNow; LiveWire Calgary; Red Deer Advocate.

Tearing out Edmonton’s bike lanes would cost $150 million to $170 million, city staff estimate, as a provincial bill looms

An internal city email to council puts the cost of removing bike lanes and active-transportation routes that took away a traffic lane at roughly $150 million to $170 million. The single biggest piece is 132 Avenue, which was fully rebuilt and would cost $100 million to $120 million to undo (CTV News Edmonton, Taproot Edmonton, Canadian Cycling Magazine).

Transportation Minister Devin Dreeshen has said a bike-lane bill is coming this fall, but not whether it would force existing lanes out. Coun. Thu Parmar says the bill would equal about a 6.8 per cent property tax hike, and Mayor Andrew Knack opposes removing finished routes (CTV News Edmonton, Edmonton Journal, Taproot Edmonton).

Why it matters: If the province orders removals, Edmontonians would pay twice: once to build the lanes and again to rip them out.

Canada

Ottawa fast-tracks a $5.9-billion container terminal at the Port of Vancouver, aiming to open it four years sooner

The federal government has listed Roberts Bank Terminal 2 in Delta, B.C., as a project of national interest, giving it up-front certainty on the remaining federal permits. The three-berth terminal on a new artificial island would add 2.4 million containers a year, about 50 per cent more container capacity at Canada’s largest port (Transport Canada, Vancouver Sun, Canada News Media).

Ottawa says the move could pull the opening forward from 2039 to 2035, with a final investment decision by mid-2027. Environmental group Ecojustice condemned the listing, saying it forces permits through ahead of a full review of the risks (Transport Canada, Vancouver Sun, Ecojustice).

Why it matters: Prairie exporters, including Alberta’s, rely on West Coast ports to reach Asia, and congestion there has cost them before.

Supreme Court orders a new trial in the crash that killed a Vancouver toddler, ruling a moment of inattention can be dangerous driving

In a 5-4 decision, the Supreme Court of Canada ordered a new trial for Seyed Moshfeghi Zadeh, whose SUV ran a red light in downtown Vancouver in July 2021, was hit, and flipped onto a sidewalk, killing 18-month-old Ocean Oshi Noelle and badly injuring her father. He had been acquitted of dangerous driving (Supreme Court of Canada, Canadian Lawyer, Global News).

Justice Michelle O’Bonsawin wrote that the trial judge wrongly treated a three-to-five-second lapse as close to automatically non-criminal; the length of inattention is “a factor, not the focus,” and a busy downtown street and a long-visible red light matter too. Justice Suzanne Côté and three others dissented (Canadian Lawyer, The Canadian Press via CTV News, Supreme Court of Canada).

Why it matters: The ruling sets the test for every dangerous-driving case in Canada, including the ones that start with someone glancing at a phone.

Congo’s Ebola outbreak passes 5,000 deaths, the second-largest on record, with no approved vaccine for this strain

Government figures released Friday show more than 5,000 people have died and more than 10,000 have been infected since the outbreak was declared in May. It has reached eight of Congo’s 26 provinces, and Uganda, Kenya and, last week, Nigeria have reported cases (AP, Reuters via Arab News, Al Jazeera).

The Bundibugyo strain behind it has no approved vaccine or treatment. Only the 2014-16 West African epidemic, which killed more than 11,000, was larger. Saudi Arabia on Friday barred travel to and from Kenya (Reuters via Al Jazeera, AP, Health Policy Watch).

Why it matters: Cases in Kenya and Nigeria mean the outbreak now touches major air hubs, which is why health agencies worldwide are watching it closely.

The UN’s Western Sahara mission loses its mandate at midnight after the U.S. and Russia block each other’s plans

The Security Council rejected two rival drafts on Friday. Russia vetoed a U.S. text that had 11 votes in favour, one against and three abstentions. Russia’s own extension then drew only three votes in favour, with the U.S. against and 11 abstaining (UN Meetings Coverage, AP, DW).

With no renewal, the mandate of MINURSO, in place since 1991, runs out on Oct. 31. Morocco and the Polisario Front, which wants independence for the territory, have long disputed it (UN Meetings Coverage, DW, Morocco World News).

Why it matters: Peacekeepers have watched the ceasefire line in the disputed desert territory for 35 years; nobody has said what replaces them.

Exxon and Chevron shares hit all-time highs as tight diesel supply swells refining profits

ExxonMobil earned US$15.4 billion in the third quarter and Chevron US$9.9 billion, both beating analysts’ forecasts. Exxon’s Gulf Coast refineries ran at full capacity and Chevron’s U.S. plants at 98 per cent to meet tight global diesel demand (CNBC, Reuters via Investing.com, AP).

Chevron’s refining profit jumped more than 300 per cent to US$4.9 billion. U.S. diesel averages US$6.32 a gallon, up more than 70 per cent in a year. Both stocks touched all-time highs on a day the wider market fell (CNBC, AP, Reuters).

Why it matters: The same diesel squeeze shows up in trucking, farming and grocery costs here; Brent crude closed October above US$117 a barrel.

Science corner

Science corner: a robot moth with artificial blood grows a brand-new light sensor where UV light hits its wing

Engineers at the University of Tartu in Estonia and the Italian Institute of Technology 3D-printed a clear plastic robot shaped like a blue underwing moth and pumped about 3 millilitres of “artificial hemolymph” through veins in its wings. The fluid carries pyrrole, a chemical building block, and a light-activated starter (Advanced Science, Tech Xplore, Reflect.ee).

Wherever UV light shone on the body for about a minute, the pyrrole soaked into the plastic turned into polypyrrole, a black, electrically conductive polymer that works as a UV detector. A robot that had no way to sense UV could then flap its wings or flash an LED when the light returned. Unexpectedly, the new sensor grew more sensitive with each exposure, a kind of built-in memory, and still worked after four months (Tech Xplore, Reflect.ee).

Why it matters: Robots sent into collapsed buildings, forests or other planets usually carry only the sensors someone guessed they would need. Growing parts on the spot could let a machine adapt to places nobody could map ahead of time.

Sources (4)
  • Advanced Science — Receptogenesis in a Vascularized Robotic Embodiment
  • Tech Xplore — Robot moth grows a UV sensor by using an artificial circulatory system
  • Reflect.ee — Tartu researchers’ robotic butterfly grew a new sense organ inside its wing
  • arXiv — Receptogenesis in a Vascularized Robotic Embodiment (preprint)
Community Note

This is a proof of concept: one chemical recipe, an irreversible reaction, and a stencil chose where the sensor formed. The authors say robots that decide for themselves what to grow are still a long way off. Tech Xplore’s piece is based on the Estonian Research Council’s release; the peer-reviewed paper is in Advanced Science.

Sources: Advanced Science (peer-reviewed); Tech Xplore; Reflect.ee.

AI news and advances

Google’s first data-centre satellite runs an AI model in orbit, and its first message home is a haiku

A Project Suncatcher prototype launched this month on a SpaceX Falcon 9 carries four Google TPU chips and up to 1 kilowatt of solar power. After about three weeks of checks, engineers prompted the onboard Gemma 4 model, and it beamed back: “Blue marble below / Data streams in silent grace / Starlight feeds my core” (Google, TechCrunch, The Verge).

Power limits mean only one TPU can run, for about 20 minutes at a time. The tests now turn to heat and radiation, which can flip bits in memory. If the chips hold up, Google plans two linked satellites in 2027 as a step toward solar-powered AI data centres in space (TechCrunch, Google, The Verge).

Why it matters: AI data centres are straining power grids on the ground. Moving some computing into orbit, where the sun never sets on the panels, is one long-shot answer.

Sources (4)

  • TechCrunch — Google’s first data center satellite is online and writing poetry
  • The Verge — Google’s satellite AI server transmits its first response to Earth
  • Orbital Wire — Google satellite beams first AI haiku from orbit
Community Note

One small test satellite is far from a working space data centre. Google’s own researchers say the economics depend on much cheaper launches, likely in the 2030s.

Sources: Google; TechCrunch; The Verge; Orbital Wire.

OpenAI will cut off chats that fixate on violence and hold back weapons details from teen users

OpenAI says ChatGPT will be able to end conversations automatically when a user seems fixated on violence or harming others, and may pause accounts temporarily after repeated cases. For users under 18, it will restrict practical information on weapons and security systems even when a question looks harmless (OpenAI, Reuters via CNA, CNBC TV18).

It is also limiting detail about past mass attacks for everyone, tightening the age-prediction system that moves likely minors into the teen version, and plans to alert parents if a teen’s account is paused over violent chats. The changes roll out over the next few weeks (Reuters via CNA, CNBC TV18, OpenAI).

Why it matters: Many teens use ChatGPT for homework, so these rules shape what students can and cannot ask it.

Sources (3)
