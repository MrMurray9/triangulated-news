Alberta

A torn aorta, a crowded waiting room and an eight-hour wait: the inquiry into a father’s death at the Grey Nuns ER hears its first week of testimony

Prashant Sreekumar, 44, went to the Grey Nuns Community Hospital emergency department around noon on Dec. 22, 2025, with chest pain. His wife, Niharika, says he had waited about eight hours when he was finally taken in, then collapsed in her arms saying he was dizzy. A judge-led fatality inquiry that opened Monday heard he died of an aortic dissection — a tear in the body’s main artery (CTV News Edmonton, Taproot Edmonton).

A cardiac surgeon testified that the condition needs immediate treatment, usually emergency surgery, and can be hard to spot because it mimics a heart attack or stroke. The triage nurse said the department was crowded and short of beds that day, and that Sreekumar didn’t show the warning signs he’s trained to look for. A pathologist urged doctors and medical examiners to share more information so similar deaths can be caught as a pattern (CTV, Taproot, Ai Channel). Hearings run through Friday and resume Dec. 14–18 (Alberta Justice).

Why it matters: It’s the first public, under-oath look at how an Edmonton ER lost track of a patient who was dying in its waiting room — and it can lead to formal recommendations.

Smith tells Carney she wants a Quebec-style immigration deal for Alberta — and the prime minister’s answer is “We’ll see”

At her first news conference since the vote, in Calgary on Wednesday, Premier Danielle Smith said, “I accept the results of the referendum,” and that she told Prime Minister Mark Carney on Tuesday her government is drafting a proposal modelled on the 1991 Canada-Quebec Accord, which lets Quebec pick most of its own economic immigrants. “That’s the framework of the kind of agreement we would like to negotiate … to make sure that we’re matching newcomers with the jobs that are here,” she said. She expects related changes in time for the legislature’s spring sitting (CTV News/CP24, Calgary Herald, Global News).

Asked on Parliament Hill whether Alberta could get Quebec-style powers, Carney replied “We’ll see” in French, adding that Quebec is distinct because of its language and culture and that Ottawa “will not budge” from what it calls sustainable immigration levels. About 53 per cent of voters backed more provincial control over immigration in the unofficial count (Global News, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Immigration control is the clearest concrete mandate to come out of the vote, and it’s the first test of whether Smith and Carney can deal rather than fight.

What changed since the last edition: This morning’s edition covered Smith’s televised address and her pledge of no second separation vote. New this evening: her Wednesday news conference, her call with Carney, the Quebec accord as the model, a spring-session timeline, and Carney’s noncommittal reply.

Ottawa and Ontario put up $8.7 billion for steel mills, auto plants and a Ring of Fire road to blunt U.S. tariffs

Prime Minister Mark Carney and Premier Doug Ford announced the joint plan in Ottawa on Wednesday. Why it matters: Ontario’s steel and auto plants have taken the hardest hit from U.S. tariffs, and how Ottawa spends to protect them matters for every province waiting on its own deal.

Food bank visits hit a record 2.35 million a month — and nearly one in five people in line now has a job

Food Banks Canada’s annual HungerCount, released Wednesday, counted about 2.35 million visits in March 2026, up nearly 9 per cent from last year and 117 per cent from 2019. Visits rose in every province and territory except Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador, with the steepest jumps in the North: 24 per cent in Yukon and 29 per cent in the Northwest Territories and Nunavut (The Canadian Press, Canadian Grocer, Food Banks Canada).

Nearly one in five clients is employed, up from one in 10 in 2019. The report says food prices are up nearly 30 per cent since 2021, housing 28 per cent and transportation almost 24 per cent. “Canada’s bleak new reality is that millions of people are making impossible trade-offs every day as they choose between food or rent,” CEO Kirstin Beardsley said. It asks Ottawa to double the Canada Workers Benefit and widen EI to cover gig workers (CP, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Food bank lines are one of the clearest real-world measures of how far paycheques are falling behind basic costs.

World

The UN now says 1.5°C will be breached — current policies point to 2.8°C — but cutting emissions 18 per cent by 2030 could still pull warming back down

The UN Environment Programme’s annual Emissions Gap Report, released Wednesday, projects about 2.8°C of warming by 2100 under the policies countries actually have in place. Fully carrying out their national climate plans would mean about 2.6°C, and adding every net-zero pledge would bring it to roughly 2.1°C (UNEP, UN News, AP).

For the first time the report treats overshooting 1.5°C as unavoidable. In its best case, warming would peak around 1.7–1.8°C and drift back to 1.5°C by 2100 — but only with global emissions cut about 18 per cent by 2030 and 69 per cent by 2040, plus large-scale carbon removal (UNEP, The Guardian, RTÉ). The AP reports that rising U.S. emissions are a big reason the world lost ground this year.

Why it matters: It’s the scorecard governments take into the next round of UN climate talks, and every tenth of a degree changes the odds of heat waves, wildfires and crop losses — including on the Prairies.

Science corner: UC Santa Barbara builds a tiny red LED that sets an efficiency record and keeps shining at 200°C — the missing colour for next-generation screens

MicroLED screens use millions of microscopic light sources, one per colour per pixel, and promise brighter, longer-lasting displays than OLED for things like smart glasses and watches. Blue and green are well in hand, but red has lagged. A UC Santa Barbara team made red microLEDs (619-nanometre light) from indium gallium nitride — the same family of material used for blue and green — that turn a record 20.8 per cent of the electrons they receive into light that escapes the chip (UCSB, ACS Photonics).

The trick is a ring-shaped mirror built into each device, called an annular distributed Bragg reflector, that bounces stray light back out. Heated to 200°C under heavy current, the LEDs kept more than 74 per cent of their room-temperature brightness, and their colour shifted by less than 8 nanometres (ACS Photonics, Schuller Lab at UCSB).

Why it matters: Making all three colours from one material family could make microLED screens simpler and cheaper to manufacture, and heat tolerance matters in tightly packed gadgets.

Sources (3)
  • UC Santa Barbara — Red microLEDs developed at UCSB achieve record efficiency of 20.8%, operate at up to 200°C
  • ACS Photonics — 20.8% peak external quantum efficiency of red (619 nm) InGaN/GaN microLEDs
  • Schuller Lab, UCSB — Roark Chao’s new paper published in ACS Photonics
Community Note

The paper is peer-reviewed, but coverage so far comes only from the university and the journal, so the record claim hasn’t been independently assessed by outside reporters. A 20.8 per cent peak is still well below the best blue LEDs, and wall-plug efficiency (electricity in, light out) was 13.2 per cent. These are lab devices, not a product.

Sources: UC Santa Barbara; ACS Photonics; Schuller Lab.
