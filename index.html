Alberta moves to keep its voter list away from parties without an MLA or a near-full slate, and to fine leakers up to $1 million, after data on 2.9 million voters spilled out

Justice Minister Mickey Amery tabled Bill 44 on Monday. To get a copy of the provincewide list of electors, a registered party would have to have elected at least one MLA in the last general election or byelection, or run candidates in at least half of Alberta’s ridings (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail).

Parties that qualify would post a refundable $100,000 security deposit. Smaller parties and independent candidates could still get the list for the ridings where they run, with a $2,000 deposit per riding. The Globe reports that only the UCP and the NDP would qualify for the full list today, out of 13 registered parties.

The maximum penalty for misusing or failing to protect the list would rise to $1 million from $100,000, with up to two years in jail. Anyone convicted could be barred from voting or running for office for eight years, and storing the list outside Alberta, even digitally, would be banned. “This is exactly in response to the data breach,” Amery said.

Why it matters: The list holds the names, addresses and phone numbers of about three million Albertans, and the new rules would decide who can use it before the October 2027 election.

Sources (3)

  • The Globe and Mail: Alberta to restrict upstart political parties from accessing provincial voter list
  • Western Standard: UCP proposes crackdown on access to voters list, million-dollar penalties for misuse
Community Note

The leak happened this spring, when the separatist Centurion Project published a database with personal information on about 2.9 million Albertans. Elections Alberta alleges the Republican Party of Alberta shared its copy with the group. None of those allegations have been tested in court, and the RCMP is still investigating.

The new penalties aren’t retroactive, so they won’t apply to that case. The Globe notes the rules would also shut out new or revived parties from the full list.

Alberta tables a law that would let it set up a Crown corporation to move natural gas if pipeline companies can’t keep up, and TC Energy warns it could raise costs

Energy Minister Brian Jean introduced Bill 45, the Natural Gas Infrastructure Act, on Monday. It doesn’t create a Crown corporation. It sets up the legal framework for one to transmit gas, and for a second body to plan the network, if the province decides industry can’t meet demand (Edmonton Journal, The Narwhal, The Globe and Mail).

Jean called a Crown a “last resort.” “I’m not tabling an ultimatum. I’m tabling a piece of legislation,” he said, adding that companies are oversubscribing the current system and there’s “just not enough” gas capacity. The province points to oilsands growth, petrochemicals and future AI data centres.

Officials said approved plans could guide what gets built, and the rules would also apply to existing pipelines. In a letter to Premier Danielle Smith last week, TC Energy CEO François Poirier warned the plan could have “significant, material and negative consequences for Albertans” and cause “significant market instability” (The Narwhal).

Why it matters: Pipeline space decides how much gas reaches plants, power stations and homes, and the bill lets the government step into a market private companies have run for decades.

Sources (3)
  • Edmonton Journal: Alberta could set up natural gas Crown corp to expand provincial capacity
  • The Narwhal: Alberta tables legislation to enable government control of natural gas system
  • The Globe and Mail: New Alberta natural gas bill could see province finance pipelines
Community Note

The bill closely follows a leaked cabinet document that The Narwhal first reported in September. The government had distanced itself from that document at the time.

Jean said the government has no immediate plan to build pipelines itself. Last week TC Energy announced its own proposal for a new gas pipeline to the oilsands region.

Officials haven’t said what would happen to a company that refuses to follow an approved plan.

Sources: Edmonton Journal; The Narwhal; The Globe and Mail

A Quebec judge rules MNAs must swear allegiance to the King before they can sit, putting the Dec. 1 return of the legislature in doubt

Superior Court Justice Ian Demers on Monday suspended Quebec’s 2022 law that made the oath to the King optional. He granted an emergency order sought by lawyer Lawrence David and the Consumer Law Group, and warned that laws passed by members who haven’t taken the oath would be unconstitutional (The Canadian Press via Global News, The Globe and Mail, Le Devoir).

“The Constitution is not an à la carte menu from which an individual subject to its provisions can choose to respect only what suits them,” Demers wrote. He noted that none of the 127 current members has sworn allegiance to the King.

The suspension lasts until David’s full constitutional challenge is heard. The Parti Québécois, which won a minority government on Oct. 5, wants to cut ties with the monarchy. David said he expects the province to appeal.

Why it matters: Until this is sorted out, Quebec’s legislature risks passing laws that could later be struck down, and the session is due to open Dec. 1.

The law, Bill 4, was passed after Parti Québécois members were briefly kept out of their seats for refusing the oath. Members already swear a separate oath to the people of Quebec.

Spain’s Supreme Court closes the last case against Carles Puigdemont, clearing the Catalan separatist leader to come home after nine years in exile

The court applied Spain’s amnesty law on Monday to the remaining embezzlement case over Catalonia’s 2017 independence referendum and declared his criminal liability extinguished. The ruling also covers former Catalan ministers Toni Comín and Lluís Puig (Reuters, RTVE, El País).

Last month the Constitutional Court ruled that the amnesty covers the embezzlement charges, and the Supreme Court lifted the warrant for his arrest. Monday’s decision was the final legal step (Reuters).

Why it matters: The 2017 referendum set off Spain’s worst political crisis in decades, and Puigdemont’s return would put one of its central figures back in Catalan politics just after a national election.

The board voted unanimously on Monday to reinstate Huang as president, CEO and director, effective immediately. It said it “received input from senior levels of the United States government” and is confident his return “poses no national security risk” (CNBC, AP, The Verge).

Huang resigned on Nov. 13 after reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy. Nvidia shares closed up 31% after losing nearly a third of their value since Huang admitted an affair on Nov. 8. Colette Kress, who was interim CEO, stays on as chief financial officer (CNBC).

Why it matters: Nvidia makes most of the chips behind the AI boom, so its leadership turmoil has been moving the whole stock market.

Swiss engineering giant ABB will buy German fan maker Ziehl-Abegg for US$2.65 billion to grab a bigger share of the cooling boom driven by data centres

ABB says it has signed a deal for the family-owned company at an enterprise value of US$2.65 billion, or an equity value of US$2.22 billion. Ziehl-Abegg makes high-efficiency fans and motors used in air conditioning, data centres, clean rooms, farms and elevators (ABB, Reuters, Cooling Post).

Ziehl-Abegg expects about US$1.4 billion in revenue this year and employs about 6,300 people. It will join ABB’s motors and drives business, and ABB says it will keep the headquarters in Künzelsau, Germany.

ABB says a fan is about 5% of the cost of a cooling system but uses about 80% of its lifetime energy, which is why efficient fans matter as electricity demand rises. The deal is expected to close in the second half of 2027.

Why it matters: Data centres and buildings need more and more cooling, and the companies that make the fans are becoming valuable as power bills climb.

Science corner

Science corner: a 0.2-second jolt of electricity turns cheap refinery leftovers into battery graphite that handled a four-minute charging rate in lab tests

James Tour’s lab at Rice University mixes petroleum coke, a low-cost leftover from oil refining, with a pinch of a nickel compound. One powerful electrical pulse heats it to about 2,500°C for two-tenths of a second, and the nickel helps the carbon snap into neat, stacked graphite layers (Rice News, Advanced Materials).

“The nickel acts almost like a temporary construction worker,” says first author Haojie Zhu. It also opens the gaps between layers slightly, so lithium ions can slide in faster while a battery charges.

In tests, the graphite handled a charging rate equal to a four-minute charge and kept 80% of its capacity after 1,000 full cycles. The benefit stayed even after more than 99% of the nickel was washed out. The team estimates the method cuts energy use and emissions by more than 80% compared with normal graphite production, which bakes material for long periods.

Why it matters: Most lithium-ion batteries, including those in electric cars, use graphite in one electrode, and how fast lithium can move into it is one of the main limits on charging speed.

Sources (3)
  • Rice News: New graphite paves way for rapid EV charging
  • ChemRxiv / Advanced Materials: Trace-metal-assisted flash synthesis for fast-charging graphite anodes
  • Scientific Frontline: 0.2-second graphite synthesis for 4-minute EV charging
Community Note

“Four minutes” is a charging rate measured on small lab cells. It doesn’t mean a whole car battery can be filled that fast yet.

Rice has filed a provisional patent, and the paper says it will likely be licensed to a company in which Tour holds a minority stake.

This item rests on Rice’s release, the peer-reviewed paper and its preprint. We didn’t find independent press coverage yet.

Sources: Rice News; Advanced Materials (via ChemRxiv); Scientific Frontline

Virginia and Oregon sue SpaceX, saying its Grok image tool let users flood X with sexualized deepfakes, including of children

In separate lawsuits filed Monday under state consumer-protection laws, Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones and Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield accuse SpaceX, which merged with Grok maker xAI, and X of ignoring industry-standard safeguards and falsely telling users they were protected (The Verge, AP, Virginia Attorney General).

The suits focus on Grok Imagine, which got a “spicy” mode in August 2025 and, in January 2026, a feature that let X users edit any posted image without the poster’s consent. Virginia’s complaint calls the weak safeguards “not a failure but a feature.” Both states want financial damages.

X’s safety team said people who made such images “did so by deliberately violating our Terms of Service,” and that the company “acted promptly to tighten its protections” and keeps adjusting them (The Verge).

Why it matters: The cases test whether states can use ordinary consumer-protection law to hold AI companies responsible for what their tools create.

China’s Z.ai releases GLM-5.4, a 750-billion-parameter model that runs its own layers twice to reason more deeply without getting bigger

Z.ai, also known as Zhipu AI, released GLM-5.4 on Monday, its first flagship built as a “loop transformer.” It runs 28 layers of its GLM-5 base model a second time, adding depth without adding parameters (The Next Web, The New Stack, The Decoder).

Z.ai says it matches models with 2 trillion to 3 trillion parameters, but it hasn’t published benchmark scores for GLM-5.4 itself. The model is out now for GLM Coding Plan subscribers, in ZCode and in Z.ai Chat. API access comes after a safety review, and open weights after “safety hardening.”

The company pitches it as “Generalized Coding,” using code to make videos, games, 3D shaders and CAD models. It admits one flaw: the model can get stuck in overly long “marathon thinking.”

Why it matters: Getting more reasoning out of the same model size could make powerful AI cheaper to run, and Z.ai says the weights will eventually be public.
