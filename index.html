Alberta

Smith invokes the Sovereignty Act against Ottawa’s new hate-crime law, saying she doesn’t want “somebody’s pastor” jailed for a sermon

Premier Danielle Smith’s government said Tuesday it will table a motion under the Alberta Sovereignty Within a United Canada Act targeting the federal Combatting Hate Act, Bill C-9, which took effect in July. The motion was expected in the legislature Tuesday afternoon (Edmonton Journal, CTV News, The Canadian Press).

Alberta’s main objection is that the federal law removed a Criminal Code defence for people expressing a religious opinion in good faith. “We just don’t want to wait until somebody’s pastor has been hauled off to jail,” Smith said. If passed, the motion asks cabinet to consider ways the province could push back (Edmonton Journal, CTV News).

Federal Justice Minister Sean Fraser’s office said sermons and scripture readings given in good faith don’t meet the law’s standard, which requires a clear intent to promote hatred. It also pointed out that hate-propaganda charges in Alberta already need the consent of the province’s own justice minister, Mickey Amery. Opposition Leader Naheed Nenshi called the motion “red meat” for UCP members ahead of the party’s annual meeting in Calgary this weekend.

Why it matters: It’s the latest use of Smith’s signature law to challenge Ottawa, this time over speech and religion rather than energy or guns.

Sources (4)
  • Edmonton Journal: Alberta uses Sovereignty Act to challenge federal hate speech law
  • CTV News Edmonton: Smith moves to assert ‘freedom of speech and religion’ in new stand against federal law
  • The Canadian Press (via Global News): Alberta takes symbolic stand against federal hate crime law, says it silences speech
  • Government of Alberta: Alberta Sovereignty within a United Canada Act
Community Note

A Sovereignty Act motion is largely symbolic. It can’t strike down or suspend a federal law, and only the courts can rule on whether C-9 is constitutional. Critics, including Nenshi, say a province can’t pick which parts of the Criminal Code apply within its borders.

Ottawa’s position is that religious expression is still protected by the Charter. Justice Minister Amery told reporters Alberta had 14 hate-crime cases in 2025–26 but had no numbers yet for the period since C-9 took effect.

Alberta RCMP apologize for suggesting murdered 12-year-old Fiasal Hussein’s “choices” played a part in his death

In a statement Tuesday, Alberta RCMP commanding officer Trevor Daroux said police “regret that our words caused further hurt” to Fiasal Hussein’s family and friends. He was walking back a line from an Oct. 27 news conference, where a major crimes officer said “whatever challenges or choices may have led to his untimely death, he was still a child” (CityNews Edmonton, CTV News, Edmonton Journal).

Why it matters: Police are formally changing how they talk about children drawn into crime, treating them as exploited victims rather than youth who made bad choices.

After 100 hours of debate, MPs send the major projects bill back to the House, with one small new check on Ottawa’s power to end strikes

The special committee studying Bill C-39, the Building Canada Strong Act, voted Monday night to send it back to the House of Commons with a few amendments. Liberal MPs rejected the bigger changes that labour and environmental groups had asked for (The Globe and Mail, Politico).

One change the Liberals did accept was a Conservative amendment adding limited parliamentary oversight when the labour minister uses Section 107 of the Canada Labour Code to end a strike or lockout (National Post). The bill would let cabinet name “regions of national interest” where major projects get fast-tracked. It also changes labour law to make big disruptions at railways, airlines and ports less likely.

“Liberals defeated every amendment that would have safeguarded our Charter-protected right to strike,” said Siobhán Vipond of the Canadian Labour Congress. Conservative MP Dan Albas said the marathon sittings left MPs “tired and cranky.” The Globe reports the bill could become law before Parliament breaks in December.

Why it matters: Prime Minister Carney calls this the most important economic bill of this Parliament. It changes how fast pipelines, mines and ports get approved, and how strikes in those sectors can end.

Sources (3)

  • National Post: Liberals agree to limited parliamentary oversight of minister’s power to end strikes
  • Politico Canada Playbook: 100 hours at committee
Community Note

The committee’s vote doesn’t make C-39 law. It still needs a final vote in the House and passage in the Senate, where a special committee has already started hearing witnesses.

Unions say they’ll challenge the strike provisions in court once the bill passes. The government says the changes are needed to keep supply chains moving.

Residents of a South Korean island get an “EVACUATION, AIR RAID” alert as Russian warplanes fly toward it

At 7:52 a.m. Tuesday, Korea time, South Korea’s interior ministry told the roughly 8,700 people on Ulleungdo, an island 120 km off the east coast, to take shelter. Several Russian military aircraft had entered the country’s air defence identification zone and flown toward the island. The alert was lifted three minutes later (The Guardian, KBS World, SBS).

The Joint Chiefs of Staff said the planes came “to the point just before violating Ulleungdo’s airspace,” so the alert was sent as a precaution. Fighter jets were scrambled, and the defence ministry summoned Russia’s defence attaché to protest (The Guardian, Stars and Stripes).

Residents described confusion. One told broadcaster YTN that an elderly neighbour called to ask whether war had broken out. A follow-up text from the provincial government wrongly said the aircraft was “presumed to be North Korean.” The province said it had reused wording from a drill message by mistake.

Why it matters: South Korea hasn’t used shelter alerts like this before. It shows Seoul is now treating Russian flights near its territory as a real threat to civilians.

Science corner: KAIST builds a bendable battery that fits inside a drone’s hollow legs, so the frame can store power too

Most drones carry a battery pack as extra weight. A team led by Prof. Il-Kwon Oh at KAIST in South Korea, working with Sweden’s Chalmers University of Technology, wants the drone’s own frame to hold the energy. They grew tiny nickel cobalt selenide nanowires directly onto woven carbon cloth, making a flexible electrode for a lithium-sulfur battery (KAIST, Interesting Engineering, DongA Science).

Because the wires grow out of the fibres instead of being painted on, they stay attached when the electrode bends. The team bent it 1,000 times around a curve just 2.5 mm across without damage. Pouch cells made with it fit inside oval, square and drone-leg-shaped parts and kept an LED lit. In one test, the battery held 99.8% of its voltage even after the tube around it cracked under load (Interesting Engineering).

The nanowires also help with lithium-sulfur’s biggest weakness. They trap the sulfur compounds that usually leak out during charging and wear the battery down. The work was published in Advanced Composites and Hybrid Materials.

Why it matters: If parts of a drone or robot’s body can double as its battery, the machine gets lighter, which could mean longer flights or room for more cargo.

Sources (5)
  • Interesting Engineering: Bendable lithium-sulfur battery retains 99.8% voltage across 1,000 flex cycles
  • DongA Science: KAIST integrates curved lithium–sulfur batteries into drone legs
  • The Herald Business: KAIST develops electrode that survives 1,000 bends, turning drone legs into batteries
  • KAIST News Center
  • Advanced Composites and Hybrid Materials: the study
Community Note

This is a lab proof of concept. The demos lit an LED, not a flying drone, and the team says it hasn’t yet measured how much weight would be saved or how much flight time would be gained.

The paper was published online Oct. 1. KAIST publicized it on Nov. 18.

AI news and advances

OpenAI says a general chatbot model, GPT-6.2, designed proteins that actually stuck to their disease targets in lab tests

OpenAI reported Tuesday that GPT-6.2 designed small proteins meant to bind five targets linked to cancer and immune disorders, including PD-L1 and VEGF-A. When lab company Onava made and tested them, 35 of 322 designs bound their targets, about 11% (OpenAI, Nature, The Defiant).

Nature reports that general-purpose language models from OpenAI and Anthropic are starting to do protein work that used to need specialist tools like Google DeepMind’s AlphaFold. A Nov. 16 test by researchers at Phylo and Harvard found Claude models beat specialized protein models at predicting obscure bacterial protein sequences.

“We want to see a world where a scientist can just describe in natural language what they want,” said Joy Jiao, who leads life-sciences research at OpenAI (Nature).

Why it matters: If everyday AI models can help design proteins, drug discovery could get faster and more scientists could do it, not just specialist labs.

Sources (4)

  • The Defiant (Frontier): OpenAI reports lab binders from GPT-6.2 protein designs
  • AlphaSignal: OpenAI’s GPT-6.2 designs real proteins that bind drug targets in the lab
Community Note

Specialist tools still did key steps. GPT-6.2 drew the structures, but a separate protein-design program chose the genetic sequences, and the model did poorly at predicting 3D structures (Nature, OpenAI).

The lab tests only showed the proteins bind their targets, not that they work as medicines. Some researchers told Nature the models may partly be repeating their training data rather than understanding biology.

The NHS starts enrolling women in the world’s biggest test of AI for reading mammograms, about 700,000 over three years

The first women have joined the EDITH trial at Cambridge University Hospitals. It’s the first of 30 U.K. screening sites, and about 700,000 women having routine mammograms are expected to take part over three years. The £10-million study is funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research and led by the universities of Warwick and Cambridge (NHS England, University of Cambridge, The Independent).

Right now, two specialists read every screening mammogram. The trial tests whether an AI tool can safely let one specialist do most of them, freeing radiologists to see patients. Researchers say they tested four AI systems before the trial. “All clinical decisions during EDITH will remain with NHS staff,” said co-lead Prof. Sian Taylor-Phillips.

Women get the usual screening invitation, with details about the trial and a way to opt out (NHS England).

Why it matters: Screening programs everywhere, including Alberta’s, are short on radiologists. This is the biggest real-world test yet of whether AI can safely ease that shortage.

Sources (4)

  • University of Cambridge: Cambridge co-leading world’s largest trial to use AI to help detect breast cancers earlier
  • The Independent: NHS launches world’s largest AI breast cancer trial
  • Cambridge University Hospitals: Cambridgeshire women first to join world-leading breast screening trial
Community Note

The trial is just starting, so there are no results yet on whether AI finds more cancers. Every scan will still be read by at least one human specialist.

Most coverage comes from the NHS and the universities running the trial. The Independent notes radiology leaders have warned that AI isn’t a quick fix for staff shortages.

Coach Mike Babcock says he’ll likely move back to centre after tonight. Devon Levi starts in goal. Zach Hyman is still out with the migraine that sidelined him on the weekend, and there’s no timeline for his return. Edmonton (17-5-4) hosts Pacific rival Vegas (14-6-5) for the first of four meetings this season, then hosts Seattle on Friday (EdmontonOilers.com, NHL.com).
