Alberta

Alberta cuts the core classes needed to graduate high school from 56 credits to 47, and makes gym mandatory right through Grade 12

Education Minister Demetrios Nicolaides announced the overhaul Friday at a Calgary charter school. Students will still need 100 credits for a diploma, but optional credits rise from 44 to 53. Grade 10 stays the same for English, social studies, math and science. In grades 11 and 12, the separate English and social studies requirements become 10 combined “humanities” credits, and math and science together need just five (Global News, Calgary Herald, Red Deer Advocate).

Physical education and wellness becomes mandatory in all three high school grades, along with new financial-literacy credits, and every graduate will still have to write at least one diploma exam. The change starts with Grade 10 students in fall 2028 and covers all grades by fall 2030. Nicolaides said it’s partly about steering more young people into the trades. The Alberta Teachers’ Association says it wasn’t consulted and worries the change weakens core instruction (Calgary Herald, Government of Alberta).

Why it matters: Today’s Grade 6 and 7 students will be the first to graduate under these rules, with more room for trades, work experience and dual-credit classes but less required English, social studies and science.

Canada

Carney closes two Asian trade deals in Manila: talks are done on a free-trade pact with the 10-country ASEAN bloc and a separate one with the Philippines

What changed since the last edition: On Nov. 9 we reported Canada was expected to close a Philippines deal at this summit. Both that deal and a wider ASEAN agreement are now officially concluded.

At the ASEAN summit in Manila on Saturday (local time), Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that Canada and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations had “substantially concluded” negotiations on a free-trade agreement. Ottawa says the deal, covering a region of almost 700 million people, could add nearly $2 billion to Canada’s economy and about 14,000 jobs, mainly in farming and manufacturing, with signing aimed for 2027 (Prime Minister’s Office, AP, Philippine Daily Inquirer).

Carney and President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. also wrapped up a separate Canada-Philippines deal, a year after they agreed to pursue one, along with closer military and cyber-security co-operation and an extension of Canada’s satellite program that tracks illegal fishing boats to 2033. Carney also told reporters he’d had a long conversation with Donald Trump before the trip and that Canada-U.S. trade talks, which collapsed in August, are making progress again (AP, The Globe and Mail, CNA).

Why it matters: Southeast Asia is one of the fastest-growing markets for Canadian beef, canola, pork and lumber, and these deals are Ottawa’s biggest step yet to sell less to the U.S. and more to everyone else.

Trump, Xi and Putin will hold their first-ever three-way meeting next week in China, with nuclear arms cuts and Iran on the agenda but not Ukraine

The White House said Friday the three leaders will meet on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, with the meeting set for Nov. 19. Spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump wants to talk about reducing nuclear weapons, which he considers “very dangerous.” Trump leaves on his Asia trip Monday (AP, The Japan Times, Anadolu Agency).

A senior U.S. official played down expectations, calling it “the entry point, the first conversation” rather than a negotiation, and said no agreement is expected. Trump plans to raise the eight-month-old Iran war, but Ukraine will be left out because China isn’t part of the separate U.S.-Russia talks. Trump’s one-on-one with Xi was described as a check-in on their trade truce, with no tariff changes expected (Anadolu Agency, AP, Lianhe Zaobao).

Why it matters: The U.S., Russia and China hold almost all of the world’s nuclear weapons, and the three leaders have never sat down together, so even a first conversation could shape arms control for years.

Overloaded 1980s-era switches and copper lines caused Friday’s U.S. ground stop, the longest since 9/11; flights resumed Friday night but delays will last days

What changed since the last edition: Last night’s edition reported the stop as it happened, with no cause known. The FAA has now lifted it and given a preliminary cause: old equipment overwhelmed by data, with no sign of a cyberattack.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said switches and routers in the FAA’s older analog network, which still carries voice and radar data over copper lines, became overloaded with data, causing inconsistent radar and radio contact with planes. Newer digital equipment kept working. “It’s an old system that went down and went down everywhere,” he said (Los Angeles Times, AVweb, Al Jazeera).

Airspace reopened at 8:15 p.m. Eastern (6:15 p.m. Edmonton time) after more than four hours. Duffy said more than 3,500 planes were in the air when the stop began and departures would restart gradually. FlightAware counted more than 10,000 delayed or cancelled flights. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no sign of a cyberattack, and officials ruled out the FAA’s new AI advisory system as a cause (Reuters, Al Jazeera, AVweb).

Why it matters: Airlines warned it could take days to get crews and planes back where they belong, so anyone flying to or through the U.S. this weekend should check their flight before leaving home.

Applied Materials posts the biggest quarter in its history, up 53% to US$10.4 billion, yet its shares fall 6% as supply shortages cap its outlook

The world’s largest maker of chipmaking equipment reported record fourth-quarter revenue of US$10.41 billion after the bell Thursday, with adjusted earnings of US$4.16 a share, both ahead of forecasts. It expects about US$11.1 billion in the current quarter, give or take US$500 million (Applied Materials, Seeking Alpha).

That guidance came in a little below what analysts wanted, and the stock fell about 6% Friday as at least 10 firms cut their price targets. Management said tight supply, not weak demand, is holding sales back, and CEO Gary Dickerson predicted spending on chipmaking tools will jump more than 30% next year (Investing.com, Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: Applied sells the machines every chipmaker needs, so its order book is an early read on how long the AI building boom can keep going.

Science corner

Science corner: Cornell puts the lasers that chill quantum-computer atoms onto a chip, cooling an ion three times faster than a standard lab setup

Trapped-ion quantum computers store information in single charged atoms held still by electric fields. Every so often the ions jiggle and have to be cooled with lasers back to almost perfect stillness, and that pause eats up most of a calculation’s time. Cornell engineers built the cooling light into the chip that traps the ion, shaping it into a steady “standing wave” (Cornell Chronicle, PRX Quantum).

In tests with a single calcium ion, the chip brought it near its lowest possible energy in about 150 microseconds, versus roughly 500 for a conventional laser setup, and it cooled several kinds of motion at once. Because the light comes from built-in waveguides rather than mirrors on a lab bench, the team says the design can be copied across large chips with many ions (Cornell Chronicle, Interesting Engineering).

Why it matters: Faster cooling means more time spent computing and less time waiting, one of the bottlenecks between today’s small quantum machines and useful large ones.

Sources (3)
  • Cornell Chronicle: Researchers develop faster laser cooling for quantum computers
  • PRX Quantum: Rapid multi-mode trapped-ion laser cooling in a phase-stable standing wave
  • Interesting Engineering: Chip-based lasers cool trapped ions 3x faster for quantum computing
Community Note

Coverage rests on Cornell’s release and the peer-reviewed paper, so we treat it as a light science item.

Orders for Anthropic’s US$64-billion IPO hit six times the shares on offer by day three of its roadshow, ahead of a Nov. 18 pricing

What changed since the last edition: Our last Anthropic IPO story, on Nov. 7, covered talk of a lower valuation. Since then it has set a price range and listing date, and the first demand figures are in.

Anthropic began marketing its stock to big investors on Nov. 10, offering 825 million shares at US$70 to US$78 each, which would raise about US$64 billion at the top of the range. It plans to set the final price Wednesday and start trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on Thursday, Nov. 19 (Anthropic, CNBC, Yahoo Finance).

Bloomberg first reported the book was about six times oversubscribed, and Reuters says demand is running ahead of SpaceX’s IPO at the same stage. Investors are still asking whether Anthropic’s Claude can hold its lead as Google catches up, and some said the company gave thin answers in meetings. Banks’ models lean on forecasts of roughly US$270 billion in revenue by 2028 (Bloomberg Law, Reuters via CNA).

Why it matters: It would be one of the largest stock-market debuts ever, and its reception will signal how much public investors are willing to pay for AI companies after this autumn’s tech sell-off.

OSpider-Man, the Hulk and Deadpool rappel down a children’s hospital in Omaha, washing windows and waving to young patients
