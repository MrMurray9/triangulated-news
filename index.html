Alberta

“City of Champions” is going back up: Alberta will put the old slogan on new highway signs into Edmonton after the Elks’ Grey Cup win

Transportation Minister Devin Dreeshen announced the signs on social media, holding one up in front of the Grey Cup with Elks owners, executives and players. The blue signs show the skyline, the Walterdale Bridge and the legislature, and read “Welcome to Edmonton – The City of Champions,” with a lower panel marking the Elks’ 2026 title (Edmonton Journal, CTV News Edmonton).

About four signs are planned, likely near city entrances on Gateway Boulevard and Yellowhead Trail, with installation starting in the next few weeks, weather permitting. The city first put up the slogan in 1988, after the Oilers’ and Eskimos’ championships and the community’s response to the 1987 tornado; council took the signs down in 2015 (Edmonton Journal, Culture Alberta).

Why it matters: The slogan has been a running argument in Edmonton for a decade. The province is putting it on its own highway signs, so the city doesn’t get the final say, though council is now also looking at it as part of a rebrand.

Alberta will now pay part of its carbon-capture grants while projects are still being built, not only after they start running

Under the Alberta Carbon Capture Incentive Program, companies in oil and gas, petrochemicals, power and other industries can get grants covering 12% of eligible capital costs. Until now that money came only once a project was operating; the change lets some of it flow during construction, and the program now runs through 2035 (The Canadian Press, Western Standard, Government of Alberta).

Energy and Minerals Minister Brian Jean said the change lines the program up with the federal carbon capture investment tax credit and should speed projects up. It comes as Ottawa, Alberta and the five biggest oilsands producers try to finish deals on the Pathways carbon-capture pipeline, after missing an aim of announcing them around Nov. 15 (The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: Carbon capture is the price of admission for more oilsands growth and the proposed West Coast pipeline, and paying earlier makes the expensive build easier to finance.

Sources (3)
  • CityNews Edmonton (The Canadian Press): Alberta tweaks carbon capture incentive program to allow grants during construction
  • Western Standard: Alberta extends carbon capture grants to 2035 and allows payments during construction
  • Government of Alberta: Alberta Carbon Capture Incentive Program
Community Note

The grant rate itself (12% of eligible capital costs) hasn’t changed; what changes is when the money is paid.

Pathways is being developed by Cenovus, Canadian Natural, Suncor, Imperial and ConocoPhillips Canada, which say it could store about six million tonnes of CO2 a year by the mid-2030s. No final agreement has been announced.

Sources: The Canadian Press; Western Standard; Government of Alberta

Canada

The Supreme Court will hear Saskatchewan’s school pronoun case on March 22 and 23, a test of whether courts can still review laws that use the notwithstanding clause

Saskatchewan’s 2023 Parents’ Bill of Rights requires a parent’s permission before a student under 16 can change the name or pronouns they use at school, and it invokes the notwithstanding clause. UR Pride challenged it; the province argued courts have no jurisdiction once the clause is used. Lower courts let the case go ahead, and both sides appealed (980 CJME, Supreme Court of Canada).

The March hearing won’t decide whether the law itself is constitutional. It’s about whether judges can still rule that a law breaches the Charter after the notwithstanding clause has been invoked. There are 28 interveners: Ottawa, Manitoba and B.C. are expected to argue courts can still review, while Alberta and Quebec side with Saskatchewan (980 CJME, NetNewsLedger).

Why it matters: Alberta has used the notwithstanding clause in its own laws, so the court’s answer will shape what Albertans can challenge in court.

Sources (3)
  • Supreme Court of Canada: Hearing schedule, winter 2027 session
  • 980 CJME: Sask. pronoun law case to be heard by Supreme Court in March
  • NetNewsLedger: Supreme Court sets March hearing on Saskatchewan school pronoun law
Community Note

The Supreme Court agreed to hear the case almost a year ago; this week it set the dates. A ruling could take months after the hearing.

The case is Government of Saskatchewan v. UR Pride Centre for Sexuality and Gender Diversity (file 41979).

Sources: Supreme Court of Canada; 980 CJME; NetNewsLedger

The COP31 climate summit runs past its deadline after a new draft deal drops every mention of fossil fuels

Nearly 200 countries were still deadlocked in Antalya, Turkey, late Friday. A closing session approved some technical items, but the main political deal wasn’t done. “We are close to the finish line, but we are not there yet,” said COP31 president Murat Kurum. A third draft is expected Saturday (AFP, The Guardian).

Australia’s first draft called for a faster phase-out of fossil fuels; Thursday’s second draft removed them entirely. A dozen countries, including France, Panama and the Solomon Islands, demanded “a clear transition away from fossil fuels,” and Panama threatened to walk out. The draft does propose having electricity meet 35% of global final energy demand by 2035 (AFP, Carbon Brief, UN News).

Why it matters: Canada’s oil and gas sector and Alberta’s carbon-capture plans sit right in the middle of what’s being argued over: fossil-fuel language, electrification and whether carbon capture counts as a real solution.

The UN’s World Food Programme plans to help fewer people next year, 91 million instead of 110 million, while warning of a third global food crisis

WFP launched a US$12-billion appeal for 2027, down from its 2026 goal of reaching 110 million people. That covers only about a third of the 266 million people facing acute hunger, as the Iran war pushes up food and shipping costs and a strong El Niño threatens harvests. Its 2026 appeal of about US$13 billion is only half funded (Reuters, World Food Programme).

“We could soon be facing a third global food crisis in six years,” WFP’s Matthew Hollingworth said, after the price spikes of the pandemic and Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine. About 72% of the new request is for emergency response, with places like war-torn Sudan at risk of famine (Reuters, UN News, WFP).

Why it matters: Higher fuel and shipping costs are the same pressures pushing up grocery bills here; in poorer countries they decide whether people eat.

Why it matters: Canada’s biggest grocer is warning that the relief in food inflation may not last once the holiday freeze ends.

Science corner

Science corner: a 1.5-millimetre lens with nanoscale patterns keeps AR-glasses text readable in bright sunshine

Smart glasses work by bouncing light from a tiny display through the lens to your eye, but a lot of it leaks away on the trip, so images wash out outdoors. A team from POSTECH in South Korea and Samsung Research etched the lens’s inner pathway, or waveguide, with structures smaller than a wavelength of light, each shaped for where it sits so light gets steered toward the eye instead of escaping (Nature, POSTECH).

Two stacked layers, one for blue and green and one for red and green, make full colour in a lens about 1.5 mm thick, roughly a third of today’s commercial lenses. The prototype showed crisp white text outdoors in daylight and full-colour images indoors with the display at just 30% power (DongA Science, POSTECH).

Why it matters: Brightness and bulk are the two big reasons people don’t wear AR glasses all day, and this lens works on both while using about the same power.

Sources (3)
  • Nature: Metasurface waveguides for all-day augmented reality glasses
  • POSTECH: Thin lenses, clear images in bright daylight
  • DongA Science: POSTECH and Samsung cut AR glasses lens thickness to 1.5 mm
Community Note

This is a research prototype. The team still needs to get the same results with a single layer and cut a rainbow-like blur caused by the fine structures.

Lead researcher Noh Joon-seok says his lab has already mass-produced metasurface lenses at a few thousand won each and aims to see them in commercial glasses within three years.

Sources: Nature; POSTECH; DongA Science

AI news and advances

An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis, and early outside checks of the computer proof are holding up

Levent Alpöge posted a short note Friday saying that, working with Claude, he’d found a proof of the 1859 hypothesis about how prime numbers are distributed, one of the seven US$1-million Millennium Prize Problems. He released it as code in Lean, a language that lets computers check each logical step, and says the method also covers the generalized version (AFP, The Verge, Scientific American).

Independent mathematicians, including MIT’s Andrew Sutherland, have compiled the Lean proof and say it checks out using only the standard axioms. Experts are still running a further check that the code proves the actual Riemann hypothesis and doesn’t quietly redefine anything. “It’s really the biggest thing I think that’s ever happened in mathematics,” specialist Brian Conrey told AFP (Scientific American, AFP).

Why it matters: If it holds up, it would be the first time an AI has helped crack the most famous open problem in mathematics.

Sources (3)
  • Phys.org (AFP): Anthropic researcher says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis
  • The Verge: Anthropic mathematician claims to have solved prestigious Millennium Prize problem with Claude
  • Scientific American: The AI Riemann hypothesis proof is almost certainly true, independent mathematicians attest
Community Note

This is still a claim. The Clay Mathematics Institute requires a solution to be published and survive two years of scrutiny before it can win the prize, and Alpöge says he hasn’t yet mastered all the mathematics in it or written a full human-readable version.

Alpöge says he told Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei before announcing, and the note came out the day after Anthropic’s stock market debut; Anthropic hadn’t commented.

Sources: AFP; The Verge; Scientific American
