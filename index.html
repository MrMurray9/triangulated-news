Alberta

Alberta will bring in a law as early as next week to shut out “speculative” data centres and keep big ones away from homes, the tech minister says

Technology Minister Nate Glubish told The Logic on Friday that the rules will target proposals with no real business plan, no customers or no financing, and projects on “terrible sites.” “That’s not something that Alberta is going to tolerate,” he said. The bill could also set province-wide limits on how close data centres and their power plants can be to homes, and deal with worries about farmland (The Logic).

The push follows a fight in Olds, where the Alberta Utilities Commission rejected a 1.4-gigawatt gas plant for the proposed Synapse data centre because more than 700 homes sat within 800 metres. At a town hall in Lacombe, Glubish told residents nobody should have to worry about a gigawatt-scale plant “right across the street” from a subdivision. Alberta’s grid operator is tracking about 28 gigawatts of requested data-centre power, up from roughly 20 at the end of 2025 (The Logic, The Albertan, CentralAlbertaOnline).

Why it matters: Alberta is courting tens of billions of dollars in AI data centres, and these rules will decide where they can go and how close they can get to towns and farmland.

Sources (4)
  • The Logic: Alberta is bringing in rules to block frivolous data-centre proposals
  • The Albertan: Alberta tech minister promises new siting rules for data centres
  • CentralAlbertaOnline: Lacombe residents challenge minister at AI town hall meeting
  • CentralAlbertaOnline: Alberta weighs setback guidance, new water rules for AI data centres
Community Note

No bill has been tabled yet, so the exact setback distances aren’t known. Earlier, Premier Danielle Smith said five kilometres is “probably too much,” but 500 metres might not be enough in some cases.

The AUC’s Olds ruling covered only the power plant; the commission said it doesn’t regulate data centres themselves, which is part of why residents want provincial rules.

Sources: The Logic; The Albertan; CentralAlbertaOnline

Canada

Ottawa puts $200 million into B.C.’s Woodfibre LNG so the export plant near Squamish can ship up to 24% more gas to Asia

Energy and Natural Resources Minister Tim Hodgson announced the grant Friday. The plant on Howe Sound was designed for 2.1 million tonnes of liquefied natural gas a year; the money should push that to 2.5 to 2.6 million tonnes and lets the owners explore a second phase. It’s about three-quarters built, and the first cargo is expected to sail next year (Natural Resources Canada, The Logic, Vancouver Sun).

Woodfibre runs on B.C. hydro power instead of burning gas to chill it, and was the first major industrial project in Canada approved through an Indigenous-led environmental review, by the Squamish Nation. A minister’s spokesperson told The Logic the money is a grant, paid out only as the project hits milestones, such as owners Pacific Energy and Enbridge deciding the expansion is worth it (Natural Resources Canada, The Logic, My Coast Now).

Why it matters: Most Canadian LNG comes from Western Canadian gas fields, including Alberta’s, and Ottawa is betting on Asian buyers as it tries to sell less of everything to the U.S.

Russia demands the world juniors be moved out of Alberta or cancelled after Canada bars its players, and threatens to seek compensation

What changed since the last edition: On Nov. 12 we reported Ottawa’s decision to keep Russian players out. Russia has now formally demanded the event be moved or cancelled and is threatening to seek compensation.

Russian Ice Hockey Federation president Vladislav Tretiak said the IIHF must either let Russia play, move the tournament, not hold it, or punish Hockey Canada, Russian state news agency TASS reported. He also said Russia could seek compensation and asked the IIHF ethics committee to sanction countries organising boycotts (Euronews, AP, Inside the Games).

The 10-team tournament opens Dec. 26 in Edmonton and Red Deer. Ottawa has said even Russians already playing in North America can’t join, because their work visas don’t allow it. Moving it this late would be extremely hard, and several countries, including Finland, Norway and Estonia, are now also barring Russian athletes. The IIHF hasn’t commented on the demand (Euronews, AP).

Why it matters: Edmonton and Red Deer have tickets sold and venues booked for the holidays, so how the IIHF handles this will decide whether the tournament goes ahead as planned.

Carney draws a line in U.S. trade talks: no deal unless Canada keeps a “viable” auto industry, and he reveals a long call with Trump, Greer and Miller

What changed since the last edition: This morning we noted Carney saying talks were moving again. He has now set a firm condition, a viable auto sector, and named who was on his call with Trump.

“This government will not settle for a deal with the United States that is not consistent with a viable Canadian auto sector,” Prime Minister Mark Carney told reporters in Manila on Saturday, calling cars “one of the most challenging issues” in the talks. He said a “quite long, substantive conversation” with Trump this week also included U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and White House deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller (The Canadian Press via Global News, The Globe and Mail, CP24).

Canada walked away from the talks in August over last-minute U.S. demands that included a hit to the auto sector. Carney also said Chinese investment in Canada’s car industry is capped in time and volume, and repeated that Ottawa is “not satisfied in any way” with how Stellantis is handling the sale of its Brampton plant (Global News, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Auto parts and vehicles are Canada’s biggest manufactured export to the U.S., and the outcome will shape tens of thousands of jobs in Ontario and supplier plants across the country.

Hungarian investigators dig up nearly US$20 million in cash, some buried in forests, in a bribery probe of one of Orbán’s former ministers

Hungary’s new National Asset Recovery and Protection Office said searches on Nov. 12 and 13 in Budapest and three other regions turned up forints, euros and U.S. dollars hidden in farm buildings, warehouses, a home, a safe in a remote spot and holes in the ground in forests. Its video shows officers digging after dark. Finance Minister András Kármán said 12 treasury workers needed eight hours to count it all (Al Jazeera with AP and Reuters, Euronews, DW).

The probe targets Miklós Seszták, development minister from 2014 to 2018, who is suspected of taking more than US$37 million in bribes linked to overpriced second-hand buses for a state transport company and government building projects. He was detained in September and denies wrongdoing. Prime Minister Péter Magyar, whose party ended Viktor Orbán’s 16 years in power in April, called it the end of the “Fidesz-KDNP mafia” (Al Jazeera, Politico Europe, NBC News).

Why it matters: It’s the most striking result yet of the new government’s promise to chase corruption from the Orbán years, and a test of whether those cases hold up in court.

Turkey and Qatar sign a mutual defence pact, promising a joint response if either is attacked, as the Iran war reshapes Gulf security

President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani signed the deal in Istanbul on Friday, one of five agreements from their yearly strategic committee. The two countries pledge a “unified position” against any outside armed attack on either one, and will hold joint exercises, train each other’s personnel and build weapons together (France 24 with Reuters and AFP, Anadolu Agency, Qatar News Agency).

It follows the Mecca defence pact Turkey signed with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan in August, which the Turkish parliament ratified last week. Euronews notes the Gulf’s security map is shifting quickly because of the Iran war and signs the U.S. is rethinking its long-standing presence in the region. Turkey already has a military base in Qatar (France 24, Euronews).

Why it matters: Turkey has NATO’s second-largest army and Qatar hosts a major U.S. air base, so a formal promise to defend each other ties a NATO member more tightly to Gulf conflicts.

Business

North Korean hackers stole more than US$400 million in crypto by faking video calls with victims’ real friends, the FBI says, as the U.S. seizes their servers

The FBI calls the scheme “FaceStealer.” Hackers sent meeting invites from hijacked accounts, then played secretly recorded webcam clips of earlier victims so a trusted contact seemed to be on the call. A staged “technical problem” led targets to install malware, and in some cases the thieves waited more than a year before draining wallets. The U.S. Justice Department says it seized the cloud servers behind it and more than 900 recorded webcam videos (DW with Reuters and AP, U.S. Justice Department, BleepingComputer).

Most targets work in crypto and tech, and the group is increasingly going after open-source software developers, which could open the door to wider supply-chain attacks. The FBI says it’s also starting to use AI deepfakes of well-known crypto figures. Separately, prosecutors said they have court approval to seize or freeze more than US$1.2 billion from last month’s Binance heist (DW, Reuters via Investing.com, BleepingComputer).

Why it matters: If a familiar face on a video call can be faked, the old advice of “just get them on camera” no longer proves who you’re talking to, for businesses or for families.

Science corner

Science corner: a new recipe for the liquid inside water-based batteries lets iodine carry twice the charge, a step toward fire-safe grid storage

Big battery farms that store solar and wind power mostly use lithium-ion cells, which can catch fire and need mined metals like cobalt. Batteries that use water as their liquid are safer and cheaper but hold less energy. A UCL-led team got iodine, the material that stores the energy, to move four electrons each cycle instead of two (UCL News, Nature).

The trick was stopping a very reactive form of iodine from breaking down in water without the corrosive, expensive salt solutions earlier attempts needed. The new liquid uses some molecules to keep water away from the iodine and others to latch onto it. Zinc-iodine test cells kept about 94% of their capacity after 2,000 charge cycles, and worked in both coin-shaped and flat pouch cells (Nature, TechXplore).

Why it matters: Cheaper, non-flammable storage would make it easier for places like Alberta to bank wind and solar power for calm, dark winter evenings.

Sources (3)
  • UCL News: Breakthrough opens new possibilities for safer, water-based batteries
  • Nature: Non-halogen aqueous electrolytes for four-electron iodine conversion
  • TechXplore: Water-based batteries store more energy in iodine with new electrolyte design
Community Note

These are small lab cells; the team says its next step is testing at larger scale and in real-world conditions.

Coverage rests on UCL’s release and the peer-reviewed Nature paper, so we treat it as a light science item.

Sources: UCL News; Nature; TechXplore

AI news and advances

Google DeepMind’s AI agents mathematically prove that core parts of Signal’s encryption work as promised, in four weeks instead of years

DeepMind’s new AlphaProof Code system checked the compiled machine code of libsignal, the library behind the encryption in Signal and also used by WhatsApp and Google Messages. It proved key guarantees, such as that old messages stay safe even if a key is later stolen, using about 6.5 million lines of machine-checked proofs (Google DeepMind, WIRED, The Next Web).

No new flaws turned up. As a test, the team ran it on old Signal versions with known, long-fixed bugs, and it correctly refused to verify them. Signal’s chief technology officer said it gives the company “even greater confidence.” Humans still reviewed the goals at key points; researcher Swarat Chaudhuri said they didn’t want AI writing both the test and the answer (WIRED, The Decoder).

Why it matters: AI is being used to find software bugs faster than people can fix them; tools that prove code is correct could help defenders catch up.

Sources (4)
  • Google DeepMind: Introducing AlphaProof Code
  • WIRED: A new AI tool from Google DeepMind proved the Signal protocol is secure
  • The Next Web: Google DeepMind’s AI agents formally verify Signal’s encryption code
  • The Decoder: AlphaProof Code verified Signal’s cryptographic core in four weeks
Community Note

It didn’t verify the whole Signal app, group chats or the underlying maths of the encryption, which were assumed to be sound.

DeepMind did the work for free as research and won’t say what it cost or whether it will sell the service.

Sources: Google DeepMind; WIRED; The Next Web; The Decoder
