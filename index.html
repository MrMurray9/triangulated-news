Alberta

Alberta’s hospital minister tells UCP members abortion access is “under review,” and hours later the party says it isn’t

Adriana LaGrange, Alberta’s minister for hospital and surgical health services, told about 2,000 members at the UCP’s annual meeting in Calgary on Friday that the province is reviewing access to abortion and that she expects to be briefed “in the very near future” (The Globe and Mail; Western Standard).

LaGrange, who said she is Catholic and spent 26 years with a Red Deer group that opposes abortion, called it “a disgrace” that Canada has no law protecting the unborn, but added that the procedure is protected federally and Alberta “has to make it available” (The Globe and Mail).

Late Friday, a UCP spokesperson said Alberta is not reviewing its abortion policies and that no change or review has been announced (CTV News Calgary). Premier Danielle Smith has said before that her government would not change laws on a woman’s right to choose.

Why it matters: Abortion has been off the UCP’s agenda, so a minister saying it’s under review, followed by a denial the same night, leaves open questions about what the government is actually looking at.

Sources (3)
  • The Globe and Mail: Alberta government to review access to abortion, health minister says
  • CTV News Calgary: Alberta not reviewing abortion policies, UCP spokesperson says
  • Western Standard: LaGrange says abortion policy review under way in Alberta
Community Note

The two statements conflict: the minister said a review is under way, and a party spokesperson said there is none. Neither side has released any review documents.

Under federal law, abortion is legal across Canada. Provinces run and fund the services, which is where access can differ.

Sources: The Globe and Mail; CTV News Calgary; Western Standard.

Interpol joins the hunt for two Edmonton kids missing since August, as their father now faces kidnapping charges

Edmonton police say Interpol has issued a Yellow Notice, a worldwide missing-person alert, for Lesley, 9, and George, 6, who disappeared with their father, Scot MacDermid, 43, from north Edmonton on Aug. 28 (Edmonton Police Service; Global News).

MacDermid’s charges have been upgraded from abduction in breach of a custody order to two counts of kidnapping. He is also wanted on Canada-wide warrants for child endangerment, intimidation, assault causing bodily harm and 18 firearms offences tied to 2022 (Edmonton Police Service; CTV News Edmonton).

Three people have been charged with helping him evade police: Theodore Fidelak, 44, the man seen with the family in Lloydminster, plus Melissa Beck, 42, and Deanne Elliott, 60. Police believe people “in Canada and abroad” are still helping him (Global News).

Why it matters: Police now believe the father may have left Canada or will try to, so the search for the two children has gone international.

  • CTV News Edmonton: Interpol involved in kidnapping case of 2 Edmonton children

Canada

Ottawa’s first national forestry plan puts $6.5 billion toward moving mills from two-by-fours to biofuels and engineered wood

Natural Resources Minister Tim Hodgson released Canada’s first federal strategy for the forest sector on Friday, a “Vision 2050” plan backed by $6.5 billion that was first set aside in Budget 2026 (Natural Resources Canada; The Globe and Mail).

The plan puts about $4 billion toward new or rebuilt large facilities, $2 billion toward modernizing existing mills, and $500 million toward securing a reliable wood supply. The goal is to move beyond lumber, pulp and paper into higher-value products like biofuels, biochemicals and engineered building materials (Natural Resources Canada; POLITICO).

“Status quo is not an option,” Hodgson said, as the industry deals with U.S. softwood tariffs that leaders now describe as a lasting part of doing business with the United States (Means & Ways; POLITICO).

Why it matters: Forestry supports jobs in hundreds of small towns, including many in Alberta, and mill closures from tariffs have been hitting those communities hard.

Sources (4)
  • Natural Resources Canada: Minister Hodgson unveils first-ever federal strategy for Canada’s forest sector
  • The Globe and Mail: Ottawa outlines $6.5-billion plan to reinvent struggling forestry sector
  • POLITICO Canada Playbook: Hodgson’s forestry strategy
  • Means & Ways: ‘Status quo is not an option,’ says Hodgson
Community Note

The $6.5 billion is not new money; it was announced in the budget. What’s new is the strategy that says how it will be spent.

The plan also says Ottawa will look at ways to lower insurance costs for mass-timber buildings, but it gives no details yet.

Sources: Natural Resources Canada; The Globe and Mail; POLITICO; Means & Ways.

Carney orders all Liberal MPs to back his omnibus Bill C-39, a day after his House leader said that wouldn’t be needed

Speaking to reporters in Yellowknife on Friday, Prime Minister Mark Carney said Liberal MPs will “vote as a government” on Bill C-39, which makes it a whipped vote (CTV News; iPolitics).

Carney called the omnibus bill, which includes measures to speed up major projects, “the most important economic legislation that we have” (iPolitics; The Canadian Press).

Only a day earlier, Government House Leader Steven MacKinnon called the bill a “slam dunk with our own MPs” and said a whipped vote wouldn’t be necessary (CTV News; iPolitics).

Why it matters: Whipping the vote suggests the government is worried some of its own MPs could break ranks on a bill critics say gives Ottawa too much power to fast-track projects.

Estonia will shut all five road crossings into Russia on Tuesday, and people are queuing more than a day to get through first

From Dec. 1, Estonia is suspending crossings at all of its road checkpoints with Russia, Narva-1, Narva-2, Koidula, Luhamaa and Saatse, for six months, after which the government will review the decision (ERR News; Estonian Police and Border Guard Board).

Prime Minister Kristen Michal said Russia’s “hostile activities are showing no signs of abating,” pointing to more than 10 attacks against Estonia recorded by its security agencies, including an attempted arson attack this month (ERR News; Reuters).

Ahead of the deadline, long lines have formed at Narva, where some travellers have waited more than 24 hours to cross (Euronews; DW News). Rail freight through Narva will keep running under existing controls.

Why it matters: Narva’s crossing is one of the EU’s last direct road links to Russia, and closing it cuts off families split across the border, many of them Russian speakers in eastern Estonia.

Business

U.S. regulators shut Indiana’s United Fidelity Bank, and First Citizens takes over its $4.5 billion in deposits overnight

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency closed United Fidelity Bank of Evansville, Indiana, on Friday and named the FDIC as receiver, saying the bank was in an unsafe or unsound condition (OCC; FDIC).

North Carolina’s First Citizens Bank won the bidding and is taking on about $5.9 billion in assets, $4.5 billion in deposits and 22 branches in Indiana, Illinois, Colorado, Florida and the U.S. Virgin Islands. Its winning bid included an asset discount of about $2.6 billion (First Citizens BancShares; FDIC).

It’s the first Evansville bank to fail since Integra Bank in 2011, the Evansville Courier & Press reports. The branches open Saturday as First Citizens, and customers’ cards, cheques and direct deposits keep working.

Why it matters: Mid-size bank failures are rare, and this one shows how the FDIC tries to hand a failed bank to a buyer quickly so depositors don’t lose access to their money.

Sources (4)

  • FDIC: First-Citizens Bank assumes deposits of United Fidelity Bank
  • First Citizens BancShares: Whole bank purchase of United Fidelity Bank
  • Evansville Courier & Press: Federal government closes Evansville-based United Fidelity Bank
Community Note

Insured depositors don’t lose any money in this kind of sale. First Citizens is the same bank that bought Silicon Valley Bank after it failed in 2023.

Regulators haven’t publicly said in detail what caused the failure.

Sources: OCC; FDIC; First Citizens BancShares; Evansville Courier & Press.

S&P keeps France’s A+ credit rating but warns of a downgrade, switching its outlook to negative

S&P Global Ratings affirmed France’s A+ rating late Friday but changed its outlook from stable to negative, meaning a downgrade is possible within about two years (France 24; Reuters).

The agency pointed to rising debt, weak growth, higher interest costs and a divided parliament that make it harder to cut the deficit. It expects a deficit of about 5.3% of GDP in 2027 (Reuters; Xinhua).

S&P said it could lower the rating if deficits don’t come down meaningfully over the next two years (France 24; Reuters).

Why it matters: A weaker rating can raise what France pays to borrow, and France is the eurozone’s second-largest economy.

Science corner: Stanford’s ultra-thin niobium phosphide wires carry current better than copper, and get better as they shrink

Every chip hides kilometres of copper wiring, and at the smallest sizes copper starts to choke: its electrons scrape against the wire walls, wasting energy as heat. A Stanford-led team reports in Nature that ribbons of niobium phosphide, just 5–6 nanometres wide and about 1.5 nanometres thick, conduct better than copper at that scale (Stanford Report; Nature).

The trick is that niobium phosphide is a “topological semimetal.” Much of its current flows along the surfaces and edges instead of the core, so making the wire skinnier actually lowers its resistance. Senior author Eric Pop compares copper to a pipe that turns into a rough straw; the new material’s walls are “just electrically more slippery” (Stanford Report; Science).

The ribbons worked at room temperature, despite roughness and oxidation from fabrication, and were made with industry-standard methods, with TSMC among the collaborators. Next, the team will test how long they last (Stanford Report; Interesting Engineering).

Why it matters: Wiring losses are a growing share of the energy chips burn, especially in AI data centres, so a better tiny wire could mean faster, cooler, more efficient processors.

Sources (4)
  • Stanford Report: Tiny wires could make computer chips more efficient
  • Nature: Unconventional resistivity reduction in NbP semimetal nanoribbons
  • Science: Strange ‘topological’ wires conduct electricity better the thinner they get
  • Interesting Engineering: New 6-nm niobium phosphide wires could replace copper
Community Note

This is a lab result, not a product. Durability, temperature behaviour and fitting it into chip factories still need testing, the researchers say.

Sources: Stanford Report; Nature; Science (AAAS); Interesting Engineering.

OpenAI countersues Apple, claiming it stole device secrets through a returning engineer and leaned on shared suppliers

OpenAI has filed counterclaims in federal court in California, accusing Apple of obtaining trade secrets about OpenAI’s unreleased hardware through a former employee who went back to Apple and briefed staff on OpenAI’s device plans (Reuters; Decrypt).

OpenAI also alleges Apple pressured suppliers the two companies share to reveal OpenAI projects or stop working on them, including pushing one supplier to move OpenAI work to a separate overnight shift (Decrypt; Arab News).

Apple denies the claims and calls them a distraction from the lawsuit it filed against OpenAI in July (Reuters; Arab News).

Why it matters: OpenAI is building its own AI devices, and this fight with Apple is about who controls the talent and the factories that make consumer hardware.

Israel fast-tracks AI data centres as national infrastructure, but its power grid caps each one at 200 megawatts

Israel’s government has classified big data centres as national infrastructure, letting them skip some local planning steps and get approved faster, as part of a push to become an “AI superpower” (Al Jazeera; Government of Israel).

The flagship Ofek project near Ashdod is planned at 130 megawatts at first, growing to 200 megawatts, with completion targeted for late 2029 (Data Center Dynamics).

Electricity is the bottleneck: requests to connect to the grid far exceed available power, so Israel’s Electricity Authority is proposing a 200-megawatt cap on each new facility (Calcalist Ctech; Al Jazeera).

Why it matters: Countries are racing to build AI computing at home, and Israel’s plan shows how quickly power supply becomes the limit.

Sources (3)
  • Al Jazeera: How Israel is fast-tracking data centres in bid to become ‘AI superpower’
  • Calcalist Ctech: Israel to cap new data centers at 200 megawatts
  • DCD: Dalia Energy plans Israel’s ‘biggest data center’
Community Note

The 200-megawatt cap is a proposal from the Electricity Authority and hasn’t been finalized. Critics quoted by Al Jazeera also raise concerns about the military use of the computing power.

Sources: Al Jazeera; Calcalist Ctech; Data Center Dynamics; Government of Israel.
