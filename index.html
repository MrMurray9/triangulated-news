Alberta

Alberta’s new riding maps clear their last debate, and the NDP says it will take them to court as soon as they become law

Bill 40, the Electoral Divisions Act, passed third reading in the legislature Wednesday. It still needs royal assent. The maps would grow the legislature from 87 seats to 91 before the 2027 election (The Canadian Press via Global News; MyParliament bill tracker).

In Lethbridge, the two city ridings would become four: one fully urban and three “hybrid” ridings that join parts of the city to rural areas, some reaching the U.S. border. Urban ridings in Calgary and Edmonton would hold about 6% more people on average than others, while rural ridings would be smaller (The Canadian Press; MyLethbridgeNow).

NDP Leader Naheed Nenshi called the maps “illegal” and “unconstitutional” and said details of the court challenge come at a news conference Thursday. Earlier Wednesday, NDP deputy leader Rakhi Pancholi was escorted out of question period for saying the government was cheating, a word the Speaker had ruled out of order (Global News). Justice Minister Mickey Amery said courts have upheld hybrid ridings where they preserve effective representation (The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: These maps decide how much each Albertan’s vote counts in the 2027 election. A court fight this close to an election could leave Elections Alberta preparing for two different maps.

  • MyLethbridgeNow: Nenshi threatens court challenge as Alberta electoral boundary bill advances
  • Lethbridge Herald: Nenshi, Miyashiro rally Lethbridge crowd against electoral map changes
Community Note

The bill isn’t law yet: royal assent is the last step, and Nenshi has said a challenge can only be filed after that.

The government threw out the original boundaries commission’s main recommendations and had a new panel, with two NDP and two UCP appointees and a retired judge as chair, redraw the map. Sixty-three Alberta professors signed a letter warning the process sets a dangerous precedent (The Canadian Press).

Edmonton will host the Grey Cup again in 2028, its sixth time and first since 2018

The CFL announced Wednesday that the Edmonton Elks will host the 115th Grey Cup at Commonwealth Stadium (Play Alberta Field) on Sunday, Nov. 5, 2028 (CFL; Edmonton Elks).

Edmonton has hosted five times before: 1984, 1997, 2002, 2010 and 2018. CFL commissioner Stewart Johnston called the bid “a statement about the passion of football in the City of Edmonton” (CFL). The province also announced the win, along with a separate one bringing the BreakOut West music festival and conference to Edmonton for the first time in a decade (Government of Alberta).

The Grey Cup comes with a week-long festival, and Elks president Chris Morris said the goal is to leave “our city and Canadian football stronger long after the final whistle” (CFL).

Why it matters: Grey Cup week fills hotels, restaurants and downtown with visitors from across the country, so it’s a big tourism weekend for the city in November 2028.

Canada

Senators change Ottawa’s lawful-access bill to guard against forced security holes, but keep six months of metadata storage

Bill C-22 would make tech companies build in ways to preserve data and hand it to police and CSIS with a warrant, and would let the public safety minister order providers to keep user metadata, such as who contacted whom and when, for up to six months (The Globe and Mail; Tech Times).

This week the Senate’s national security committee went through it clause by clause. It widened the meaning of a “systemic vulnerability,” which companies can’t be forced to create, to cover a credible risk that secure information could be accessed. It also let providers ask the minister to consult a new technical advisory committee before an order takes effect (The Globe and Mail; Chamber of Progress).

Senators rejected an amendment from Sen. Colin Deacon that would have stopped Ottawa forcing companies to keep metadata they don’t already collect (Tech Times; Michael Geist). The head of Toronto VPN company Windscribe has told senators he’ll move it out of Canada if the bill passes in its current form (TechRadar).

Why it matters: Almost everyone uses services that would be covered. The bill sets how much of your phone and internet data companies must store and when they must hand it over.

Sources (5)

  • Tech Times: Senate panel curbs monitoring-tool orders, rejects data retention cap
  • Michael Geist: The Bill C-22 surveillance map

  • Chamber of Progress: Senate committee strengthens Bill C-22
Community Note

The bill isn’t law. The full Senate still has to vote, and any amendments go back to the House of Commons, where the Liberals can accept or reject them.

Michael Geist is a University of Ottawa law professor who has criticized the bill, and Chamber of Progress is a tech-industry policy group, so both have a point of view; the facts above match the Globe’s reporting.

Carney answers the Pentagon’s attack on Canada’s military: Trump “has not raised” it with him, and the U.S. warns of “mitigation measures”

What changed since the last edition: Tuesday morning’s edition reported the Pentagon’s “fact sheet” calling Canada an unreliable defence partner. Since then, Prime Minister Mark Carney has responded for the first time, and a senior Pentagon official has threatened further steps.

Asked Wednesday before a Liberal caucus meeting, Carney said: “I can assure you that in my discussions with the president of the United States, we’re focused on conflict resolution around the world and he has not raised issues such as has been reported recently” (The Globe and Mail; The Epoch Times).

He said Canada is “taking responsibility” for its defence, pointing to its NATO mission in Latvia and Arctic work with allies, and making “historic investments in our military,” starting with pay for troops (The Epoch Times).

A senior Pentagon official said the U.S. could impose “mitigation measures” if Canada doesn’t turn its spending pledges into investments that matter for North American defence, adding “we’re postured to up the ante.” Defence Minister David McGuinty said Tuesday that “we don’t report to the United States” and that Canada is on track to spend the equivalent of 4% of GDP on defence by 2030 (BetaVoices).

Why it matters: The dispute comes as Canada tries to buy more of its military equipment at home rather than from the U.S., and as trade talks with Washington are set to resume.

A fuel tanker overturns and burns on Cairo’s Ring Road, killing 31 people, and police hold the driver they say clipped it

The crash happened Wednesday on the Ring Road in Warraq, in Giza on Cairo’s western edge. Egypt’s prosecutors said 31 people were killed and 21 hurt, and the fire spread to about 30 vehicles, including cars, minibuses, taxis and motorcycles. Witnesses said most of the dead were in one minibus (Daily News Egypt; AFP; The Associated Press).

Prosecutors said Thursday they had detained a car driver who admitted going 110 km/h and weaving between lanes. They said his car hit the back of the tanker, a tanker tire burst, and the truck struck a concrete barrier and rolled, spilling fuel that caught fire (Daily News Egypt; Egypt Today; Arab News).

Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly visited the injured in hospital and said he would ask banks and businesses to help compensate families, including replacement cars for those who lost theirs (Daily News Egypt).

Why it matters: It’s one of Egypt’s deadliest road disasters in years, on one of greater Cairo’s busiest highways, and it adds pressure over road safety and fuel trucks on city roads.

Sixteen miners are dead and 10 still missing after flooding traps 26 people in an illegal gold mine in Indonesia

Heavy rain flooded an unlicensed gold mine in Bogor regency, West Java, trapping 26 workers in shafts about 100 metres deep. By Thursday, rescuers had pulled out 16 bodies and were still searching for 10 people (The Associated Press via SBS News; CNBC Indonesia).

Rescuers are pumping water out of two tunnels, but high water, narrow and slippery passages and poor light are slowing them. Police didn’t get reports of the trapped miners until about 16 hours after the flooding (SBS News; Xinhua).

Officials say the chance of finding anyone alive is getting slimmer (The Associated Press; India Today).

Why it matters: Illegal gold mining is common in Indonesia and deadly accidents keep happening because the pits are dug without safety rules or permits.

Bangkok’s emergency medical centre reported seven injured, four critically (Bangkok Post; Thai PBS World). AP and France 24 later put the number hurt at nine.

The energy minister’s office said the fire started in equipment attached to a diesel distillation tower, not a storage tank. A district office urged nearby residents to leave and students were moved out of nearby schools, but officials later said air quality was normal and no evacuation order was issued (Thai PBS World; Bangkok Post).

Why it matters: The refinery sits inside the city and can process up to 120,000 barrels a day, so a fire there is a risk to the people living around it and to Thailand’s fuel supply.

Business

Washington proposes new tariffs of mostly 15–20% on 14 economies it accuses of overbuilding factories, and Canada isn’t on the list

The U.S. Trade Representative released findings Wednesday from 14 investigations into what it calls “structural excess capacity” in manufacturing, and proposed tariffs for each economy (USTR; Reuters).

The proposed rates are 15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan; up to 18% for Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand; up to 20% for China and Singapore; up to 25% for India; and up to 35% for Vietnam (USTR; S&P Global).

Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC most rates aren’t added on top of existing duties: “In effect, it’ll look more like a top up.” China is the exception. A senior official said its 20% would stack on older tariffs of about 25%, taking U.S. duties on Chinese goods to roughly 45%. Greer said none would take effect for at least a couple of months (CNBC).

Why it matters: They come months after the U.S. Supreme Court struck down Trump’s broad “liberation day” tariffs, and they shape prices and supply chains for many goods Canadians buy too.

Sources (4)
  • USTR: Determinations and proposed actions in 14 Section 301 investigations
  • CNBC: U.S. proposes new tariffs on 14 trading partners, including China and the EU
  • Reuters: Trump unveils new tariffs over excess industrial capacity
  • S&P Global: US proposes tariffs on 14 economies over excess manufacturing capacity
Community Note

These are proposals, not final tariffs. Section 301 cases normally include a public comment period before rates are set.

China’s foreign ministry said “the so-called ‘overcapacity’ does not really exist.” Canada is not one of the 14, but separate U.S. tariff threats against Canada are still on the table ahead of trade talks.

Sources: USTR; CNBC; Reuters; S&P Global

Christie’s buys Dreweatts, a 267-year-old English country auction house, betting on antiques again

Christie’s said Wednesday it is acquiring Dreweatts, founded in 1759 and based at Donnington Priory near Newbury, Berkshire. The price wasn’t disclosed (Christie’s; The Art Newspaper; Antiques Trade Gazette).

Dreweatts holds about 70 sales a year, known for country-house sales, furniture, Old Masters, silver, clocks and wine. It reported record auction turnover of £37.56 million in 2025, up 26% from 2024 (The Art Newspaper).

Dreweatts keeps its name, staff and saleroom, and chief executive Henry House and chairman Will Richards will keep running it (Christie’s; Dreweatts).

Why it matters: It’s a turn for Christie’s, which closed its South Kensington saleroom for antiques and traditional collecting in 2017 to chase the high-end contemporary art market (The Art Newspaper).

Science corner

Science corner: a gallium oxide power switch from Hong Kong handles a megawatt in pulses, about 100 times more than earlier ones

Power semiconductors are the switches that control electricity in chargers, electric cars and the power grid. Most are silicon, which is close to its limits. Engineers at the University of Hong Kong, working with Virginia Tech and Nanjing University, built a module from six gallium oxide chips that repeatedly switched 1,000 volts and 1,000 amps a thousand times a second, a peak of one megawatt (HKU; Nature Communications).

Gallium oxide handles huge voltages but traps heat. The team designed the chip and its package as one system: it is cooled from the side where the heat is made, and a layer of barium titanate helps it hold off high voltage (HKU; Nature Communications).

HKU says it’s the first module of this kind of material to reach that power level, more than 100 times past earlier demonstrations (HKU).

Why it matters: Better high-power switches could mean smaller, more efficient equipment for fast EV chargers, power grids and pulsed-power uses like medical and industrial equipment.

Sources (4)
  • HKU: HKU engineers build world-first power module using emerging semiconductor material
  • Nature Communications: A megawatt ultra-wide bandgap semiconductor module for pulsed power electronics
  • The HKU Lead: World’s first megawatt ultra-wide bandgap semiconductor module
  • VLSI.kr: Gallium oxide 1 megawatt module — peak pulsed power, not continuous output
Community Note

The megawatt is a peak during short pulses, not continuous output. The module can’t run a megawatt load nonstop.

This is a lab demonstration in a peer-reviewed paper, not a product on sale.

Sources: Nature Communications; HKU; VLSI.kr

AI news and advances

U.S. regulators let SpaceX launch up to a million satellites meant to work as AI data centres in orbit

The Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday approved “Starmind,” SpaceX’s plan for up to one million low-orbit “orbital compute modules” powered by the sun (NPR; NBC News; Ars Technica).

FCC chair Brendan Carr said “America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence.” Under the order, SpaceX must launch its first satellite by 2033, reach 100,000 by 2035 and 500,000 by 2038, and finish by 2040 (NPR; SiliconANGLE; FCC order).

Some scientists doubt it will work. Astrophysicist Adam Becker called it “a fantasy,” saying it’s hard to get rid of heat in a vacuum and warning of “a cascading set of space junk collisions” in crowded low Earth orbit (NPR).

Why it matters: AI data centres on Earth use huge amounts of power and water. This is the biggest bet yet that the work can move to space, and it affects SpaceX, whose stock we track below.

Sources (4)
  • NPR: FCC says SpaceX can deploy 1 million satellites for data centers in space
  • NBC News: SpaceX gets green light to deploy 1 million satellites for data centers in space
  • Ars Technica: The FCC has authorized SpaceX to deploy 1 million satellites
  • SiliconANGLE: FCC clears SpaceX to put up to 1 million Starmind data centers in space
Community Note

An FCC licence is permission, not proof it can be built. SpaceX has until 2033 to launch even the first one, and scientists quoted by NPR doubt the physics and economics.

The approval covers radio links for tracking and control. The satellites would mainly talk to each other with lasers.

Sources: NPR; NBC News; Ars Technica; SiliconANGLE
