World juniors warm-ups are coming to six Alberta towns: Team Canada plays Finland and Sweden in Red Deer before the tournament

Hockey Canada on Thursday released the pre-tournament schedule for the 2027 IIHF World Junior Championship: 12 exhibition games from Dec. 17 to 23 in Cold Lake, Blackfalds, Red Deer, Camrose, Lloydminster and Edmonton. Canada, which will hold its selection camp in Calgary, faces Finland on Dec. 18 at 7:30 p.m. and Sweden on Dec. 20 at 5 p.m., both at Red Deer’s Marchant Crane Centrium, then meets Sweden again at Rogers Place on Dec. 22 (Red Deer News Now, Sportsnet/The Canadian Press, CFWE).

The smaller centres get marquee matchups too: Cold Lake opens the slate with Germany vs. Switzerland on Dec. 17, Camrose has Switzerland vs. Slovakia (Dec. 19) and Switzerland vs. Finland (Dec. 22), Lloydminster gets Latvia vs. Germany (Dec. 19), and Blackfalds hosts three games including Czechia vs. USA on Dec. 23. Red Deer’s four games also include Slovakia–Czechia and USA–Latvia. Tickets go through HockeyCanada.ca or local box offices; Blackfalds sales open Oct. 27 on Showpass. The tournament itself runs Dec. 26 to Jan. 5 in Edmonton and Red Deer.

Why it matters: It spreads the world juniors beyond the two host cities — and gives families in smaller Alberta towns a rare, affordable chance to see future NHL players in their home rinks.

Wainwright fire started on a grenade-launcher range, military police say — and the Board of Inquiry could take up to a year

At a briefing in Edmonton on Thursday, Provost Marshal Brig.-Gen. Vanessa Hanrahan said the wildfire that swept through troops at CFB Wainwright began during training on the automatic grenade launcher range, and that the range’s location kept firefighters from knocking it down before the wind shifted. About 30 military police investigators are on the case; officials said natural gas was not a factor. Chief of the Defence Staff Gen. Jennie Carignan said she was “heartbroken” (The Canadian Press via InfoNews, CTV News).

National Defence says a Board of Inquiry could take nine to 12 months and its findings will be made public; Defence Minister David McGuinty said its terms of reference are still being drafted. Twenty-seven soldiers remain in hospital, 13 of them in critical condition, and 12 have been discharged. Ontario is sending 28 burn-care health workers and equipment to Alberta, Premier Doug Ford said (Toronto Sun). The four soldiers who died — Sgt. Morgan Wong and Ptes. Shaun Farrell, Aidan Goulding and Hayden Lykkemark — served with the 3rd Battalion, Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry.

Why it matters: It is one of the deadliest training accidents in the Canadian Armed Forces in decades; the investigation will decide whether range-safety and fire rules change for every base.

What changed since the last edition: This tragedy was last covered in an earlier edition. New today: the military named the grenade-launcher range as the fire’s point of origin, gave a nine-to-12-month timeline for the Board of Inquiry, updated the hospital count, and Ontario committed burn specialists.

Referendum count blows past its 48-hour deadline, and “No” now leads four constitutional questions — including Senate abolition

Elections Alberta missed its 10:59 p.m. Wednesday target for finishing the count; by 8:30 a.m. Thursday about 19.4 million ballot answers had been tallied from 97 to 98 per cent of voting locations, with more than 47,000 workers involved. Official results, including turnout, are due Oct. 29 (Edmonton Journal, CityNews Edmonton).

As the last ballots came in, “No” moved ahead on questions six through nine — on how judges are chosen, abolishing the Senate, opting out of federal programs with compensation, and giving provincial law priority — after leading only two of them Wednesday evening. The judges question was running about 54 per cent “No.” “Yes” still leads the five immigration-related questions, including citizenship ID to vote (about 58 per cent), and about 75 per cent chose to remain in Canada. “It’s certainly a clear loss on those questions,” University of Alberta economist Andrew Leach said; Premier Danielle Smith said Albertans “would just rather that we spend our time focusing on things where we can make some meaningful change” (CTV News Edmonton, The Canadian Press via The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: The constitutional questions were the government’s mandate for confronting Ottawa; “No” results weaken that push, while the immigration results give Smith cover to keep pressing on newcomers and voter ID.

What changed since the last edition: On Oct. 20 we reported “Yes” leading eight of the nine other ballot questions. Since then the counting deadline was missed and late ballots flipped four constitutional questions to “No.”

The Supreme Court will have the last word on whether Ottawa lawfully invoked the Emergencies Act against the 2022 convoy protests

The Supreme Court of Canada agreed Thursday to hear the federal government’s appeal of rulings that its use of the Emergencies Act during the Freedom Convoy protests was unreasonable and violated the Charter. As usual, it gave no reasons. It will also hear cross-appeals from the Canadian Civil Liberties Association and the Canadian Constitution Foundation (Global News/The Canadian Press, The Globe and Mail, National Post).

Federal Court Justice Richard Mosley ruled in January 2024 that there was no national emergency justifying the Act; the Federal Court of Appeal unanimously upheld that in January 2026. A separate public inquiry, the Public Order Emergency Commission, concluded in 2023 that the legal threshold had been met. A hearing isn’t expected before spring 2027 and could slip to the fall.

Why it matters: It will be the first time Canada’s top court defines when a government may use emergency powers — a precedent for every future federal crisis, from blockades to pandemics.

Granting leave says nothing about how the court will rule. The Act was invoked for nine days in February 2022 under the Trudeau government; the current government chose to keep the appeal going. Rulings so far have split: two courts said the invocation was unreasonable, while the commission said it met the threshold.

Sources: The Canadian Press; The Globe and Mail; National Post.

Ottawa gives Stelco’s U.S. owner 15 days to reverse up to 500 Hamilton layoffs or face court action

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Thursday she has written to Cleveland-Cliffs, demanding it walk back planned layoffs of up to 500 workers at Stelco’s Hamilton operations within 15 days or the government will go to court. When Cliffs bought Stelco in 2024 it made Investment Canada Act undertakings, including keeping unionized jobs at no fewer than 1,766. “Cleveland-Cliffs has repeatedly failed to demonstrate that they are compliant with their undertakings. That is unacceptable,” Joly said (Global News, CTV News, The Globe and Mail).

Cliffs argued in an Oct. 9 letter that its commitments don’t prohibit layoffs and blamed 50 per cent U.S. steel tariffs; the union says only about 40 jobs were offered at the Lake Erie works. Remedies under the Act can include court-ordered penalties of up to $10,000 a day per breach or, in extreme cases, forced divestiture (Reuters).

Why it matters: It’s the first big test of whether foreign buyers’ job promises under the Investment Canada Act can actually be enforced while tariffs batter Canada’s steel industry.

Magnitude-7.0 earthquake hits a remote part of Tibet late at night, killing at least 16 and flattening hundreds of homes

The quake struck Baqen (Baqing) County in Nagqu, in China’s Tibet Autonomous Region, at 10:33 p.m. local time Thursday — 8:33 a.m. in Edmonton. By early Friday local time, 16 people had died and 32 were injured, 11 seriously, and 339 houses had collapsed, according to Xinhua. China activated a Level-IV emergency response and sent fire and railway rescue crews into the freezing high-altitude area (Xinhua, AP, Reuters).

The U.S. Geological Survey put the magnitude at 7.0, centred about 191 kilometres east-northeast of Nagqu city. The region is sparsely populated, which limits the toll, but cold, darkness and damaged mountain roads slow rescuers (USGS, AP).

Why it matters: It is the strongest quake in Tibet since the deadly January 2025 Dingri earthquake, and the first hours of rescue in such remote terrain decide how many survivors are found.

Finland licenses the world’s first permanent underground tomb for spent nuclear fuel

The Finnish government on Thursday granted Posiva an operating licence for Onkalo, a repository about 430 metres deep in bedrock at Olkiluoto, in Eurajoki, that will hold up to 6,500 tonnes of uranium in spent fuel from the Olkiluoto and Loviisa power plants. No other country has licensed a final deep geological disposal site (Posiva, NucNet, Yle).

Disposal won’t begin right away: the nuclear regulator STUK must first verify commissioning tests, and no start date has been set. STUK issued its safety assessment in August; Posiva, owned by utilities TVO and Fortum, applied in December 2021, capping a process that began more than 40 years ago. The facility is designed to operate for more than a century before being sealed (Posiva, Daily Finland).

Why it matters: Where to put nuclear waste for 100,000 years is the industry’s great unsolved problem; Finland’s licence gives other countries — including Canada, which picked a site near Ignace, Ont., in 2024 — a working model.

Science corner

A shirt sleeve that works like a phone screen: NUS engineers weave a washable, touch-sensitive colour display out of thread

In a paper published in Nature on Oct. 21, a team led by Asst. Prof. Tural Khudiyev at the National University of Singapore made fibres that act as both display pixels and touch sensors. Woven together — 54 pixel fibres carrying 96 LEDs each — they form a 96-by-54-pixel colour screen about 17 by 10.5 centimetres on a sleeve, refreshing at 30 frames per second and responding to more than one finger at once (NUS, Nature).

The trick is manufacturing: the fibres are drawn from a heated preform like glass, producing threads about a millimetre thick and up to 10 metres long, with more than 5,000 chips and 20,000 electrical connections made by laser and controlled solder. Companion fibres add a microphone, speaker and motion, light and temperature sensors. The garment survived 50 machine washes and 1,000 tight bends (NUS, Nature news, TechXplore).

Why it matters: Clothing that can show maps or messages and take taps could help workers who need their hands free, people with disabilities, and soldiers or first responders — and it’s washable, which past e-textiles weren’t.

Sources (4)
  • NUS News — World’s first touchscreen in a garment
  • Nature — the research paper
  • Nature news — coverage of the study
  • TechXplore — Thread-based touchscreen garment
Community Note

It’s a lab prototype, not a product; the resolution is far below a phone’s, and no cost, battery life or commercial timeline has been given. The “world first” claim is the university’s own; Nature’s peer review covers the science, not the marketing.

Sources: NUS; Nature; TechXplore.

AI news and advances

Canva launches Craft, its first home-built AI image model, trained on millions of human-made templates

Canva on Thursday released Craft, an image model that first plans a layout and then generates it, aiming at designs with legible text and precise edits rather than one-off pictures. It outputs up to 2K resolution in more than 30 aspect ratios and is available to all users through Canva’s Dream Lab and to developers through an API; researchers from Canva-owned Leonardo.ai helped build it (Canva, TechCrunch, The Verge).

Canva says faster “Craft-fast” and “ultrafast” versions are coming, along with access inside ChatGPT and Gemini through the Model Context Protocol. On the Design Arena leaderboard it ranked fourth, behind OpenAI’s image models (The Verge, Digital Trends).

Why it matters: Canva has more than 200 million users, many of them teachers and small businesses; owning its model cuts its reliance on OpenAI and Google, and training on licensed templates is a bet against the copyright fights other image models face.
