Alberta

No “fall back” for Alberta this Sunday: clocks stay put for good as the province moves to year-round Alberta Time

For the first time, most Albertans will not set their clocks back an hour on Sunday, Nov. 1. The province is staying on what it calls Alberta Time, which is UTC−6, all year, so the clock you use now is the one you keep through the winter (Government of Alberta, timeanddate.com, The Canadian Press).

Most of British Columbia, Manitoba, part of northwestern Ontario and the Northwest Territories are making the same move. Businesses mostly welcome the end of the twice-yearly change, but economists warn mornings will be darker for longer this winter, which can disrupt sleep and productivity at first (The Canadian Press, Government of Alberta).

Phones and computers that update automatically should handle it, but older devices, car clocks and some calendar apps may still try to “fall back.” Microsoft tells Outlook users to pick the new “Mountain Time (Alberta)” zone and check recurring meetings (Microsoft Support, timeanddate.com).

Why it matters: If a device jumps back an hour on Sunday morning, it is wrong. Meetings with people in Saskatchewan now line up all year, while the gap with Ontario and Quebec grows to one hour in winter.

Canada

Ottawa’s first national-security plan in 20 years says Canada must prepare for threats up to and including war, and names frontier AI as a top danger

Prime Minister Mark Carney released The Plan for a Stronger Canada on Thursday, the country’s first national-security strategy in more than two decades. The 13-page document covers defence, foreign policy, economic security, public safety and intelligence, and says Canada must be ready for everything from cyberattacks and economic coercion to a major war (Prime Minister’s Office, The Globe and Mail, National Post).

Ottawa plans to build its own intelligence-gathering capacity, strengthen cyber defences and widen intelligence partnerships beyond the Five Eyes. Officials singled out frontier artificial intelligence, including models acting beyond what their designers intended and AI-assisted hackers, as one of the gravest emerging threats (National Post, Politico, Privy Council Office).

The plan names eight sectors to protect, including critical minerals, energy, defence, food and payments. The government promises to update national-security laws within a year, hold a first ministers’ meeting on it, and refresh the strategy at least every four years (Privy Council Office, Prime Minister’s Office).

Why it matters: It sets the direction for new spending and new laws on spying, cyber defence and protecting key industries, which could affect everything from tech companies to who can buy Canadian firms.

Hackers steal client files from two of Canada’s biggest law firms, McCarthy Tétrault and Davies

McCarthy Tétrault says it found a coordinated social-engineering attack on Oct. 22 in which an outsider got into some files, including client information, and it began notifying those affected on Oct. 25. A source told The Globe and Mail about 31,000 files were taken, a number the firm has not confirmed (McCarthy Tétrault, The Globe and Mail, The Logic).

Davies Ward Phillips & Vineberg separately confirmed an Oct. 23 attack that exposed information from one account, and is contacting clients. Both firms have called in police and outside cybersecurity experts, amid a wider phishing campaign in which hackers pose as IT staff to target Canada’s legal and financial sectors (The Globe and Mail, La Presse, The Logic).

Why it matters: Big corporate law firms hold confidential details of mergers, lawsuits and deals, so a leak could expose companies and people who never dealt with the hackers directly.

About 2,000 Salvadoran soldiers land in Haiti to hunt for four missing colleagues and take on the gangs

El Salvador has rushed roughly 2,000 troops to Haiti, days after four Salvadoran technicians serving with the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force went missing in a gang ambush. A security official says the deployment will be capped at about 2,500 for now (Reuters, AP, El Mundo).

President Nayib Bukele says the soldiers will help with anti-gang operations, not just the rescue. Haiti also set new election dates this week. But it is still unclear who commands the Salvadorans, what their rules of engagement are, and whether they will fold into the UN force or act alone, which worries rights groups given Haiti’s history with foreign troops (Reuters, AP).

Why it matters: It is the first big test of Bukele’s hard-line anti-gang tactics outside El Salvador, in a country where gangs control much of the capital.

Drones sink a Greek-owned container ship off Bulgaria; all 18 crew are rescued

The Liberian-flagged Contship Jet, chartered by Israeli line Zim and sailing from Haifa to Constanța, Romania, sank in the Black Sea after three sea drones struck it off Bulgaria’s coast on Wednesday. All 18 crew, 14 from the Philippines and four from Ukraine, were rescued and taken to Varna (AP, Maritime Executive, Naftemporiki).

The ship carried 421 containers, some with hazardous cargo. Industry reports blame Russia for the strike. Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis called for an urgent “international truce” to protect Black Sea shipping (TradeWinds, Reuters, AP).

Why it matters: An attack this far from the front lines, off a NATO member’s coast, raises insurance costs and risks for all Black Sea trade, including grain shipments.

Final Israeli count: Netanyahu’s bloc ends at 53 seats, eight short of a majority, and Likud calls the vote “stolen”

What changed: On Wednesday most ballots were counted and the bloc stood at 54 seats. The full count drops it to 53, confirms it has lost its majority, and Likud has started alleging fraud.

With all votes counted, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc won 53 of the Knesset’s 120 seats, short of the 61 needed to govern. Gadi Eisenkot’s opposition bloc took 48 and Arab-led parties 19. Likud is still the biggest single party, with 27 seats (Ynet, JNS, AP).

Likud now claims the election was “stolen,” without offering evidence. Neither camp has a clear path to a majority without the other side’s help or the Arab parties, raising the odds of long coalition talks or even another election (AP, South China Morning Post, Ynet).

Why it matters: Israel’s next government will shape the war in the region and relations with the U.S. and Canada, and right now no one can form one easily.

About US$6.3 billion drained from Binance in the biggest crypto theft ever; investigators point to Pyongyang’s TraderTraitor hackers

Starting around 2:05 p.m. Edmonton time on Thursday, an attacker moved about US$6.3 billion out of Binance wallets on 10 blockchains, most of it within 20 minutes. That is roughly four times the previous record, the US$1.5 billion Bybit hack in 2025 (TRM Labs via BleepingComputer, Chainalysis, AP).

Chainalysis and TRM Labs say with high confidence that North Korea’s TraderTraitor unit, also called Lazarus, did it, after stolen coins were seen mixing with money from last month’s Bitget hack. Binance says the break-in began with a DevOps engineer’s compromised laptop, about US$3 billion has been frozen, deposits are unaffected, and it will cover all losses. Withdrawals are paused and should resume within a day or two (AP, Cointelegraph, BleepingComputer).

Why it matters: Western agencies say stolen crypto helps pay for North Korea’s weapons programs, and the hack may shake confidence in exchanges as Bitcoin slides.

U.S. economy grows at a 3.4 per cent pace, its fastest in a year, days before the midterms

U.S. gross domestic product rose at a 3.4 per cent annual rate from July to September, up from 2.2 per cent in the spring and above the roughly 3.2 per cent economists expected, the Commerce Department’s first estimate shows (U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, USA Today, Axios).

Why it matters: A hot U.S. economy supports Canadian exports but could push U.S. rates higher, which tends to lift Canadian borrowing costs and weigh on the loonie.

Science corner

Science corner: tiny magnets implanted in muscle could let amputees steer a bionic leg more naturally

MIT engineers implant a few three-millimetre magnetic beads into the remaining muscles of a person’s leg. Sensors worn on the skin track how the beads move, so a computer knows how long each muscle is and how fast it is changing, wirelessly and in real time. The team calls the method magnetomicrometry (MIT News, Nature Sensors).

In tests with three people with below-knee amputations, the bead signals matched or beat the electrical muscle sensors that bionic limbs use today, and stayed steady when muscles were held tight for long periods, where electrical readings often drift. The system is now in clinical studies with a bionic ankle-foot (MIT News, Nature Sensors, ClinicalTrials.gov).

Why it matters: Better signals from the body mean a prosthetic that moves more like a real limb, with less of the jerky, delayed response many users live with now.

Sources (3)
  • MIT News — Magnetic sensors track muscle movement, for better prosthetic limb control
  • Nature Sensors — A wireless magnetic implant system for continuous neuromuscular sensing
  • ClinicalTrials.gov — Magnetic bead tracking system with a bionic prosthesis for transtibial amputees
Community Note

The results come from only three participants, so this is early proof that the idea works, not an approved medical device. Larger trials will need to show it is safe over years.

Sources: MIT News; Nature Sensors (peer-reviewed); ClinicalTrials.gov.

Meta quietly tests Style Sketch, an AI app that builds a closet from your Instagram photos and picks outfits from what you already own

Meta has released Style Sketch, a stand-alone app that scans your Instagram photos, or pictures you upload, to build a digital wardrobe. It then suggests outfits from clothes you already have, shows them on a model matched to your body shape and skin tone, and can plan a trip’s outfits around the weather and your schedule (TechCrunch, Gadgets 360, Storyboard18).

For now the app is only on iPhone and Android in Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, a common way for Meta to test products before a wider launch (TechCrunch, Gadgets 360).

Why it matters: It shows big tech pushing AI into everyday choices like getting dressed, and it does that by reading your photos, which raises fresh questions about how much personal data these apps use.

ChatGPT gets the blame: a Flávio Bolsonaro ally says the chatbot slipped “president-elect” into his deleted letter

What changed: Yesterday morning’s edition covered Flávio Bolsonaro flying to Miami and refusing to concede. Since then he posted, then deleted, the “president-elect” letter, and his camp is blaming ChatGPT for the wording.

Flávio Bolsonaro, who lost Brazil’s presidential runoff to Lula by more than four million votes, briefly posted a letter calling himself the country’s “president-elect,” then deleted it about an hour later and swapped in “leader of Brazil” (Reuters, NDTV, O Globo).

His ally Paulo Figueiredo says ChatGPT was responsible: Eduardo Bolsonaro used the chatbot to finish the document, and it pulled in wording and an outdated coat of arms from an earlier draft. Brazilian outlets report a different version too, saying U.S. officials pressed Flávio to drop the claim because it could look like American recognition (Reuters, O Globo, G1).

Why it matters: Whatever the cause, it is a high-profile example of AI text going out unchecked in a tense political moment, and of “the AI did it” becoming an excuse.
