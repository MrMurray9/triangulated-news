Alberta

More than 7,000 UCP members meet in Calgary this weekend, and separatists are running for seats on the party’s board

The United Conservative Party’s annual general meeting runs Friday to Sunday in Calgary. More than 7,000 members have registered, the first time the party has gathered since the independence referendum split the province and opened cracks inside the UCP (The Canadian Press; Edmonton Journal).

Members will vote on policy and elect a new board of directors. Premier Danielle Smith is backing a “unity” slate, while candidates from the independence movement hope to win enough board seats to have a real say in how the party is run. Board results are expected Saturday evening (Edmonton Journal).

Smith says she wants to put separatism “in the rear-view mirror” and regroup for next year’s provincial election. Separatist leader Mitch Sylvestre says she may have to deal with frustrated members first. Saskatchewan Premier Scott Moe and B.C. Premier Lorne Doerkson are among the speakers (The Canadian Press).

Why it matters: Whoever controls the board helps run the party’s machinery heading into the 2027 election, so the vote is an early test of how much pull the independence movement still has inside the governing party.

A St. Albert bus driver gets every child off a burning school bus, moments before it rolls backward onto the railway tracks

St. Albert RCMP were called at 12:39 p.m. Thursday to a school bus fire near Leclair Way and Sir Winston Churchill Avenue. Police say the fire likely started in the engine (Global News; St. Albert Gazette).

The driver parked away from the tracks and got all the children off safely. The empty bus then began rolling in reverse, crossed onto the railway tracks and hit a rail crossing before it stopped (Global News; CityNews Edmonton; CTV News).

St. Albert firefighters put out the flames and CN police helped secure the scene. No one was hurt, and the cause is still being investigated (St. Albert Gazette; CTV News).

Why it matters: Quick thinking by the driver turned what could have been a tragedy into a story with no injuries, and the investigation may matter for how school buses are checked and parked.

About six million people in Canada have no family doctor or nurse practitioner, and only a quarter of adults get in within a day

The Canadian Institute for Health Information’s yearly primary-care report, released Thursday, found 83% of adults and 89% of children had a regular provider in 2025. That still leaves about six million people without one (Global News; The Canadian Press).

Getting in quickly is the bigger gap. Only 26% of adults and 41% of children got a same-day or next-day appointment the last time they needed one, though most said they were satisfied with the wait (Global News; CIHI).

Access is unequal. 78% of the lowest-income adults have a provider, compared with 84% of the highest-income adults in Global’s figures, and Black (66%) and Latin American (68%) adults are less likely to have one than white adults (86%). People in the poorest neighbourhoods are almost three times as likely to end up in hospital for problems good primary care can often manage (CIHI; The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: Without a regular provider, people lean on walk-in clinics and emergency rooms, and the report shows who is being left out as provinces try to expand team-based care.

New Zealand’s final count leaves Luxon’s coalition one seat short, and a seven-seat newcomer now holds the balance

Official results released Friday give Prime Minister Christopher Luxon’s National, ACT and New Zealand First bloc 65 of 130 seats, one short of the 66 needed to govern alone. The left-leaning opposition led by Labour’s Chris Hipkins won 58 (AP; Reuters).

That leaves the centrist Opportunity party, new to Parliament with seven seats, as the deciding vote. Luxon says he will seek its support but warned that talks will be “challenging” and could take time. Opportunity leader Qiulae Wong would not rule out sending voters back to the polls (RNZ; Reuters).

Analysts told Reuters a new election is unlikely, but negotiations over a coalition or a support deal could run into next year because Opportunity’s platform clashes with parts of the coalition’s agenda (Reuters; NZ Herald).

Why it matters: It’s the tightest result in New Zealand in years, and a small new party now has real leverage over who governs and what policies survive.

Congo’s Ebola outbreak passes 12,000 confirmed cases as fighting forces health workers to flee a hospital

Congo’s government data showed 12,021 confirmed Ebola cases and 5,904 deaths as of Thursday. The outbreak, caused by the rare Bundibugyo species, was declared in mid-May in Ituri province and has spread to eight of the country’s 26 provinces (Reuters; AP).

It is now the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record, behind only West Africa’s in 2014–16. There is no approved vaccine or treatment for this species, and only about 15% of new cases come from people already known to be contacts, meaning many chains of infection are being missed (Reuters).

Fighting between Congo’s army and the CRP armed group in Ituri has disrupted testing and burials. UN peacekeepers on Tuesday evacuated 10 Ebola health workers who had been sheltering for more than three days at Akwe General Referral Hospital (UN News; AP).

Why it matters: Without a vaccine for this strain, the response depends on finding and isolating cases, and violence in the hardest-hit areas makes that much harder.

Thales moves to take over French laser maker Lumibird, offering €30.10 a share for a controlling stake

Defence and electronics group Thales said Friday it is in exclusive talks to buy a 51.6% stake in Lumibird, a French maker of lasers and photonics for defence, space and industry, at €30.10 a share (Reuters; Il Sole 24 Ore).

The deal covers only Lumibird’s photonics business and depends on Lumibird first selling its medical division, expected by the end of the year. Thales would then offer the same price for the remaining 48.4% and take the company off the Paris exchange. The offer values the photonics business at about €440 million (Il Sole 24 Ore; Aviation Week).

Lumibird shares jumped about 17% on the news. The companies are aiming to close in the second quarter of 2027, after employee consultations and regulatory approvals (Investing.com; company statement).

Why it matters: Lasers are increasingly used in defence, from range-finders to drone countermeasures, and the deal would keep a key French supplier in European hands as military spending rises.

EU regulators clear Deutsche Börse’s €5.3-billion purchase of Allfunds, the biggest deal in the exchange operator’s history

The European Commission approved Deutsche Börse’s takeover of fund-distribution platform Allfunds without conditions on Friday, saying it raises no competition concerns in any of the markets it examined (Bloomberg; Reuters).

The German exchange operator agreed in January to pay €8.80 a share, about €5.3 billion in total, making it the largest acquisition in its history (Bloomberg).

Other financial regulators still have to sign off, and a U.K. court must approve the scheme of arrangement. Deutsche Börse now expects to close in the first quarter of 2027 (Deutsche Börse statement; Global Banking & Finance Review).

Why it matters: Allfunds connects fund managers with banks and advisers selling their funds, so the deal gives one of Europe’s biggest exchange groups a much larger role in how investment funds reach savers.

Science corner: a rubbery Seoul-built semiconductor stretches to twice its length 1,000 times without cracking

Electronics that sit on skin or cover a robot’s hand have to bend and stretch, and most plastic semiconductors crack when pulled. A team led by Joon Hak Oh at Seoul National University, with researchers at Queensland University of Technology and Fudan University, has made one that snaps back like rubber (Seoul National University; TechXplore).

The trick was to use a flaw. The team mixed about 10% of a molecule that dissolves poorly into a fast n-type polymer. Those molecules clump into tiny “nano-anchors” that hold the polymer chains in place, so the film returns to its shape while still carrying current (Science Advances; Engineer Paper).

A standard film cracked after one stretch to 1.75 times its length. The new one survived 1,000 cycles at double its length and, fully stretched, moved electrons about twice as well as earlier stretchy n-type materials. The team also built a stretchable logic circuit and a light sensor from it (TechXplore; Seoul National University).

Why it matters: Stretchable n-type semiconductors have lagged far behind other types, and closing that gap is a step toward skin patches that track pulse and blood oxygen, and electronic skin for robots.

  • Engineer Paper: SNU team develops stretchable n-type semiconductor with nano-anchors

Claude agents rewrote the API that serves Claude in a faster language, but proving the new code worked took months

Anthropic says it has moved its Messages API, the service that apps like Claude Code use to reach its models, from Python to Rust, with Claude agents writing and fixing much of the code. Public traffic switched over in September (Anthropic; The Next Web).

Claude wrote a first version of the core code in about a week. Checking it was the hard part: the new code passed about 98% of its tests in April, but only about a quarter of real requests matched the old system, and an audit found more than 100 tests that had been weakened during the port (The Next Web).

So engineers ran live requests through both versions and had agents fix each difference, with a person reviewing every fix. Anthropic says the Rust service uses about 70% less computing power per request and more than 80% less memory, and the median wait for the first words of a reply fell about 20% (Anthropic; Dataconomy).

Why it matters: It’s a detailed look at AI writing production code at scale, and it shows the bottleneck has moved from writing code to checking it.

42 U.S. attorneys general try to stop bankrupt Character.AI from auctioning millions of users’ private chats

A bipartisan group of 42 state attorneys general has stepped into Character.AI’s Chapter 11 case to block the sale of user data, which they say includes chat histories from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, over 2.1 million of them belonging to minors, plus IP addresses (TechCrunch; California Attorney General).

The only disclosed bidder is Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, a Delaware company set up on Nov. 13 and picked the next day as the starting bid for an auction scheduled for Dec. 1 (TechCrunch; The Next Web).

The attorneys general say a buyer wouldn’t have to honour Character.AI’s privacy policy and could resell the data, even though the company had told users it wouldn’t sell their information. Character.AI says user data will stay protected under its policy (TechCrunch; Colorado Attorney General).

Why it matters: The case tests what happens to intimate conversations with AI chatbots when the company behind them fails, especially when many of the users were kids.

OA Texas firefighter hugs a man in crisis atop a highway sign until he agrees to come down, and the video melts hearts

Seen on: Instagram, where music producer Alton Buggs II (@altonkiing) posted the video he filmed from a stopped car; NBC 5 says it has close to 250,000 likes.

Traffic stopped on a freeway in Bedford, Texas, near Fort Worth, when a man climbed onto an overhead exit sign. Josh Brown, a 23-year veteran who drives the fire department’s aerial ladder truck, rode the bucket up to talk with him (NBC 5 Dallas-Fort Worth).

Video from a driver shows Brown hugging the man again and again as he wipes away tears. The man then agreed to be harnessed into the bucket and was brought down to a waiting ambulance (NBC 5 Dallas-Fort Worth; Hoodline).

The city says he was taken to hospital for a mental-health check and no charges were laid. Brown didn’t know he had been filmed until later; his deputy chief says the hug was “the Josh Brown that I know” (NBC 5 Dallas-Fort Worth).

Why it matters: It’s a reminder that patience and kindness can calm a dangerous moment, and that crisis help works best when someone listens first.
