Alberta

Alberta offers new oilsands projects a 2% royalty for their first five years, its biggest incentive yet in the push to double output by 2035

The province unveiled its Oil Sands Growth Program on Friday. Approved new mines, in-situ projects and expansions would pay a royalty of just 2% of gross revenue for their first five years of production, rising one point a year to 6% in years nine and 10. After that, or if a project misses its production, environmental or regulatory commitments, it drops back to the regular royalty system (Edmonton Journal, The Globe and Mail, Government of Alberta).

Existing production doesn’t qualify, and the province says the program doesn’t skip environmental reviews or Indigenous consultation. It is due to launch in spring 2027, but projects announced after Nov. 15 will be grandfathered in. Officials wouldn’t estimate how much royalty money Alberta gives up, saying the discount only applies to projects that wouldn’t have been built otherwise. Premier Danielle Smith wants production to reach eight million barrels a day, roughly double today’s level of about 3.7 million (Edmonton Journal, Government of Alberta).

Why it matters: Oilsands royalties brought in about $14 billion last year and help pay for schools and hospitals, so a cut in the early years is a big bet that more projects will more than make up the difference later.

Sources (3)
  • Edmonton Journal: Alberta offers new oilsands projects reduced royalty rate in bid to double output
  • The Globe and Mail: Alberta’s Smith unveils new oil sands royalty regime
  • Government of Alberta release (via BOE Report): A new era of oil sands growth
Community Note

The province’s forecast of about $205 billion in extra revenue by 2040 assumes the full doubling happens; it isn’t an estimate of what this program alone will bring in.

Under the current system, oilsands projects pay 1% to 9% of gross revenue before they pay back their costs, depending on oil prices, so the new 2% rate isn’t a huge drop for every project in every year.

Sources: Edmonton Journal; The Globe and Mail; Government of Alberta

Canada

Stelco backs down and will recall all 175 laid-off Hamilton workers by Dec. 1, with back pay, after Ottawa threatened court action

What changed since the last edition: Our last Stelco story, on Oct. 25, reported Joly’s deadline to reverse the Hamilton job cuts or face a forced sale. The company has now agreed to recall every laid-off worker with back pay, and Ottawa says it will drop the threat of court action.

Stelco said Friday it will restart its Hamilton finishing lines on Dec. 1 and bring back all 175 temporarily laid-off workers, paying them retroactively for the wages they missed. The company said it had lined up enough customer orders to restart, though it will take several weeks to get the lines running again (The Canadian Press via Global News, The Globe and Mail, Reuters via U.S. News).

Its U.S. owner, Cleveland-Cliffs, had blamed U.S. tariffs and cheap imported steel for the layoffs. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said they broke the job guarantees Cleveland-Cliffs agreed to when Ottawa approved its 2024 takeover, and warned it could be forced to sell Stelco. After the recall was announced, Joly said Ottawa won’t pursue legal action once it receives formal notice (Global News, The Globe and Mail).

Why it matters: It’s the first time Ottawa has used the conditions in a foreign takeover approval to force a company to reverse layoffs, and it lands the same week the government is trying the same pressure on Stellantis.

The FAA orders every departing flight in the U.S. to stay on the ground after the country’s air traffic control centres lose communications

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop at 3:54 p.m. Eastern (2:54 p.m. Edmonton time) on Friday, holding all departing flights at U.S. airports “due to extraordinary circumstance and reasons for safety.” Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a communications problem was affecting all of the country’s Air Route Traffic Control Centers, the hubs that guide planes between airports. Planes already in the air could keep flying, and international flights headed to the U.S. were handled case by case (AP, The Washington Post, USA Today).

Reuters counted nearly 7,000 U.S. flights delayed or cancelled. By about 5 p.m. Eastern (4 p.m. Edmonton time) the FAA’s status page no longer listed the national stop, but big airports including Atlanta and New York’s JFK were still reporting departure delays of more than an hour and a half, and rising. The FAA hadn’t said what failed (USA Today, FAA National Airspace System Status).

Why it matters: A nationwide hold this rare ripples for a day or more, including for Canadians flying to or through the U.S. this weekend, so check with your airline before heading to the airport.

The U.S. Supreme Court agrees to decide whether Trump can shut off asylum at the Mexican border, with arguments likely next spring

The justices said Friday they will review a federal appeals court ruling that struck down President Donald Trump’s January 2025 proclamation declaring “an invasion” at the southern border and the Homeland Security guidance that stopped people who cross from applying for asylum. The case is called Mullin v. Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services (AP, SCOTUSblog, Supreme Court order list).

A D.C. Circuit panel ruled 2–1 that Congress never gave the president the power to skip the removal procedures and asylum rights written into U.S. law. That ruling is on hold during the appeal, so the restrictions stay in force for now. The court will also look at whether the trial judge could protect a whole class of migrants at once, but it declined to take up the government’s separate challenge to how that class was defined (SCOTUSblog, ACLU).

Why it matters: The decision will set how far any U.S. president can go in closing the border by proclamation, a question that also shapes migration pressure on Canada’s side.

Business

Microsoft’s Outlook and Teams outage stretches past 24 hours, forcing some firms back to fax and paper, before service finally starts to recover

Problems began Thursday morning Edmonton time and spread worldwide, knocking out Exchange Online, the cloud system behind Outlook email and calendars, along with Copilot and some security and admin tools. Microsoft says a large number of its servers unexpectedly went down, found no sign of a cyberattack and ruled out AI as the cause. By Friday afternoon a wider rollout of fixes was stabilising things, though the company still had no timeline for full recovery (Reuters, TechCrunch, The Guardian).

Downdetector logged more than 7.4 million reports, peaking near 20,000 a minute. Britain’s NHS fell back on paper records, a Taiwanese finance firm confirmed trades by fax, and several companies delayed earnings. A Morningstar DBRS analyst put insured losses at US$300 million to $500 million. Microsoft’s shares ended Friday flat (Reuters, NBC News).

Why it matters: Most workplaces run their email and meetings through one or two cloud giants, and a day-long failure shows how little backup many of them have.

Science corner

Science corner: a dome-shaped camera ‘retina’ with 367,500 pixels sees in colour across 160 degrees and spots motion while sending almost no data

Our eyes work so well partly because the retina is curved, so light lands in focus all the way to the edges. Flat camera chips need stacks of extra lenses to fake that. Engineers at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology have built a hemispherical artificial retina instead, using a 3D laser technique to pack 1,905 pixels per inch onto a dome. Their first version, in 2020, had just 100 pixels; this one has 367,500 (HKUST, Nature Materials).

Stacked blue, green and red layers give full-colour images across a field of view wider than 160 degrees. The clever part is motion: the sensor flags movement itself instead of streaming every frame to a computer. In the team’s tests that cut the data needed by more than 99.95% while still recognising motions correctly 98.6% of the time. Lead researcher Fan Zhiyong says a million-pixel version is the next goal (HKUST, TechXplore).

Why it matters: Robots, drones and self-driving cars burn power and time shipping video to a processor, so an eye that only reports what moves could make machines smaller, faster and more efficient.

Sources (3)
  • HKUST: HKUST develops high-resolution bionic eye
  • Nature Materials: An aberration-corrected bionic eye for full-colour vision and event-driven motion detection
  • TechXplore: High-resolution bionic eye combines color imaging with more efficient motion detection

This is a lab device, not a medical implant for people; it’s built for machine vision.

AI news and advances

OpenAI’s new ChatGPT Wallet lets its AI agents buy things for you, using one-time payment tokens and your approval on every purchase

OpenAI on Friday launched ChatGPT Wallet, which lets its “dots” agents finish a purchase after finding what you asked for. It was built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa: you add a card once, and for each purchase the card network hands the agent a single-use payment token instead of your real card number. You review and approve every purchase with a security check, and you keep your card’s usual rewards and fraud protections (OpenAI, CNBC, Bloomberg Law).

To start, it’s only for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the U.S. who are 18 or older. OpenAI says it isn’t taking a cut of sales for now. Not every store is on board: CNBC notes Amazon blocks AI agents from buying on its site, and United Airlines said this week it will block agents from booking tickets (CNBC, OpenAI Help Center, Asharq Al-Awsat).

Why it matters: Letting software spend real money is the step that turns AI assistants from search tools into shoppers, and how stores and card networks handle it will shape online buying.

Sources (5)

  • CNBC: OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet, allowing its ‘dots’ AI agents to make purchases for users
  • Bloomberg Law: OpenAI launches ChatGPT Wallet for agentic payments

O“Spite for Strays”: a divorcée’s $10,000 donation, made to annoy her ex, turns a Saskatoon cat rescue’s cheeky post into a $30,000 worldwide fundraiser

Seen on: The rescue’s own social media posts, shared around the world (The Canadian Press, WestCentralOnline).

A supporter of SOS Prairie Rescue came into some money in her divorce and offered to match donations up to $10,000, knowing that spending it on rescue cats would bother her ex-husband. Rescue president Ashlyn Weninger wrote it up as a joke one Thursday night (“Somewhere, an ex-husband is having a very bad day”), and by the next morning it was circling the world (The Canadian Press via Global News, CTV News Saskatoon, WestCentralOnline).

The match was met in under 72 hours. Donations came from across Canada and the U.S. and as far as the U.K., Croatia and Sweden, pushing the total past $30,000. The money goes to vet bills: the volunteer rescue had to close intake a few weeks ago and needs about $60,000 to reopen. The campaign runs until Dec. 1 (Global News, WestCentralOnline).

Why it matters: It’s a funny story with a real payoff: more cats getting care, and a reminder that a well-written post can rescue a struggling charity.
