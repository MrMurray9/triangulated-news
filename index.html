Alberta

It’s mid-November and Alberta is under wildfire advisories: Edmonton has had only 58% of its normal fall precipitation

A dry, unusually warm start to November has pushed fire warnings well past the usual season. Alberta Wildfire issued fire advisories on Nov. 9 for the Rocky Mountain House and Grande Prairie forest areas, citing dry conditions, strong winds and rising fire danger, and is asking people not to burn when winds top 15 km/h. Calgary issued a city fire advisory on Nov. 10, and Lethbridge banned all open fires in its river valley on Nov. 9, with several surrounding counties adding restrictions (Alberta Wildfire, City of Calgary, Global News).

Global News meteorologist Tiffany Lizee says Edmonton has had only 58% of its normal fall precipitation and Calgary 67%. Weekend winds on Nov. 7 and 8 gusted to 122 km/h in Stavely and 100 km/h in Slave Lake. Forecasters also warn that the strong El Niño now in the Pacific usually brings Alberta a dry, mild winter, which could dry fuels further before spring (Global News, Lethbridge County).

Why it matters: Grass fires spread fast in dry, windy weather even in November, and a snow-poor winter would leave the province drier going into the 2027 fire season.

An advisory is the lowest level of fire warning, below a restriction or a ban. There is no province-wide ban, and Edmonton itself has not announced one. Check albertafirebans.ca for your area.

This story rests mainly on official notices (Alberta Wildfire, the City of Calgary, Lethbridge County) plus Global News. The El Niño outlook is a seasonal forecast, not a certainty.

Canada

Ottawa says Russian players won’t be allowed into the world juniors in Edmonton and Red Deer, even if they already hold Canadian visas

Secretary of State for Sport Adam van Koeverden said Wednesday that Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada will individually assess and stop Russian and Belarusian athletes and officials from entering Canada to compete at the tournament, which starts Dec. 26 in Edmonton and Red Deer. The ban covers players who already have valid visas, including Russians now playing in the Canadian Hockey League (The Canadian Press via Global News, AP, TSN).

Last month the Court of Arbitration for Sport ruled that the International Ice Hockey Federation must reinstate Russia, and Norway’s federation said it had been told Russia would be added in its place. The IIHF hasn’t formally changed the field, and its website still lists Norway. Finland, Sweden, Czechia and Norway have said they won’t play against Russian players. Ottawa says its position won’t change while the war in Ukraine continues (Global News, AP, TSN).

Why it matters: The tournament is in Alberta in six weeks, and this likely keeps Russia out and could put Norway back in.

Millions join Spain’s first general strike in 14 years, called by tenants’ groups over sky-high rents and home prices

The 24-hour stoppage on Wednesday was started by tenants’ associations and then backed by the big unions. Unions reported about 90% participation in the car and port sectors, many schools and universities closed or ran at minimum, and the tenants’ confederation said about 4 million people took part by midday. Pickets and burning-tyre roadblocks shut roads into Barcelona, and public transport there nearly stopped (The Guardian, AP, NBC News, EFE).

Marches filled Madrid and Barcelona in the evening; organisers claimed 400,000 in Barcelona, while local police put it at 55,000. Officials said more than 50 people were arrested across the country, and some clashes broke out at night. Rents in Spain have risen about 80% in a decade. The strike comes ahead of a snap general election on Nov. 29 that Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called after right-wing parties blocked his emergency housing law (The Guardian, AP).

Why it matters: Housing costs are the top issue in a vote polls suggest a right-wing coalition could win, and many countries, Canada included, face the same squeeze.

A three-storey apartment building in Brooklyn collapses into a neighbouring construction pit, killing four people and injuring at least 23

The building at 2625 Bedford Ave. in Flatbush came down just after 4 p.m. local time Wednesday, falling into a hole where crews had been digging that day for a new building on the next lot. FDNY Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore said four people were killed and at least 23 hurt, seven of them children, and that officials didn’t yet know how many people might still be in the collapse zone (Gothamist, Engineering News-Record, NBC New York).

City records show the excavation was permitted for a proposed nine-storey building that hadn’t yet been approved. Mayor Zohran Mamdani said the Department of Buildings and police will investigate the cause. A pregnant woman was pulled from the rubble in critical condition, and the Red Cross opened a reception centre at the Flatbush YMCA. Rain and wind forecast for Thursday could make the site more unstable (Gothamist, CBS New York, NBC New York).

Why it matters: Digging beside older buildings is a known risk in dense cities, and the investigation will look at whether the excavation next door was done safely.

Foxconn posts a record quarterly profit, up 34%, as AI server racks now bring in more than half its revenue

Hon Hai Precision, better known as Foxconn, said Thursday that it earned NT$77.5 billion (about US$2.35 billion) from July to September, up 34% from a year earlier and ahead of analysts’ forecasts. Revenue rose 46% to NT$3.02 trillion. Its cloud and networking division, which builds AI servers for Nvidia and big cloud companies, made up 54% of revenue, up from 51% the quarter before (Focus Taiwan, Reuters via Investing.com, Malay Mail).

The world’s biggest contract electronics maker, best known for assembling iPhones, said it expects strong growth next year from AI demand but warned that chips, memory and other parts are in short supply. Its gross margin slipped slightly to about 6% (Reuters via Investing.com, Focus Taiwan).

Why it matters: Foxconn builds much of the world’s AI hardware, so its results are a direct read on whether AI spending is still growing, and it says it is.

Science corner

Science corner: MIT engineers 3D-print a material patterned like leaf veins, and it soaks up impacts better than neat honeycomb designs

Most engineered “metamaterials” repeat one tiny building block over and over, like a honeycomb. That works, but if one spot cracks, the identical cells around it tend to give way in the same manner. A team from MIT and Northeastern University tried the opposite: a web of beams of many different sizes, from microns to centimetres, like the veins in a leaf or a dragonfly’s wing. An algorithm draws random lines that get shorter and thinner each round, and two simple settings tune how stiff the result is (MIT News, Nature).

They 3D-printed credit-card-sized samples in polymer, then crushed them and dropped weights on them next to regular lattice and honeycomb samples. The network designs held up better and spread damage out instead of collapsing all at once. A version modelled on the thick rind of a pomelo protected a fragile object buried in its centre. “This is a completely new way of thinking for metamaterials,” said MIT’s Carlos Portela. The study is published in Nature (MIT News, Interesting Engineering, 3DPrinting.com).

Why it matters: Better shock absorbers could mean lighter helmets, safer packaging and armour that doesn’t fail all at once when it’s hit.

  • Nature: Scale-rich network-based metamaterials

AI news and advances

Google’s new robot brain ties bows and sorts car parts in Mercedes-Benz factories, hitting 96% success, and comes with an open test for tricking robots

Google DeepMind released Gemini Robotics 2.5, the AI model that turns instructions and camera video into robot movements. Google says it succeeds 30% to 100% more often than the previous version on lab tasks and works 20% to 30% faster. In tests, Apptronik’s Apollo 2 humanoid used it to tie a bow with five-fingered hands, a hard job because ribbon bends and flops (Google DeepMind, Ars Technica, The Verge).

In a trial with Mercedes-Benz, Apollo 2 robots running the model sorted car parts into kits at European plants with a 96% success rate. DeepMind also released ASIMOV-Jailbreak, a free set of 1,500 prompts designed to trick robots into unsafe actions, so other labs can test their systems. The model runs in the cloud and is going to partners such as Apptronik, Agile Robots and Boston Dynamics, not the public (Ars Technica, Humanoids Daily, Google DeepMind).

Why it matters: General-purpose robots that can learn many factory jobs are getting closer, and a shared safety test is an early step toward rules for robots that work near people.

Sources (4)
  • Google DeepMind: Gemini Robotics 2.5
  • Ars Technica: DeepMind reveals Gemini Robotics 2.5 and open safety benchmark ASIMOV-jailbreak
  • The Verge: Google DeepMind’s new AI model gives robots improved dexterity
  • Humanoids Daily: Gemini Robotics 2.5 puts Apollo 2 on Mercedes-Benz lines
Community Note

The success-rate and speed gains are Google’s own lab figures. A DeepMind director told Ars Technica that closing the last 4% gap to human reliability will be a “steep road.”

Demo videos were sped up 2x, according to Ars Technica, and the model still runs on remote servers, which adds lag.

O“Only in Canada”: a Toronto store lets shoppers test parkas at −15°C, and the video tops 18 million views
