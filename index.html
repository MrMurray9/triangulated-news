Alberta

Porter flies its first international routes from Alberta starting today, with Edmonton to Puerto Vallarta

Porter Airlines’ first international flight from Alberta is scheduled to leave Edmonton International Airport for Puerto Vallarta today, Tuesday. The route runs three times a week, on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays (Porter Airlines, CTV News Edmonton, Daily Hive, PuertoVallarta.net).

Three more routes from Edmonton follow this month: Phoenix, daily, from Friday, Nov. 6, then Los Cabos and Las Vegas, four times a week each, from Nov. 16. Porter flies Embraer E195-E2 jets with no middle seats, and includes beer and wine in the fare. Until now, Porter’s Edmonton flights only went to cities in Canada (Porter Airlines, Daily Hive, CTV News Edmonton).

Why it matters: Edmontonians get another nonstop option to Mexico and the U.S. Southwest for the winter, which adds competition for WestJet and Flair on the same routes.

Canada

Champagne promises Thursday’s federal budget will simplify taxes for small and medium-sized businesses

Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne told a Canadian Club Toronto breakfast on Monday that the budget he tables on Thursday, Nov. 5, will make the tax system simpler for small and medium-sized companies. He gave no details, but hinted at changes to the SR&ED research-and-development tax credit, and said the budget is about changing how Canada’s economy grows. “It’s not about them… This is about us,” he said of the pressure from U.S. trade policy (The Canadian Press via Global News, The Globe and Mail, Investment Executive).

The government already counts on its “productivity super-deduction,” which lets companies write off investments faster, costing about $36 billion over five years. The Canadian Federation of Independent Business wants the small-business tax rate cut instead. Champagne didn’t answer questions about a sovereign wealth fund or selling stakes in airports (The Canadian Press, The Globe and Mail, Investment Executive).

Why it matters: Small businesses employ about two-thirds of private-sector workers, and their tax bill and paperwork are one of the few things a budget can change quickly.

Sources (3)

  • The Globe and Mail: Budget will include new tax measures for small businesses, Finance Minister says
  • Investment Executive: Federal budget is about changing Canada’s growth model: Champagne
Community Note

This is a preview, not the budget: no measure, cost or start date was announced. Ministers often signal friendly measures before a budget; the full numbers, including the deficit, come Thursday afternoon.

Sources: The Canadian Press; The Globe and Mail; Investment Executive.

Russian bombs destroy a Ukrainian aid centre and its shelter in Zaporizhzhia, killing at least 31

Russian aerial bombs hit a former school used as a humanitarian-aid centre in the village of Komyshuvakha, in southern Ukraine’s Zaporizhzhia region, on Monday, then struck the shelter beneath it. By Tuesday morning at least 31 people were confirmed dead, including four police officers and two rescue workers, and more than 45 were still missing under the rubble (The Associated Press via NBC News, Interfax-Ukraine, The Guardian, Kyiv Independent).

President Volodymyr Zelensky said Russia dropped about 10 heavy guided bombs; the region’s acting governor, Mykhailo Semikin, called it one of the worst attacks on civilians in the region since the full-scale war began, and three days of mourning were declared. Overnight, Russia launched 289 drones and 76 missiles across nine regions, wounding at least 62 people in Kharkiv and knocking three DTEK power plants offline. U.S.-brokered talks between Ukraine and Russia are scheduled in Abu Dhabi for Nov. 10–11 (AP, Interfax-Ukraine, The Guardian).

Why it matters: It is one of the deadliest single strikes on civilians this year, a week before the next round of U.S.-led peace talks, and it comes as Russia hits power plants ahead of winter.

Belgian PM De Wever offers to resign after budget talks collapse, and the King puts the decision on hold

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever offered his government’s resignation to King Philippe on Monday after the five parties in his coalition failed to agree on the 2027 budget. The government needs about €10 billion in savings by 2029; Belgium ran a deficit of 5.2% of GDP in 2025. The French-speaking liberal MR party rejected proposed VAT and tax increases (The Associated Press, AFP, Politico Europe).

The King has not accepted the resignation. He is meeting party leaders through the week: Les Engagés and CD&V on Tuesday, Vooruit and MR on Wednesday and De Wever’s N-VA on Thursday, before seeing De Wever again on Friday morning (AP, Politico Europe, VRT NWS).

Why it matters: Belgium is home to the EU and NATO headquarters, and a government collapse would leave one of the eurozone’s biggest deficits without a plan as Brussels presses members to cut.

Cyberattack switches supermarket fridges to defrost overnight in at least nine European countries

Attackers remotely switched refrigerators and freezers into defrost mode overnight Sunday to Monday at supermarkets in at least nine European countries, with temperatures reaching up to 50°C in some cases. Belgium was hardest hit, with about 75 stores including Delhaize, Carrefour, Intermarché and Spar locations and an IKEA in Mons. Stores were also affected in Ireland (13 SuperValu shops), Sweden, Luxembourg, Estonia, Finland, the Netherlands and Portugal (AFP, Brussels Times/Belga, The Guardian, Euronews).

Belgium’s federal prosecutor has opened an investigation. No group has claimed responsibility. The equipment maker Danfoss said 17 companies using its systems in the Netherlands were affected. It follows a similar attack on French supermarkets on Oct. 12, and the Belgian retail federation Sabcobel says some fixes need technicians to visit in person (AFP, Brussels Times, The Guardian).

Why it matters: It shows how ordinary internet-connected equipment can be used to cause real-world losses across borders, from spoiled food to empty shelves.

Business

Ryanair’s half-year profit falls 15% as fuel costs double, and it weighs pay freezes and smaller cabin crews

Ryanair said Monday that profit after tax for the six months to the end of September fell 15% to €2.15 billion. It carried 6% more passengers, but at lower average fares, while the price it paid for the fuel it hadn’t locked in ahead of time roughly doubled (Ryanair, RTÉ, The Guardian).

The airline warned its fuel bill could be about €2 billion higher in the financial year to March 2028. CEO Michael O’Leary said higher fares won’t cover all of it, so Ryanair is looking at pay freezes, fewer cabin crew per flight where rules allow, tougher talks with airports and more use of AI (The Guardian, RTÉ, Ryanair).

Why it matters: Europe’s biggest airline by passengers is a bellwether for travel prices: if Ryanair can’t absorb fuel costs, fares for winter and next summer are likely to rise across budget carriers.

Science corner

Science corner: a robot that watches solar-cell films form in milliseconds, then learns how to make better ones

Perovskite solar cells are cheap to make from liquid, but the thin light-absorbing film is fussy: small changes in temperature, humidity or timing as it dries can ruin it, so labs still rely on a lot of trial and error. Researchers at the University of Osaka built a robotic platform that makes the films automatically while cameras record what happens, catching colour changes in fractions of a second and the splash of each “antisolvent” droplet within a few milliseconds (University of Osaka via TechXplore, Nanowerk, ACS Nano).

Machine learning then sorted through the many variables to find which ones mattered most, and pointed to an ideal range for how the droplets hit the film. Using those settings, the team made a lead-based perovskite cell that turned 24.4% of sunlight into electricity, in the range of good silicon panels. The work is published in ACS Nano (TechXplore, Nanowerk).

Why it matters: Finding the right recipe faster is one of the main hurdles to getting perovskite panels, potentially cheaper than silicon, out of labs and onto roofs.

Sources (3)
  • TechXplore (University of Osaka): Robot platform combines AI and rapid imaging to improve perovskite solar cell fabrication
  • Nanowerk: Robots and AI optimize perovskite solar cell fabrication
  • ACS Nano: Automated Perovskite Film Fabrication with High-Resolution Data Acquisition and Machine Learning Analysis
Community Note

This is a lab result on small test cells; 24.4% is good but not a record, and perovskites still have to prove they last for decades outdoors. Coverage is based mostly on the university’s own release; the peer-reviewed paper is in ACS Nano.

Sources: University of Osaka via TechXplore; Nanowerk; ACS Nano.

AI news and advances

OpenAI pledges to cut up to a gigawatt of data-centre power use whenever the grid is under strain, starting in Georgia

OpenAI announced a “Grid Protectors Program” on Monday: at U.S. data-centre campuses it builds itself, it will cut how much electricity it draws when extreme weather or other emergencies strain the grid. The first commitment is at its planned campus in Effingham County, Georgia, where OpenAI says it will reduce demand by up to 1,000 megawatts on request, roughly what 700,000 homes use (OpenAI, Axios, The Verge).

The details will be set with each utility, including how much power, how fast and for how long. OpenAI’s head of energy policy, John McCarrick, told Axios the industry has dropped the idea that data centres must run 99.999% of the time. Federal regulators and a Senate permitting bill are both pushing data centres toward this kind of flexibility (Axios, The Verge).

Why it matters: Fears that AI data centres will drive up power bills and cause blackouts are a big source of local opposition; flexible demand is one of the few ways to add them without new strain on the grid.

Sources (3)
  • OpenAI: Introducing the Grid Protectors Program
  • Axios: OpenAI pledges data center power cuts
  • The Verge: OpenAI pitches data center power plants as a way to protect nearby areas
Community Note

This is a pledge, and the Georgia campus is still years from full power. How often cuts would actually be requested, and whether backup generators would fill the gap, will depend on agreements with Georgia Power that haven’t been published in full.

Sources: OpenAI; Axios; The Verge.

ORobots raced up China’s Mount Tai carrying eggs and watermelons, and a few of them tumbled down the stairs

Seen on: Douyin and other Chinese platforms, where clips of the climbs and falls spread widely, as reported by WION and AFP.

China’s state broadcaster CMG held its first robot climbing competition on Mount Tai, in Shandong province, on Monday. About 90 teams of humanoid robots and robot dogs tackled a 3.9-kilometre route of about 3,100 stone steps, some carrying eggs or watermelons to show balance (AFP via CBS News, WION, TechNode).

The fastest robot dog, from a Unitree-linked team, finished in 43 minutes 41 seconds; most hikers take more than two hours. A humanoid team won a shorter 1.6-kilometre course in 27 minutes 26 seconds. Clips of the misses spread just as fast: one bandana-wearing humanoid collapsed mid-climb and another tumbled back down the steps (AFP, WION, TechNode).

Why it matters: It’s a fun, public stress test of how far walking robots have come, and how far they still have to go, on real-world stairs instead of a lab floor.

Sources (3)

  • TechNode: Robots climb Mount Tai in China’s first major mountain challenge
Community Note

The event was organised by Chinese state media, which also promotes the country’s robot industry. Times are from the organisers. Some robots had human handlers nearby, and teams chose different courses, so results aren’t directly comparable.

Sources: AFP; WION; TechNode.
