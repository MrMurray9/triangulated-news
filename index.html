Alberta

Edmonton’s living wage is now $22.65 an hour, and Banff, measured for the first time, tops Alberta at $27.45

The Alberta Living Wage Network’s 2026 rates cover 20 communities, and every one is above Alberta’s $15 minimum wage, unchanged since 2018. Edmonton’s rate rose 1.6% to $22.65, Calgary’s 1.5% to $26.90 and St. Albert’s 6.3% to $27.20, while Spruce Grove’s fell 4.2% to $22.70. Nine communities went up and 10 went down, as rents, costs and government benefits shifted (Alberta Living Wage Network, Rocky Mountain Outlook).

Banff’s first-ever figure, calculated by Healthy Bow Valley, is $27.45 an hour on a 35-hour week: $24.29 for a single adult, $27.26 for each parent in a family of four and $39.95 for a single parent with one child. Half of Banff tax filers earn under $40,000 a year, and the group is urging the province to raise the minimum wage (Rocky Mountain Outlook, Bow Valley Insider).

Why it matters: The living wage is the yardstick employers and cities use for what it actually costs to get by, and the gap with Alberta’s $15 minimum wage is now more than $7 an hour in Edmonton.

Sources (3)
  • Alberta Living Wage Network: 2026 living wage rates for 20 Alberta communities
  • Rocky Mountain Outlook: Banff’s first living wage highlights true cost of living
  • Bow Valley Insider: Banff’s new cost of living
Community Note

A living wage is not a legal minimum. It is an estimate of what a worker needs for a modest life, including rent, food, transportation, child care and a small emergency fund, but not debt repayment or saving for a home or retirement.

The rates were released Nov. 9; some Banff workers have since told reporters the estimate is still too low because it uses average rents, and rents there range from about $1,000 to $3,650 a month.

Sources: Alberta Living Wage Network; Rocky Mountain Outlook; Bow Valley Insider

Two days after tabling it, Smith says her government will amend the bike-lane bill, after Calgary’s mayor says cities were promised they wouldn’t pay for removals

What changed since the last edition: Thursday night’s edition covered the bill being tabled. Today the premier said it will be amended, after Calgary’s mayor said the province promised cities wouldn’t pay for removals.

Speaking on her Saturday radio show, Premier Danielle Smith said her chief of staff met Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas this week and that his concerns were “legitimate.” “We’ve got some amendments to propose to that,” she said. “We want it to be collaborative.” She did not say what would change, and Transportation Minister Devin Dreeshen’s office gave no details (Calgary Herald, CityNews Edmonton).

Why it matters: Who pays to rip out bike lanes, and how much say the province gets over Edmonton’s LRT, could decide big pieces of the city budget.

Canada

A secret Hegseth memo limits Pentagon contact with Canada’s military, while Canadian officials say they’ve seen no change

The Washington Post reported Friday that U.S. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth signed a classified memo last week restricting parts of the U.S.-Canada defence relationship. At the Halifax International Security Forum on Saturday, Republican Sen. Mike Rounds confirmed it exists, saying it limits meetings between generals and admirals to those that advance U.S. defence goals and requires U.S. defence officials to get permission before attending social events or think-tank conferences (The Washington Post, AP, Reuters).

The Pentagon says operations are not affected, including NORAD, NATO work and intelligence sharing, and that it wants engagement with Canada to advance “tangible” U.S. defence objectives. Canadian officials say they have seen no change on the ground and are seeking clarification (AP, The Telegraph).

Why it matters: Canada and the U.S. defend North American airspace together through NORAD, and a top-down chill on military contacts lands just as Ottawa is trying to close a trade deal with Washington.

Sources (4)

  • Investing.com (Reuters): US Defense Secretary Hegseth restricts military ties with Canada, Washington Post reports
  • The Telegraph: Hegseth weakens defence ties with Canada
Community Note

The memo itself is classified and hasn’t been published, so its full scope is unclear. What’s known comes from officials quoted by the Post and from Sen. Rounds’ public comments.

Rounds downplayed the impact, saying the measures were drafted months ago and do not affect operations between the two militaries.

Sources: The Washington Post; AP; Reuters; The Telegraph

Drones set a sanctioned Russian cargo ship on fire in the Gulf of Finland, and Finland says Ukraine is likely behind it

Why it matters: The war at sea is now reaching the Baltic, a NATO lake, a day after Russian missiles landed in Poland.

COP31 runs into a second night with no new text, as about 130 countries weigh forcing Saudi Arabia to oppose fossil-fuel language in the open

What changed since the last edition: Last night’s edition reported the summit had overrun after a draft dropped fossil fuels. Since then a full day has passed with no new text, a bloc of about 130 countries has publicly demanded the language back, and an open showdown with Saudi Arabia is being considered.

The climate summit in Antalya was due to end Friday. By Saturday evening there was still no revised draft, and EU climate commissioner Wopke Hoekstra said it would be “either… a good deal or… no deal.” The EU, the U.K., small island states, the least developed countries and many African nations marched to the presidency office to demand that “transition away from fossil fuels” be put back in (Reuters, The Guardian, RTÉ).

Saudi Arabia is also pushing for strong backing for carbon capture and storage. Tuvalu’s climate minister called the fossil-fuel reference “a red line.” Talks looked set to run into Sunday, and the Guardian reports organizers are considering an open session that would make objectors state their opposition publicly, a risky move that could collapse the talks (The Guardian, Reuters).

Why it matters: A COP that can’t even repeat the fossil-fuel language agreed in Dubai in 2023 would be a major setback for global climate deals, which shape the policies Alberta’s oil and gas industry works under.

Science corner

Science corner: wired-up prickly pear cacti work as living solar panels, and eight of them out-volt a AA battery

Researchers at UC Santa Cruz found a way to tap the prickly pear’s unusual photosynthesis. These cacti only take in carbon dioxide at night, storing it as malic acid, and sunlight breaks the acid down only on the side of the stem facing the sun. That leaves one side more acidic than the other, and the difference produces a voltage that electrodes inserted into the stem can collect (Plants, People, Planet; Popular Science).

One stem gives up to about half a volt; linking eight stems produced 4.5 volts, three times a AA battery. Voltage followed the sun closely, dipped when the plants protected themselves from too much light, and stayed strong even in cacti left without water for 15 months. The team wants to use it to power small crop-monitoring sensors on farms (TechXplore, Popular Science, heise online).

Why it matters: A plant that makes its own electricity in droughts could run cheap farm sensors without batteries or solar panels, and the cactus’s voltage doubles as a live read-out of its own health.

Sources (4)
  • Plants, People, Planet: Power harvesting from living solar panels (DOI 10.1002/ppp3.70295)
  • Popular Science: Prickly pears can generate solar electricity
  • TechXplore (UC Santa Cruz release): Harvesting electricity from prickly pear cacti
  • heise online: Living “solar panels”: How prickly pear cacti generate electricity
Community Note

The power is tiny: enough for low-energy sensors, not for lights or phones. Seven of the paper’s authors were undergraduates.

TechXplore carries UC Santa Cruz’s own release; Popular Science and heise reported it separately, and the peer-reviewed paper is the primary source.

Sources: Plants, People, Planet; Popular Science; TechXplore; heise online

AI news and advances

Countries agree to two more years of talks on “killer robots” in Geneva, but not to negotiate a binding ban

The 128 states in the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons ended overnight talks early Saturday by agreeing that government experts will work on elements of an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons through 2028, then propose whether it should become a legally binding treaty (swissinfo.ch/Keystone-SDA, AP, Politico Europe).

Nearly 100 countries wanted a mandate to negotiate a binding ban on weapons that can kill without a human giving the final order, but the United States, Russia and others opposed it. The campaign group Stop Killer Robots called the result disappointing and “the lowest common denominator” (swissinfo.ch, AFP, Politico Europe).

Why it matters: Critics say autonomous weapons are already being used in combat, and this UN forum is the main place countries are trying to set rules for them; the earliest it can now deliver anything is 2028.

Sources (4)
  • swissinfo.ch (Keystone-SDA): Geneva talks on autonomous weapons end with minimal consensus
  • AP: UN-backed talks extend AI killer robot discussions to 2028
  • AFP: Countries open path towards ‘killer robots’ pact
  • Politico Europe: Effort to regulate ‘killer robots’ delayed to 2028
Community Note

The outcome is a process, not rules. The talks work by consensus, which lets any single state block a stronger mandate.

Outlets framed it differently: AFP called it a path toward a pact, while campaigners and Swiss coverage called it a minimal result. Both describe the same decision.

Sources: Keystone-SDA; AP; AFP; Politico Europe

FA Michigan pizza shop’s Facebook post about a shy caller asking for leftovers turns into a “meal wall” funded from around the world

Seen on: Facebook: the Rosco’s Pizza page, as reported by TODAY and WNDU.

During a dinner rush at Rosco’s Pizza in Union, Mich., manager Shanna Crice got a call from a timid voice asking whether the shop threw food away. She said no, and the caller hung up. Regretting it, she posted on the restaurant’s Facebook page asking the caller to phone back “so we can have a different conversation.” The post passed 100,000 likes, with comments from as far as Australia, Scotland and Nigeria (TODAY, WNDU).

Commenters suggested a meal wall, and within a week Rosco’s had one: anyone can buy a $10 meal ticket, pinned to a board with a heart showing where the donor is from, and anyone in need can take one, “no questions asked.” Donations came in from Aruba, Spain, Portugal and across the U.S., and the shop is working with local schools and churches to get the meals to families (WNDU, TODAY, WSBT).

Why it matters: A small-town restaurant turned one missed moment into a standing way for neighbours to feed each other, and the internet paid for it.

OA teacher annoyed by her “noisy” upstairs neighbour finds an 87-year-old with Parkinson’s, and their friendship videos draw nearly 30 million views

Seen on: Instagram and TikTok: Heidi Widmer’s accounts, as reported by PEOPLE and ABC7.

Heidi Widmer, a middle school teacher who moved from Buffalo to Culver City, Calif., this summer, thought her upstairs neighbour sounded like a midnight bowling league. Then she met Carole, who has Parkinson’s, struggling down the stairs with her recycling. The bowling-ball sound was her walker. “Suddenly, I didn’t care about the noise. I just knew I had to help her,” Widmer told PEOPLE (PEOPLE, ABC7 Los Angeles, Business Insider).

Widmer now helps Carole, who had lived alone for years, with cooking, cleaning, benefits and paperwork, and posts about it on Instagram and TikTok. The landlord added an air conditioner, a stove and new carpet, letters arrived from across the U.S., and a GoFundMe for Carole’s care passed $81,000. “She’s actually my granddaughter,” Carole joked to ABC7 (ABC7 Los Angeles, PEOPLE).

Why it matters: A reminder that the neighbour you’re irritated with might just need someone to knock on the door.
